ANSWER OF CONSTELLATION ENERGY GENERATION, LLC 
Constellation submits this answer1 and respectfully requests that the Commission adopt its 
preliminary finding that the existing PJM Tariff is unjust and unreasonable because it lacks clarity 
in how it applies to a variety of co-location arrangements.  The position favored by the Indicated 
Transmission Owners (and PJM to a lesser extent) for a one-size-fits-all solution with full Network 
Integration Transmission Service (“NITS”) billed on gross load is wrong as a matter of law and 
policy.  Constellation asks the Commission to (1) adopt most of PJM’s proposed options for 
replacement rates with the modifications proposed herein, which will permit the prompt 
interconnection of data centers pursuant to critical national interests while minimizing 
transmission upgrade costs to consumers and maximizing efficient use of existing energy 
infrastructure, or (2) initiate time-limited settlement discussions to establish an appropriate 
replacement rate.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
This proceeding is about clarifying the PJM Tariff—and in some cases, adding appropriate 
rules—so that the significant data center load coming onto the system can be served promptly and 
efficiently.  The Commission was right that the existing PJM Tariff lacks requisite clarity, and this 
has paralyzed co-location efforts and service to data centers.  Consequently, the PJM Tariff is not 
just or reasonable.  This load must be connected promptly because of its critical strategic 
importance to this nation:  we are in a race with China and other nations for supremacy in Artificial 
Intelligence (“AI”), and we will lose the race if we are not able to promptly connect the data centers 
that hyperscalers require to train their models and serve users.  President Trump has explained that 
the United States needs to be “very competitive with China and others” in the development of AI 
infrastructure and that co-location enables such development “on a very rapid basis.”2  Limiting 
this new load to only one type of front-of-the-meter service that requires a years-long 
interconnection process, or even (as we have seen in Ohio) that are subject to a moratorium on 
new load connection, will lead to defeat.  The load also must be connected efficiently: the 
Commission should be looking for ways to bring this new load online while minimizing the need 
for costly system upgrades paid for by all consumers.   
According to the Indicated Transmission Owners, however, all new load should be required 
to take front-of-the-meter service and become a NITS customer paying for service based on gross 
load.3  The Indicated Transmission Owners read the existing Tariff to require this one-size-fits-all 
limitation, and they have refused to perform studies for new load seeking a different configuration.  
It is no surprise that they want to foreclose customers from any other option: large new NITS

Requiring all new load in PJM to sign up for front-of-the-meter NITS also usurps state 
authority to decide that a generator should be able to make a direct sale to an end-user.  Under the 
Federal Power Act, the Commission has no jurisdiction over “facilities used for the generation of 
electric energy” or over retail sales.5  Instead, states have jurisdiction over those subjects, and 
many states allow generation facilities to serve end-use customers.  The Pennsylvania Public 
Utility Commission explained that such a decision is not “a failure to regulate” and it is “not an 
oversight,” but rather “it is a deliberate choice that has been enshrined in Pennsylvania law for 
almost a century.”6  The Commission may not obstruct that lawful state choice by compelling 
generators to sell all their output at wholesale and by forcing end-use customers to pay for 
transmission service they do not want and do not use.7  For all these reasons, the Commission 
should find the existing Tariff unjust and unreasonable.

In designing a replacement rate, the Commission should embrace a range of possible 
models that recognize, as the Commission found, “that different co-location arrangements are 
likely to use or benefit from the transmission system in different ways depending on how they are 
configured and, moreover, that those different configurations will themselves likely have different 
impacts on the transmission system.”8  As PJM’s filing suggests, there are many potential 
configurations load could select that would allow it to connect more quickly while imposing fewer 
system costs than limiting load to a front-of-meter configuration.  Allowing load to select a 
configuration that best serves its needs will enhance national security and national economic 
competitiveness by speeding connection for those new customers who need to connect quickly and 
will save existing customers money by minimizing system upgrades.  
The available options for load should include models that attempt to use the headroom on 
the existing transmission system as well as those that avoid or minimize costly and time-
consuming system upgrades.  Some new load may want a firm, front-of-the-meter connection and 
be willing to wait for the requisite studies to be performed and transmission upgrades to be 
designed, sited, permitted, and built.  Other new load may be willing to locate behind-the-meter 
and forgo reliance on the grid to be connected more quickly while still ensuring grid reliability.  
Existing customers benefit from such alternative approaches because they reduce the amount of 
system upgrades that trigger cost socialization.  Existing customers also benefit from co-locating 
generation and supply near each other because it reduces demands on the transmission system, 
including the risks of grid instability caused by large load drop events such as those that recently 
occurred in Northern Virginia (all caused by grid-connected large loads).9  Other new loads may

Option 1 and 2 are traditional in-front-of-the-meter NITS in which load is billed on a gross 
basis (Option 2 merely joins the generator and the load at the same point of interconnection, but

transmission rate perspective for the load to site itself at or near the point of delivery.  This

Option 3 is the existing tariff model for Behind-the-Meter Generation (“BTMG”), that 
allows load to take grid service when the co-located load exceeds the co-located generator’s output. 
The load is billed for NITS on a net basis, i.e., when the load exceeds the output of the co-located 
generator.  As applied to co-location, the name “Behind the Meter Generation” is misleading as a 
significant portion of the generation is likely to remain in-front-of-the-meter, but a portion would 
be dedicated to serving behind-the-meter load, thereby reducing demands the large new load would 
normally impose on the grid.  As PSEG explains in detail in its response, this configuration is a 
time-tested model for co-location, under which the load is charged a rate based on its net usage of 
the transmission grid.12  This model incentivizes the efficient use of the grid by encouraging new 
load to locate at a site that will minimize the need for system upgrades.  While PJM states that this 
option was intended for smaller loads,13 the Tariff contains no such restriction.  And PJM’s only 
expressed concern about the use of this option by larger loads is that PJM does not currently carry 
reserves for this load.14  That is no reason to reject the option.  PJM’s current approach can easily 
be modified to include reserves for large loads if necessary.  PJM focuses on a small subset of 
existing BTMG—that using “wholesale” BTMG—but it disregards the more common retail 
BTMG.  PJM’s focus on “wholesale” BTMG appears to be intended to create the false impression 
that use of PJM’s BTMG netting rules is limited.  In fact, as PSEG explains, it is not.15

Option 4 would allow load co-located with generation to elect to receive service directly 
from the generator, with protection schemes in place to prevent the load from drawing on the grid.  
(Option 5 is similar, except that the load could call on power from the grid as available.)  Many 
states allow generators to engage in direct sales, as PJM acknowledges in its answer.16  The 
Commission should not intrude on the states’ jurisdiction by foreclosing this option, as that would 
grossly overstep federal authority.  As the load is not drawing any power from the grid—it 
physically cannot do so with protection schemes in place—it is not using the transmission system, 
so transmission rates would not apply.  In attacking Options 4 and 5, the Indicated Transmission 
Owners focus almost exclusively on arguing that the load should pay ancillary services, conflating 
benefits received from ancillary services by the co-located generator with the use of the 
transmission system to deliver energy.  It may indeed be appropriate in this type of configuration 
to pay some amount for ancillary services, such as regulation and black start.  But that does not 
mean the load should be paying a NITS transmission rate for transmission services it does not use.  
Option 4 or 5 could also serve as a transitional service for load that prioritizes connecting 
immediately, but that ultimately will want to become a NITS customer once any needed system 
upgrades are complete.  The availability of these options facilitates prompt connection and 
minimizes system upgrade costs for load willing to receive generator-contingent service. 
Option 6 is a “bring your own generation” approach that allows new generation resources 
to skip the generator interconnection queue if they can demonstrate support from an individual 
load, rather than being favored based on their shovel-readiness and contributions to reliability, as 
PJM’s Reliability Resource Initiative (“RRI”) proposal envisioned or the first-ready, first-served 
interconnection queue reforms implemented in PJM in 2023.  While PJM does not explicitly

unreasonable because it does not contain “provisions addressing with sufficient clarity or

loads are the same and want the same service.  Some prioritize firm transmission service, while 
others are willing to accept generator-contingent service if that allows them to get online faster.

them all a NITS rate based on gross load (as opposed to net) no matter their actual use of the 
transmission system discriminates because such a construct “is incapable of differentiating 
customers who impose different costs on the transmission system through co-location.”101

The premise of this question is based on a fundamental misunderstanding of the 
predominantly merchant generator model in place across most of PJM.  Under this model, 
generating resources were sold by most utilities to merchant owners over 20 years ago and, for the 
most part, have since participated in markets administered by PJM or in bi-lateral markets.  
Consumers bear no risk related to owning and operating these generating resources.  Rather, the 
merchant owners bear these risks exclusively and have no ratebase or other consumer fail-safe 
mechanism to fall back on if things do not go as planned.  If, for example, revenues are insufficient 
to support ongoing operations and a return for a generator, consumers have no obligation to provide 
any “make whole” payment (let alone a return payment) to support that uneconomic generator—
the generator is forced to go out of business, with any loss borne entirely by its owner.  And this 
risk is real.  For example, in 2019, Constellation was forced to prematurely retire its Three Mile 
Island nuclear facility (now the Crane Clean Energy Center) for economic reasons.  And 
consumers bore no risk in that unfortunate development just as they bear no risk in Constellation’s 
recently announced plan to restart the facility.383

384 As previously explained above with respect to the “duty to serve,” utilities are provided authorizations to hold 
natural monopoly positions and in exchange are subject to price regulation and have a duty to serve customers in their 
service territory.  The customers or ratepayers pay for the service they receive.  The price for that service is established 
by cost-of-service ratemaking through which the utility can “yield a reasonable return on the value of the property 
used at the time it is being used to render the service.”  Bluefield Waterworks & Imp. Co. v. Pub. Serv. Comm’n of W. 
Va., 262 U.S. 679, 690 (1923).  The utility undertakes an investment in transmission and distribution infrastructure 
and, in some instances, generation.  The utility receives the yield or rate of return on that capital investment in an 
amount that is “sufficient to assure confidence in the financial integrity of the enterprise, so as to maintain its credit 
and attract capital.” Fed. Power Comm’n v. Hope Natural Gas Co., 320 U.S. 591, 603 (1944).  The ratepayers did not 
make the investment in capital infrastructure.  Instead, ratepayers pay the utility to provide a service, i.e., delivering 
energy to their home or business.  The service rate is not a downpayment or investment in the underlying assets by 
which the utility provides that service.  If a specific generation unit is no longer being used to render service to 
ratepayers, then it is reasonable for the utility to be able to optimize that unit by other means.
