II. 
INTRODUCTORY COMMENTS 
 
PJM is a large and complex organization, with an equally complex governing structure. 
At an extremely high level, it is divided into two components: transmission planning/operations 
and energy markets. PPL Electric’s primary focus as a transmission owner has always been on 
the former. There, it works directly with PJM through the PJM Tariff and Manuals, including but 
not limited to planning, interconnections, cost allocation, rates and rate design, and operations. 
Governance issues are certainly relevant on the transmission side, and reforms are necessary in 
certain areas. For example, PPL Electric and the other PJM transmission owners recently put for-
ward a major effort to empower the PJM Board to propose changes to the Regional Transmission 
Expansion Planning Protocol, where the existing status quo gives a minority of PJM members 
veto power over any change to the planning rules.3 This effort was unsuccessful on other 
grounds, but PPL Electric remains committed to removing this impediment to rational, independ-
ent regional transmission planning, and hopes that such a change will be part of any package of 
reforms to come from this process.  
 
On the energy markets and resource adequacy side of the house, PPL Electric’s interest 
as a “poles and wires” transmission and distribution utility has historically been more indirect, 
but is nonetheless critically important. PPL Electric’s customers are reliant on the PJM markets 
to provide the capacity, energy, and ancillary services needed to keep the lights on. Approxi-
mately half of PPL Electric’s customers shop through Pennsylvania’s deregulated retail market,

A. 
The Board of Managers and Filing Rights 
 
No change to PJM governance can succeed without recognizing the essential role of the 
independent Board of Managers. PJM is a public utility under FERC jurisdiction pursuant to the 
Federal Power Act.5 It directs the operation of both the country’s largest transmission grid and 
generation fleet. It is under constant pressure from both market participants and political actors. 
It must have independent executive power to make decisions without being controlled by or be-
holden to outside parties with their own agendas. As such, the PJM Board should have full con-
trol over all elements of the RTO delegated to PJM in the CTOA. This starts with filing rights. 
Although such rights are much sought after, federal law is clear: they are unambiguously granted 
to the owners of FERC-jurisdictional assets and can only be transferred voluntarily by those 
owners.6 The modern version of PJM started with such a grant. In the CTOA, the PJM transmis-
sion owners collectively agreed to transfer to PJM, exclusively and unilaterally, operational con-
trol over their billions of dollars of transmission assets, as well as their right under federal law to 
establish and make changes to the terms and conditions of transmission service in a FERC Tariff,

B. 
The PJM Members and the Stakeholder Process 
 
In addition to the PJM Board, PJM has a large and active Membership that has retained 
significant control, formally and informally, over the organization. When PJM was established, 
filing rights over the OA were placed in the hands of the Members Committee, and PJM practice 
has been to respect that allocation even when the Board would prefer a different allocation of re-
sponsibility. At heart, the OA governs the organization itself akin to corporate bylaws, including 
key questions like Board selection and the stakeholder process. Again, FERC’s authority over 
such matters of internal governance is relatively minimal, but it can advocate for common sense 
solutions like the Board recommendations discussed above. It can also recommend an advisory 
stakeholder process as is common elsewhere in the country.   
 
PPL Electric’s core transmission-related concerns are of course associated with the stake-
holder process and are located in substantive schedules to the OA, particularly Schedule 6 gov-
erning transmission planning. Because these are not related to PJM’s internal governance or or-
ganizational design, FERC’s authority is clearer and the need for change more direct. FERC 
should work to facilitate the movement of these substantive provisions to the PJM Tariff where 
they will be under Board control.9 Beyond these issues, PPL Electric supports changes to the 
stakeholder process that will streamline its own decision-making and increase accountability. 
Ideas include reducing the number of sectors from five to three (transmission owners, power sup-
pliers, and customers) to more closely match interest alignment and investment in the system.

C. 
The Role of the States 
 
Finally, because addressing resource adequacy will require the deregulated states to come 
to the table with changes to their energy policies, it is appropriate to address the elephant in the 
room: what should the role of the states be in PJM?  
 
PPL Electric’s strong view remains that “the deregulated states must exercise more au-
thority over generation planning and investment than they have in the past.”10 At a minimum, 
this will require coordination between the states and PJM beyond the level that has occurred in 
the past. Such coordination is essential to ensure the success of the state energy policies within 
the federally regulated market construct. The worst possible outcome would be for the states to 
make a good faith effort to expand generation, but then see that effort stymied through uncoordi-
nated PJM market rules or inconsistent federal policy. PPL Electric continues to support this in-
creased coordination and is open to efforts to deepen it, including more formal roles for OPSI, 
state executives, and other officials where appropriate.  
 
On the resource adequacy technical conference panels last year, many voices went fur-
ther, calling for a more direct and potentially controlling role for the states in PJM.11 At the time, 
PPL’s response called this effort a mistake for three reasons:

1. 
What is the PJM Board’s role in ensuring that PJM is sufficiently tackling 
the region’s operational and market challenges? How does the Board eval-
uate its effectiveness in that role? How often, and under what circum-
stances, does the Board act unilaterally, and should this change? 
 
PJM’s Board sits at the center of nearly all the relevant questions facing PJM and the re-
gion. First, the PJM Board controls PJM Tariff terms and conditions. This authority was dele-
gated by the PJM transmission owners in the CTOA. Specifically, among other delegated re-
sponsibilities, the PJM transmission owners granted the Board the power to (1) direct the opera-
tion and coordinating the maintenance of their transmission facilities,14 (2) administer the PJM 
Tariff and provide transmission service thereunder,15 (3) conduct certain regional transmission 
planning,16 and (4) conduct transmission billing and payments.17 The PJM Board must act con-
sistent with (A) safe and reliable operations, (B) a robust, competitive electric power market, and 
(C) protecting against undue influence over operations.18 It embodies PJM’s core responsibility 
to ensure reliable service and just and reasonable rates.

2. 
How should states, through the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (OPSI) or

Because much of the current discussion around PJM governance reform is focused on the 
role of the states, it may be worthwhile to start with a caveat: PJM and the other RTOs are feder-

power sales. They do not provide retail service or generation planning, which are reserved to the

cially the deregulated states, which are not doing integrated resource planning. This role has not 
always been well-defined. Now that the deregulated states face a crisis of generation planning 
and resource adequacy that will take serious public policy changes to address, it is absolutely ap-
propriate to seek clarity and refinement in their interactions with PJM.

consumer advocates themselves. Consumer advocates are also PJM Members, 
participating (i.e., voting) in the End Use Customer sector.21

Responses to Potential Questions: Panel 2 
1. 
Do sector-weighted voting and existing voting thresholds strike the right 
balance between achieving consensus and taking timely action? 
 
The PJM Members Committee rules require a weighted supermajority to act. This pro-
vides formal limits on PJM’s ability to amend the OA, including its substantive schedules. PJM 
practice is to follow Members Committee direction in most circumstances where it is not re-
quired as well, providing an informal but real check on its ability to amend Tariff terms and con-
ditions, Manuals, and the RAA. This gives veto power to a relatively small set of stakeholders.36 
The stakeholder voting threshold was made deliberately high to require an effort to reach consen-
sus. It has prevented both good and bad filings, although until fairly recently, PJM was generally 
able to navigate the process despite the restriction.  
 
More recently, it has become apparent that changes need to be made to PJM governance, 
including at least some change to Member voting. Such changes should focus on making the 
stakeholder process advisory overall, reducing the ability of minority stakeholder coalitions to 
veto actions, but also reducing the ability of majority coalitions to ram through bad policies over

PJM’s objection. Everyone will of course still retain the ability to provide information and argu-
ment for why their preferred policy is best, and to participate fully at FERC, but PJM and its

Other potential changes, in addition to or perhaps instead of, a move to advisory voting 
can also be considered. For example, the five sectors of the Members Committee (transmission 
owners, generation owners, distributors, end-use-customers, and other suppliers) are largely arbi-
trary and reflect overlapping interests with an overemphasis on customer voting that is not con-
sistent with the FPA’s overall regulatory scheme (where utilities have unilateral power to file at 
FERC and customers and other stakeholders are given significant rights in the regulatory process 
itself). A more streamlined model, still a compromise from the statutory scheme, would reduce 
the membership to three sectors (transmission owners, power suppliers, and customers), better 
reflecting the real dynamics of PJM and providing clearer guidance to the Board as to how a 
given policy will be received.37  
 
Other more afield ideas include dividing the Tariff into subject areas with different stake-

(TOs would continue to have more say over transmission issues, generators more say over the 
power markets, etc.) This is more complex and will likely be difficult to implement, so it may 
not be the optimal path to begin with.

2. 
What transparency reforms would improve the quality of PJM’s stake-
holder process and increase stakeholder confidence in its decision-mak-
ing? 
 
Transparency is a useful metric for an organization as important as PJM, but increased 
transparency is unlikely to lead to an improvement in the quality of the process or increased con-
fidence in the decision-making. When FERC created ISOs in Order 88838 (and later refined the 
concept in an RTO in Order 2000),39 it placed great emphasis on creating an independent organi-
zation that nevertheless was open to input from its many stakeholders and largely operated in a 
transparent process.40 With 25 years of experience, PJM’s processes have been shown to be very 
open and transparent, with the current risk being too many “cooks in the kitchen” to produce a 
viable product. Instead of forcing additional openness that may impact the ability to have tough 
conversations and incentivize appropriate stakeholder compromises, PJM should focus on its 
goals of reliability and just and reasonable rates. PJM governance reform should be deemed a 
success if we achieve better outcomes on those metrics, rather than seeking additional transpar-
ency for its own sake.

4. 
Do existing governing document provisions (e.g., Operating Agreement, 
Manuals) impede stakeholder decision-making and what changes could be 
made to address them? 
 
Stakeholder decision-making is not the goal. The goal is reliable operations at just and 
reasonable prices.   
 
As to the details of the governing documents and how stakeholders can act to influence 
policy, there is likely no perfect solution. PJM’s consensus-based issue resolution (CBIR) pro-
cess was designed by experts in the field and has been continuously refined over many years to 
maximize opportunities for input. Over time, as stakeholders have formalized their participation, 
they have rationally become more firmly set in their opinions and unwilling to compromise on 
positions—which are based on the very real financial interests of their companies. Many issues 
in the stakeholder process simply come down to issues where no consensus can reasonably be 
reached: should prices be high or low? Should the region prioritize transmission investment or 
generation investment? Should the region favor state-specific clean energy programs that are not 
endorsed by other states in the region? No process document, no matter how refined, can elimi-
nate the disagreements that exist over these issues or the interest of Members in advocating their 
positions. 
 
Instead, the Members should select an impartial Board to make the hard decisions neces-
sary to operate a public utility and energy market as large and as complex as PJM. Members and

5. 
What reforms to the stakeholder process could ensure PJM can meet the 
region’s challenges? 
 
First and foremost, the stakeholder process that currently hamstrings many PJM decisions 
should be made advisory. The Board of Managers should run the organization, with the stake-
holders providing input and expertise, but not deciding major questions.  
 
Other questions, like who participates in the stakeholder process, what questions are pre-
sented to it, how long everyone can speak, how long the process overall can continue, etc. are 
important details that do not need to be dictated by the Commission and may be initiated via a 
separate PJM-initiated stakeholder process reform effort following these proceedings. These de-
tails will follow if the Board is able to exert its independence over critical decisions through the 
OA and RAA.

7. 
Are there elements of PJM’s committee structure that routinely contribute 
to delays? Are there stakeholder processes or issues that could be expe-
dited or changed to provide greater focus on key issues and allow faster or 
more unilateral action on others? 
 
PJM’s lower committees have been rendered largely irrelevant because the voting rules at 
the Senior Standing Committees (Markets and Reliability Committee and Members Committee) 
have different voting structures. Stakeholders who are more empowered by the MRC/MC rules 
often refuse to engage in the lower committees, where they will be out-voted due to affiliate vot-
ing rules, and instead save their substantive participation for the end of the process where they 
have more influence. The result is an extended stakeholder process, with detailed issue charges, 
problem statements, stakeholder education, interest identification, package design, and compro-
mise voting—all of which takes significant time and investment—being thrown out after months 
(sometimes, years) of work and replaced by the preferred outcome of the working stakeholder 
majority. The result is a lengthy and inefficient process that does not result in a viable product, 
because PJM or key stakeholders cannot accept the majority package which is the result of the 
arbitrary Members Committee voting rules rather than reasoned decision-making.  
 
Again, the details can always be improved to speed the process and create better out-
comes, but the fundamental problem is the veto points built into the Members Committee voting 
rules. If the PJM Board were the ultimate decider, the process would simply work differently. In-
stead of trying to reach a winning vote in an arbitrary process, the stakeholders would instead fo-
cus on honing their market designs (or other proposed tariff changes) with an eye toward per-
suading an independent decision-maker (first the PJM Board, and ultimately the Commission).

8. 
Should PJM increase the use of fast-path or time-bound review procedures 
to expedite committee action on high-importance topics? 
 
Yes, where appropriate. These processes are an important tool and should remain in the 
toolbox. They have developed something of a stigma in the stakeholder process because they are 
looked at as an “end-run” around the more deliberative “proper” process. But in many cases, 
they are the only way for PJM to arrive at a decision fast enough to respond to market needs.  
 
Again, the better solution is to empower the Board to act independently and convert the 
stakeholder process into an advisory process. When the stakeholders know the Board will make a 
decision on a given timeline, they work toward the deadline to advocate and educate and will be 
less incentivized to use the stakeholder process to strategically delay action.

Recognizing regional differences, are there lessons from other RTO/ISO 
stakeholder processes that PJM could reasonably adopt to improve speed 
in voting processes and stakeholder representation? 
 
As discussed above in response to Panel 1, Question 6 (incorporated herein by reference), 
the ISO-NE and NEPOOL stakeholder processes work in a different context that cannot be di-
rectly translated to PJM, but nevertheless there are some lessons that can be drawn from them, 
including creating a work plan, moving to an advisory stakeholder process, limiting attendance 
to certain meetings, and increasing opportunities for informal discussion.
