I. 
INTRODUCTION 
PJM remains committed to supporting its member companies, the states, and the 
Commission in the industry’s efforts to integrate large loads, including data centers (“large 
loads”), including through co-locating with generation (“co-location arrangements”).   
PJM’s approach to large load expansion—co-located or not—focuses on:  
(1) maintaining resource adequacy; (2) enabling reliable system operations; (3) facilitating 
the states’ and stakeholders’ efforts to timely and effectively integrate large loads in a 
manner that meets their unique needs; and (4) providing information and analysis that 
supports the equitable alignment of transmission system usage and compensation.3  
Substantial progress can be made in these efforts by leveraging the tools in the existing 
Tariff4 and exploring with the Commission and stakeholders other potential innovative 
market solutions, where allowed by state laws, that may specifically define and limit when 
and how load in a jurisdiction can be served by a supplier other than a franchised public 
utility.  This means that some existing state regulatory environments may permit certain 
co-location load arrangements while other existing state regulatory environments may not.

II. 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF EXISTING AND POTENTIAL NEW 
OPTIONS TO FACILITATE CO-LOCATION ARRANGEMENTS 
At least eight potential options for additional large load co-location (as well as large 
load integration in general) are summarized in a chart attached as Exhibit A.   This exhibit 
lays out the key attributes and impacts of each configuration and service option on  load 
characteristics, services consumed, studies required, rates, operations, and resource 
adequacy impacts.  Three of the options already exist under PJM’s Tariff.  The other five 
are potential options that have been proposed by others or developed by PJM in response 
to issues raised in the various predecessor co-located load proceedings.  
To be clear, PJM does not mean to suggest that all identified “options” in Exhibit 
A are equivalent, desirable, or even necessarily workable.  For example, state laws may 
specifically limit when and how load in a jurisdiction can be served by a supplier other 
than a franchised public utility.  Further, identified options are not mutually exclusive, as 
it is possible several of the options may be used seriatim or concurrently. Each new option 
outlined herein is conceptual and would benefit from continued input from stakeholders, 
Commission guidance, and a deadline for submission of any such proposals either in the 
context of a section 205 filing or in this docket.     
Before describing the options, a few points warrant emphasis.  First, as noted above, 
the availability of various options is dependent on state law.  States may specifically define 
and limit when and how load in a jurisdiction can be served by a supplier other than a
--- PAGE 6 ---
6 
franchised public utility.  This is a significant point that is explored in greater detail below 
in response to the Commission’s question A.i about federal and state jurisdiction.  At 
bottom, a state’s exclusive retail franchise territory law may restrict non-franchised 
suppliers from serving co-located load.  Further, in deregulated states, state law may (or 
may not) today (or in the future) require co-located load suppliers to be licensed energy 
providers and/or otherwise impose limitations on such suppliers by, for example, obliging 
the designation of the load as Network Load, with or without netting, or the taking of 
certain types of transmission service that could compel the designation of co-located load 
as Network Load. 
Second, large co-located loads that connect as Network Load realize significant 
benefits (as discussed in greater detail in response to question B.iii, below).  Six of the 
eight options PJM outlines (Options 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, and 8) are premised on large co-located 
load being Network Load.  The remaining two configurations (Options 4 and 5) provide 
that the load may still be Network Load if designated as such by the customer (whether or 
not required by state law), but it does not have to be Network Load.  Importantly, where 
Network Load is designated and coming onto the system, even where necessary 
transmission upgrades are not yet in service, PJM operations will endeavor to implement 
interim operating procedures to serve the Network Load in real time because there is an 
obligation to serve it. 
Third, an introductory note about study-related terminology used throughout this 
submission.  Co-located load arrangements implicate a variety of different but interrelated 
study frameworks and cost allocation constructs that PJM will describe here in an effort to 
avoid repetition throughout and promote clarity about terminology used in Exhibit A.  First, 
traditional Transmission Owner/Load Serving Entity (“LSE”)/Electric Distribution
--- PAGE 7 ---
7 
Company (“EDC”) load interconnection processes (“traditional load interconnection 
processes”) to add Network Load are generally commenced by performing what PJM will 
call a “Transmission Owner retail load integration study” to identify local upgrades 
needed to reliably accommodate the load based on local transmission owner planning 
criteria.  The results of this study identify local system needs that are presented at the 
Transmission Expansion Advisory Committee (“TEAC”).  Solutions to these needs 
typically take the form of Supplemental Projects (following Tariff, Attachment M-3) and 
are also generally presented at the TEAC.   
PJM then performs a “PJM Do No Harm study” to evaluate whether a proposed 
Supplemental Project will adversely impact the reliability of the Transmission System as 
represented in the planning models used in other PJM reliability planning studies.  If as a 
result of the do no harm study, system upgrades are found to be required, such upgrades 
will be considered part of the Supplemental Project and are the responsibility of the 
Transmission Owner sponsoring the Supplemental Project.  Supplemental Projects are cost 
allocated to the local Transmission Owner’s zone pursuant to individual Transmission 
Owner cost allocation frameworks.   
Necessary studies (for additional information, see PJM’s response to question D.i, 
infra) are essentially generator interconnection modification studies.  Such studies are only 
performed when a generator is involved, and a party to the interconnection services 
agreement is proposing to change the interconnection.  Transmission Owners review PJM’s 
necessary study analysis, and transmission owners may verify that analysis and/or perform 
additional transmission owner analysis pursuant to their processes or requirements.  
An aggregation of large load additions could trigger regional (as opposed to local) 
transmission system needs, which would be identified as part of the Regional
--- PAGE 8 ---
8 
Transmission Expansion Planning (“RTEP”) process in Schedule 6 of the Operating 
Agreement.  The load that could rise to regional RTEP needs is determined during the 
annual load forecasting process, when PJM makes a request to the EDCs/Load Serving 
Entities (“LSEs”) to submit large load additions that would not be captured in the 
parameters that are typically incorporated.  PJM presents what is ultimately accepted into 
the Load Forecast for the subsequent year.  PJM then plans its transmission system in light of 
this load forecast, and RTEP upgrades are identified and ultimately cost allocated pursuant 
to existing RTEP-specific methodologies.   
Before moving off the topic of studies, it is important to re-emphasize that  any 
configuration where the co-located load is Network Load (Options 2, 3, 7, and 8, and in 
some circumstances Options 4 and 5) is limited in scope to generator interconnection 
modifications.  As described above, Transmission Owner retail load integration studies 
would be performed in addition to necessary studies.  While stakeholder (including the 
Organization of PJM States, Inc.) and Commission comments and questions probe the 
sufficiency of the necessary study agreement/study process for load integrations, only two 
of the proposed options (Options 4 and 5 if load is not designated as Network Load) 
involve the use of a necessary study without any form of a load interconnection study.  To 
the extent the Commission sanctions further exploration of Options 4 and 5 and the market 
is interested in pursuing such options, PJM will explore opportunities to enhance the 
visibility of the necessary study process and results for interested stakeholders. 
Fourth, as to the resource adequacy dimension to co-location arrangements where 
conditions are tight, any additional load on the system requires either the addition of supply 
resources to serve that load reliably, or flexibility on the part of the load to curtail its 
consumption from the transmission system when necessary to maintain reliability.  For this
--- PAGE 9 ---
9 
reason,  PJM has outlined three potential options relating to adding additional supply and 
operational flexibility:  Option 6 (co-located load that elects to be Network Load and 
leverages provisional interconnection service or State Agreement Approach), Option 7 (co-
located load that takes a new non-capacity backed service that could be curtailed in advance 
of emergency procedures), and Option 8 (co-located participates as Demand Response).  
And as discussed below, see infra question F.2, PJM is also exploring with regulators and 
generators opportunities to leverage at least 50 hours (or more) of diesel back-up generator 
service to enhance the available demand response portfolio.  And even where shorter term 
resource adequately may not be directly enhanced by a particular co-location arrangement 
(for example, where new load is not accompanied by additional generation or operational 
flexibility), longer term resource adequacy interests are certainly indirectly supported by 
any resulting increased prices from such scenarios.

Option 3 utilizes PJM’s existing Behind the Meter (“BTM”) Generation rules.  
These rules are explained further below in PJM’s response to question D.iv.  PJM’s BTM 
Generation rules would require the co-located load to be Network Load.  The rules charge 
load for its net use of the grid, after deducting BTM Generation.  This option is available 
in two configurations.  Either the load and generation are co-located at the same Point of 
Interconnection, or the load is served by municipal utilities or EDCs BTM through a 
distribution system, if the owner or lessee of such distribution system has consented (which 
is called “Non-Retail” BTM Generation).  The load uses and pays for NITS, Energy, 
Ancillary Services, and Capacity based on the net withdrawal quantity and dependent on 
if that withdrawal is during the Peak (NITS and Capacity).

In this option, where state law permits such a configuration, the load is located 
behind the Point of Change of Ownership between the Transmission Owner and generator, 
and system protection facilities are installed to prevent delivery of energy from the grid 
should the generator go offline.  In this configuration, load is not Network Load unless

This configuration has been described as “fully” or “wholly” isolated by others, but 
this characterization is inconsistent with engineering and economic principles.  This 
configuration relies on and takes grid services (for example, ancillary services) and should 
pay its fair share of those charges.  Additionally, this configuration carries operational risk 
in that there is a dependency on the protection system to support reliability.  If the 
protection system should fail,6 the large load would instantly withdraw significant energy 
from the system.  While a few of these configurations may seem tolerable to some, the 
anticipation of large quantities of these configurations increases the risk.  Tariff

FPA provides:

the public interest, such Federal regulation, however, to extend only 
to those matters which are not subject to regulation by the States. 
(b) The provisions of this subchapter shall apply to the transmission 
of electric energy in interstate commerce and to the sale of electric 
energy at wholesale in interstate commerce, but shall not apply to 
any other sale of electric energy or deprive a State or State

exportation of hydroelectric energy which is transmitted across a 
State line. The Commission shall have jurisdiction over all facilities 
for such transmission or sale of electric energy, but shall not have 
jurisdiction, except as specifically provided in this subchapter and 
subchapter III of this chapter, over facilities used for the generation 
of electric energy or over facilities used in local distribution or only 
for the transmission of electric energy in intrastate commerce, or

over facilities for the transmission of electric energy consumed

(c) For the purpose of this subchapter, electric energy shall be held

The foregoing grants the Commission three categories of jurisdiction:  
(i) jurisdiction over “the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce,” 
(ii) jurisdiction over “the sale of such energy at wholesale in interstate commerce,”12 and 
(iii) jurisdiction over the facilities used for such wholesale sales and transmission.   
In addition, the FPA expressly limits the Commission’s jurisdiction over:  (i)  sales 
of electricity that are not wholesale sales,13 (ii) facilities used for generation, (iii) local 
distribution facilities (in other words, not associated with high-voltage transmission of 
electric energy but where the transmitted power was “stepped down” to distribution 
voltage), and (iv) facilities for the transmission of electric energy consumed wholly by the 
transmitter (which does not appear implicated by most co-located load arrangements given 
different identities between transmitter and consumer).

What changes are necessary, if any, to other PJM planning

PJM Response:    
There may be opportunities to clarify or enhance existing planning processes.  One 
possible area of exploration could relate to addressing risks that arise based on the existing 
load forecasting process.   
During the annual load forecasting process, PJM makes a request to the EDCs/Load 
Serving Entities (“LSEs”) to submit large load additions that would not be captured in the 
parameters that are typically incorporated.  This request is made in July of each year for 
the following year’s forecast.  EDCs/LSEs submit their additions and PJM reviews each 
one and consults with the submitter to ask questions and conform the data for use in the 
forecast (e.g. converting capacity to demand, assessing contractual obligations, etc.).  The 
EDCs/LSEs must present this data at the September/October Load Analysis Subcommittee 
and defend their submissions to stakeholders.  Finally, PJM presents what is ultimately 
accepted into the Load Forecast for the subsequent year.

With any forecast of future load growth, additional transmission and generation 
capacity may be required, and there is the risk of overbuilding due to a forecast that is too 
high, or underbuilding due to a forecast that is too low.  The same is true with respect to 
large load additions, regardless of whether the load is co-located with generation.  Given 
the potential size of these individual, large load additions that are in the forecast, 
uncertainty with respect to whether the large loads are actually added to the system could 
cause either overbuild or underbuild to occur based on a single customer addition.   
The method by which any required generation and/or transmission capacity 
additions are procured also has implications for who carries the risk of forecasted large 
load additions not materializing.  For example, if large load additions are added to the load 
forecast utilized in forward capacity auctions, the additional demand can result in price 
increases applicable to all demand.  To the extent the forecasted load additions do not 
actually materialize, these higher capacity costs would be allocated to all other load on the 
system.  Were the large loads to enter into bilateral or self-supply arrangements to serve 
their capacity needs, the parties to those arrangements would wear the risk of the large load 
not actually coming on line.  Similar implications may exist for transmission cost allocation 
as well. 
Given these dynamics, opportunities to enhance the load forecasting process and 
address the potential consequences of that process may be worthy of exploration.  
Commission guidance and stakeholder engagement on this specific issue and the options 
discussed herein and presented in Exhibit A and any other options that may be lodged in 
this proceeding will assist PJM in evaluating and identifying  any changes to planning 
processes.

ii. 
Please discuss whether back-up service can provide benefits to 
customers or otherwise help minimize system costs, including with 
specific examples or best practices, if possible. 
PJM Response:  
Even when co-located with an existing generator, co-located load is often (but not 
always) built with an on-site backup generator to ensure continuity of operations in the 
event its primary power source is disrupted.  These back-up generators have the potential 
to provide benefits to customers and minimize system costs by participating in the PJM
--- PAGE 69 ---
69 
markets, but currently their use is limited by the Environmental Protection Agency’s 
(“EPA”) National Emissions Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (“NESHAP”) for 
stationary reciprocating internal combustion engines (“RICE”) regulations as well as other 
environmental restrictions depending on their particular location and permit restrictions. 
PJM understands that most back-up generators installed at large load centers for 
backup power are Tier 2 under the EPA’s regulations.  Under the EPA’s NESHAP 
regulations, a Tier 2 RICE that provides back-up power for a co-located load may only be 
used in emergency situations as declared by the Reliability Coordinator or other authorized 
entity or for limited periods for non-emergency purposes.  For non-emergencies, the 
NESHAP regulations generally prohibit Tier 2 RICE units from providing non-emergency 
demand response, generating income for a facility to an electric grid, or otherwise 
supplying power as part of a financial arrangement with another entity.90  In certain 
situations, the RICE Tier 2 units may provide demand response or peak load shaving in 
non-emergencies when local system conditions could lead to a blackout.  Specifically, 
these Tier 2 units may run for up to 50 hours a year when called upon by the local balancing 
authority or local transmission system and distribution operator to mitigate or avert voltage 
collapse or line overloads that could interrupt power.91  However, this run-time counts 
towards the unit’s overall 100-hour annual run-time limit.  As a result, these back-up 
generators have historically been viewed as limited in being able to provide customer and 
system benefits by either being a demand response resource or selling excess power into 
the market.  Therefore, regulatory changes to allow such unit’s ability to provide additional
