INTRODUCTION 
Pursuant to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“Commission”) February 20, 
2025 Order (“Order”), the Center for Biological Diversity (“Center”) submits the following 
response to the parties’ March 24, 2025 filings. Feb. 20, 2025 Order at 57. The Center 
appreciates the opportunity to comment on the Commission’s Order Instituting Proceeding 
concerning co-location of large loads at generating facilities. The Center is a national, non-profit 
conservation organization with more than 1.8 million members and online activists who care 
about the country’s urgent need to expedite the renewable energy transition and protect human 
health, the natural environment, and species from the ravages of the climate emergency, 
extinction crisis, and environmental degradation.  
As discussed below, in addressing the energy needs of data centers and other large load 
facilities that are rapidly proliferating across the country, it is vital that the Commission address 
both the challenges and opportunities these facilities provide. Regarding challenges, to fulfill its 
statutory obligations—as detailed in the Addendum below—it is critical that the Commission 
take into account the environmental implications of these facilities, including both the climate 
change threats they pose and their other environmental impacts. As for opportunities, the 
Commission should consider how these facilities can help address grid and load needs through

demand response and load flexibility, and how to maximize reliance on renewable energy to 
address the facilities’ energy demand needs. 
In addition, the Center also offers comments below more specifically addressing the 
issues raised in the Commission’s Order and initial comments. First, our comments detail steps 
the Commission should take to ensure that any tariff or other measures protect consumers from 
being forced to subsidize data centers and related facility and infrastructure costs that should be 
borne by private corporations benefiting from these expenditures. Second, we address the issue 
of ensuring these facilities do not escape regulation by being mischaracterized as either behind-
the-meter or offline. At bottom, the Commission must ensure that these facilities—and the load 
growth the facilities are bringing to the Nation’s energy system—is both fair to consumers and 
consistent with FERC’s statutory mandates.

Threshold Issues the Commission Should Consider in Addressing the Matters

A. The Commission Should Consider the Environmental Implications of Any Tariff

1. Data Centers and Cryptomining Threaten the Climate. 
 
Data centers, AI, and cryptomining facilities demand enormous amounts of energy, 
which is why they are prime candidates for co-locations arrangements. However, the load growth 
these facilities are projected to bring is driving more fossil fuel generation, taking the Nation in 
the completely wrong direction to address the climate emergency. The Commission has an 
obligation to take into consideration this strong connection between these facilities and fossil 
fuels as it considers appropriate regulatory structures. 
a. 
Data Centers, AI, and Crypto-Facilities Demand Seismic-Levels of 
Energy. 
 
Data centers are highly energy- and water-intensive. These facilities host vast computing 
infrastructure—such as server racks, networking equipment, backup power systems, physical 
security features, and advanced cooling technologies—to process massive amounts of data and 
support services like artificial intelligence, cryptocurrency mining, photo sharing, and video 
streaming.  
Cloud Service Providers, largely owned and operated by major technology corporations, 
currently control approximately 67 percent of global cloud infrastructure.2 This dominance, 
coupled with Big Tech’s strategic prioritization of the AI and cryptocurrency sectors, is fueling a 
rapid proliferation of conventional and AI-specific data centers, as well as cryptocurrency 
mining operations, across the United States.

As of March 2025, the U.S. housed 5,426 recorded data centers.3 In 2023, these facilities 
consumed 176 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity, or 4.4 percent of the country’s total 
electricity use—more than the total energy consumption of Ohio in 2022.4 The energy demands 
of AI applications exacerbate this impact: while a conventional data center rack uses an average 
of 7 kilowatts (kW), AI data center racks can require up to 50 kW.5 In 2023, AI workloads 
represented about 20 percent of total data center capacity, and projections indicate a tenfold 
increase in AI-related electricity consumption by 2026. 6 
Cryptocurrency mining (“cryptomining”), which employs energy-intensive 
computational systems in facilities like data centers to solve complex algorithms and extract 
digital assets, represents perhaps the most egregious strain on America’s energy infrastructure. 
Even though less than 2 percent of U.S. residents are using cryptocurrency for transactions, 
cryptomining facilities can be even more energy-intensive than AI data centers. As of December 
2024, Bitcoin alone was estimated to consume 175.87 TWh annually—surpassing the total 
energy use of Pakistan in 2023.7

b. 
Data Centers, AI, and Crypto-Facilities’ Reliance on Climate-Killing 
Fossil Fuels Is Driving Growth in the Fossil Fuel Power Sector. 
 
Today, data centers, artificial intelligence, and cryptocurrency operations predominantly 
depend on energy sourced from fossil fuels, resulting in substantial emissions of carbon dioxide 
(CO₂) and other greenhouse gases. For example, each Bitcoin transaction is estimated to generate 
approximately 535 kilograms of CO₂, equivalent to the emissions from 1,186,233 VISA 
transactions or 89,203 hours of video streaming on YouTube.8  
The immense electricity demand from data centers, AI, and cryptocurrency mining—
largely met by fossil-fueled energy—not only accelerates climate change but also drives the 
continued operation and revival of gas and coal power plants, posing serious environmental 
risks. For instance, this year, Southern Company, a major U.S. utility, announced that it would be 
extending the operational lifespan of three coal plants in Georgia and Mississippi to address the 
growing energy demands of data centers.9 This decision permits the continued operation of a 
combined 8.2 gigawatts (GW) of fossil fuel-based, environmentally harmful energy capacity.10  
 
Similarly, in February, NRG Energy Inc., GE Vernova, Inc., and The Industrial Company 
announced their collaboration to build four new fossil gas power plants, totaling more than 5 
GW, to provide energy to data centers supporting AI and GenAI operations in the ERCOT and 
PJM markets.11

c. 
Energy Demand from Data Centers, AI, and Crypto Is Projected to 
Grow Exponentially. 
 
The energy footprint of these industries is expected to grow greatly in the coming years 
and decades, posing significant risks to the stability of the electric grid and potentially 
undermining national efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. 
Current trend analysis shows that global demand for data center capacity could grow by 
19 to 27 percent annually through 2030, reaching as much as 171 to 298 GW per year by the end 
of the decade.12 This is a sharp increase from today’s estimated demand of 60 GW and signals 
the risk of a major supply shortfall.13 Avoiding this gap will require building at least twice the 
total data center capacity constructed since 2000, but in less than a quarter of the time.14  
The demand for AI-compatible data center capacity alone is anticipated to grow at an 
average annual rate of approximately 33 percent through 2030.15 By the end of the decade, it is 
projected that roughly 70 percent of the total demand for data center capacity will be directed 
toward facilities specifically designed to accommodate advanced artificial intelligence 
workloads.16 Generative AI—currently the most rapidly expanding application of advanced AI—
is expected to represent approximately 40 percent of the total demand.17

Projections indicate that the cryptocurrency industry’s energy consumption in the United

2. These Facilities Also Have Numerous Other Adverse Environmental 
Impacts. 
 
Beyond greenhouse gas emissions, data centers result in numerous adverse environmental 
impacts, including impaired air quality, increased noise, high water demand, and harm to special-
status species and habitat.  
First, data centers degrade air quality in surrounding communities, largely due to diesel 
backup generators. Since 2017, more than 1 GW of diesel-based energy—enough to power at 
least 161,969 households for a year—has been approved as emergency power for California data 
centers alone.21 These generators not only add to greenhouse gas impacts, but they harm public 
health through emitting nitrogen oxide and particulate matter. Researchers at UC Riverside and 
Caltech found that an increase in permits for diesel generators at data centers in Virginia since 
2023 may have resulted in 14,000 asthma symptom cases and caused as much as $300 million in 
health care costs.22 By 2030, researchers predict the health burdens of data centers could amount 
to $20 billion.23 
Second, data centers impact the environment through their massive water demand. A 
mid-sized data center consumes on average around 300,000 gallons of water a day, about as

B. The Commission Should Maximize Opportunities For These Facilities To 
Facilitate Renewable Energy Alternatives.   
As noted, on the current trajectory data centers, AI and cryptomining facilities threaten to 
undermine progress on addressing the climate emergency. However, as we discuss next, the 
Commission should also consider the ways in which these facilities could instead help to

B. The Commission Has a Mandate to Facilitate the Nation’s Clean Energy 
Transition. 
Congress has directed that FERC make the Nation’s transition away from fossil fuel 
power sources a high priority. In the Energy Act of 2020 Congress set forth a national goal “to 
reduce emissions and meet 100 percent of the power demand in the United States through clean, 
renewable, or zero emission energy sources . . . .”107  Given FERC’s vital role in overseeing the 
Nation’s power sector, this provision demonstrates a Congressional expectation for FERC to 
incorporate decarbonization into all its decision-making. 
This is not a new Congressional priority. More than forty years ago, Congress passed the 
Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA), directing FERC to reduce the nation’s reliance 
on fossil fuels, and charging the agency with administering new programs to foster the 
development of renewable energy resources.108 Thus, as the Supreme Court has explained, in 
considering the use of the term “public interest,” FERC is called upon to consider the objective 
to “reduce reliance on fossil fuels,” based on the fundamental premise that the country benefits 
from renewable energy development and the “resulting decrease in the Nation’s dependence on 
fossil fuels.”109 
Making decarbonization, as well as larger questions of energy justice, a central priority at 
FERC is also entirely consistent with the remainder of FERC’s statutory mandates. As former
