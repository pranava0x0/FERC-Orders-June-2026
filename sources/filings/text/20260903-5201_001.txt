Establish Interim Resource Adequacy Service and A Large Load Registry to Address System 
Reliability (the “PJM Proposal,” “IRAS Proposal,” or “Proposal”).

BACKGROUND 
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) has proposed to define certain customers as New 
Large Loads and subject those customers to Interim Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”), a 
“service to be implemented by PJM … [w]hen triggered by system conditions.”3  Specifically, 
New Large Loads that do not bring their own qualifying new capacity (known as “BYONC”) in 
an amount that equals or exceeds their peak load will be subject to involuntary load reductions.4 
PJM contends that the IRAS framework will “support system reliability in the PJM 
Region.”5  PJM states that “the unprecedented addition of large loads, most notably data centers, 
have given rise to resource adequacy shortfalls—and associated real-time operational issues that 
IRAS is intended to address.”6  Accordingly, PJM has submitted proposed revisions to its Open 
Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) and the Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load 
Serving Entities (“RAA”) to effectuate the IRAS framework.

proposed RAA changes, “Large Loads” are customers with cumulative peak load of 50 megawatts 
or greater “at a single electrical site behind one or more points of interconnection” (and, for 
configurations involving multiple affiliated facilities or points of interconnection, “all affiliated 
load facilities within a one-mile radius will be considered a single electrical site”).7  Additionally, 
under the proposed revised tariff, “New Large Loads” are Large Loads that, after June 1, 2027, go

I. 
PJM’s Proposal Is Beyond The Commission’s Jurisdictional Reach 
A. 
The Commission’s Jurisdiction Is Subject To Strict Statutory Limits 
Section 201(b) of the FPA limits the Commission’s jurisdiction to “the transmission of 
electric energy in interstate commerce and to the sale of electric energy at wholesale in interstate 
commerce.”37  The Commission cannot regulate “any other sale of electric energy.”38   
In keeping with those formal limits, Section 201(a) declares that “Federal regulation … 
extend[s] only to those matters which are not subject to regulation by the States.”39  It also declares 
that federal regulation “is necessary in the public interest” only as to “that part of [the electric 
energy] business which consists of the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce and 
the sale of such energy at wholesale in interstate commerce.”40  Those declarations “cannot nullify 
a clear and specific grant of jurisdiction” elsewhere in the statute,41 but they are relevant as a 
guiding expression of congressional purpose where there is no clear and specific grant of 
jurisdiction and the Commission is asked to take a novel action.42

In FERC v. EPSA, the Supreme Court interpreted Section 201(b) and established a test for 
assessing whether the Commission has acted within the scope of its statutory jurisdiction.43  The 
Commission’s jurisdiction extends only “to rules or practices that directly affect the [wholesale] 
rate.” 44  And even if a Commission action has that necessary direct effect, the Commission 
nevertheless cannot intrude on a sphere that is “reserv[ed]” for the states—namely, “retail 
electricity sales.”45  Because the “wholesale and retail markets in electricity … are not hermetically 
sealed from each other,” the fact that a Commission action merely affects the retail sphere is not 
enough to create a jurisdictional problem.46  Conversely, mere effects on wholesale rates are not 
enough to make a Commission action jurisdictionally permissible:  “FERC cannot take an action 
transgressing th[e] limit” on intruding on the retail sphere “no matter how direct[] or dramatic” 
that action’s “impact on wholesale rates” may be.47  Thus, the Commission cannot “specif[y] terms 
of sale at retail,” which “is a job for the States alone,” even though such a specification could 
undoubtedly have a significant effect on wholesale rates.48

for the States, several factors are highly relevant.  First, it is critical to ask whether a Commission 
action “addresses—and addresses only—transactions occurring on the wholesale market” and

B. 
The Proposal Exceeds The Statutory Limits On The Commission’s 
Jurisdiction By Targeting Retail Activities And Thereby Impermissibly 
Intruding Into The States’ Regulatory Sphere 
1. 
PJM’s Proposal Impermissibly Intrudes On The States’ Regulatory 
Sphere   
The Proposal impermissibly takes a federal reliability function—telling distribution 
utilities how much load must be shed during an emergency—and transforms it into a federal 
regulatory regime governing which categories of retail customers must procure generation, which 
retail customers must accept curtailment if they do not, and how those curtailed customers are to 
be compensated. 
The Proposal regulates and targets New Large Loads, which are a specific type of retail 
customer.  It does so based on retail characteristics:  the size and newness of individual retail loads.  
The Proposal seeks to force changes to certain retail terms of service for those disfavored loads, 
requiring EDCs to develop interruptible service that involves curtailment of New Large Loads that 
do not bring their own new capacity (the “Eligible” New Large Loads, in the Proposal’s parlance).  
In doing so, the Proposal imposes direct requirements on retail customers and requires ongoing 
retail-side administration—for instance, confirmation that loads that have brought new capacity 
have done so within the limits set forth in the proposal.62  In addition, the Proposal’s primary input

II. 
The Commission Would Unduly Discriminate Based On Load Size, Load Date, And 
Resource Vintage If It Accepted PJM’s Proposal 
PJM’s Proposal unduly discriminates in at least two respects:  (1) against certain load, 
based on load size and load in-service date, and (2) against certain generation, based on resource 
vintage.  Those are independent reasons why the Commission should reject the Proposal. 
A.

Proposal Is Not Unduly Discriminatory And Preferential

FPA Section 205, a utility is barred from “grant[ing] any undue preference or advantage,” 
“subject[ing] any person to any undue prejudice or disadvantage,” or “maintain[ing] any

Commission finds that a “rate, charge, or classification” is “unduly discriminatory or preferential,” 
it must “determine the just and reasonable rate, charge, classification, rule, regulation, practice, or 
contract to be thereafter observed and in force, and … fix the same by order.”118 
Congress also prohibited undue prejudice or disadvantage in the specific circumstance at 
issue here:  the accommodation of anticipated capacity shortages.  Section 202(g) of the FPA 
(added by the Public Utilities Regulatory Act of 1978) mandates certain Commission obligations 
with respect to “any anticipated shortage of electric energy or capacity which would affect [any]

III. 
The Commission Must Reject PJM’s Proposal Because Market Design Flaws Make 
The Proposal Unjust And Unreasonable 
Under Section 205 of the FPA, PJM bears the burden to demonstrate both that its proposed 
tariff revisions are not unduly discriminatory and that they are “just and reasonable.” 180  To 
evaluate whether the tariff can be approved as just and reasonable, the Commission must engage 
in reasoned decision making,181 including by “examin[ing] the relevant data and articulat[ing] a 
rational connection between the facts found and the choice made.”182   
The Proposal fails that test on several grounds.  First, curtailing New Large Load before 
calling on emergency resources is irrational and detrimental to proper energy price formation and 
reliability.  Second, excluding New Large Load from the VRR Curve used in RPM Auctions to the 
extent that the load lacks sufficient BYONC (or Allocated RBP UCAP MW) will substantially 
undermine RPM’s operation and harm the capacity market and customers, reflecting the overall 
economic irrationality of the Proposal and its inconsistency with the design of the market.  Third, 
the Proposal unreasonably fails to allow any qualifying BYONC to be replaced by other qualifying 
BYONC and unreasonably limits the resources that qualify as BYONC to resources that cleared 
an auction for the first time in the Base Residual Auction for certain delivery years rather than in 
any auction for those years.  In explaining those points below, Constellation cites and relies on the 
accompanying September 3, 2026 affidavit of Aaron T. Patterson (which is attached to this filing 
as Appendix A).

A. DURING EMERGENCY OPERATIONS, RTOS SHOULD DEPLOY THE 
LOWEST-COST EMERGENCY ACTIONS FIRST WHEN POSSIBLE IN ORDER 
TO MAXIMIZE TOTAL SOCIAL SURPLUS 
11. A bedrock principle underlying organized power markets since their inception in PJM and 
elsewhere is that resources should be deployed and load served in a manner that maximizes the 
total social surplus for all participants on both the supply and demand side of the market, subject 
to all physical and operational constraints.  Under normal operating conditions in PJM’s energy 
market, this is achieved by dispatching resources in increasing order of their marginal cost and 
serving all load which has expressed a willingness to pay that is equal to or higher than the marginal 
cost of serving the final increment of load (or which has expressed a lack of demand elasticity, as 
is the case for most loads, which is treated as a willingness to pay in excess of administrative 
market price caps under normal operating conditions).  The market clearing energy price under 
this design is effectively3 determined by the intersection of the supply and demand curves, where 
the marginal cost of serving load is equal to the marginal benefit that the final increment of load 
derives from consumption.  This market organization maximizes social surplus because the sum 
of the benefits that load derives from the consumption of electricity minus the production cost of 
electricity (effectively the area between the supply and demand curves) is maximized.  Simply put, 
the maximum amount of load that can be economically served is served, and the production cost 
of serving that load is minimized.  The Commission has stated that a primary goal, or objective 
function, of organized power markets is to maximize total surplus,4 and has approved numerous

12. The benefits of maximizing total surplus remain when the system is operating under 
emergency conditions, although such maximization can be more challenging to fully achieve.  
When PJM is operating under emergency conditions the amount of available excess generation 
supply will typically approach zero, and PJM thus must rely on other actions, such as the 
curtailment of load, in order to maintain reliable operations.  All of the actions PJM may take under 
emergency operations have a cost, however, and these costs should be considered when deciding 
which emergency actions to take at any particular point in time.  For instance, curtailing load, 
whether it occurs through the deployment of demand response resources, through the curtailment 
of IRAS loads under PJM’s IRAS proposal, or via manual load shed actions, has an economic cost 
because the load in question is deprived of the benefits it derives from the consumption of 
electricity, whether that be through business disruption, damage to assets, or other problems.  The 
cost of curtailment is commonly referred to as the Value of Lost Load or “VOLL.”  Similarly, 
other emergency actions such as ordering generation to produce at emergency levels also result in 
incremental costs.  When operating under emergency conditions PJM does not take emergency 
actions based on a surplus-maximizing market optimization algorithm like it does under normal 
conditions.  Rather PJM utilizes a combination of an established deployment order coupled with 
operator judgement to decide which actions to take and when.6  Nonetheless, actions that PJM 
takes under emergency conditions still have economic costs, which are often very high.  To the 
extent that PJM deploys high-cost emergency actions while lower-cost actions which can also help 
the market maintain reliable operations remain available and unutilized, that choice unnecessarily 
increases the total costs borne by market participants and reduces total surplus.  Thus, to remain 
in alignment with the overall goal of maximizing market surplus, during emergency conditions 
PJM should seek to take lower cost actions before it takes higher cost actions if possible and should 
design its emergency action protocols and emergency action deployment order accordingly.

C. THE COST OF CURTAILING IRAS LOAD SHOULD BE REFLECTED IN

23. As discussed above, a core feature of PJM’s energy market design is that the market clearing 
price for energy under normal operating conditions is set by the incremental cost of serving the 
next increment of load (or of curtailing the final increment of load, if it is price-responsive).  
Setting the market price in this manner ensures that all potential lower-cost resources have an 
incentive to offer and provide energy during operations, and that the market sends a price signal 
for lower-cost resources to invest in their assets and enter the market.  This market design is 
commonly referred to as “marginal cost pricing.” 
24. Adhering to a marginal cost pricing design is more challenging under emergency conditions 
because the marginal costs of many emergency actions are not well defined but nonetheless 
becomes even more important under such conditions.  During emergency conditions, PJM needs 
every potential resource to provide energy to serve load, and the costs of emergency actions that 
it would otherwise take if load cannot be served using conventional resources are very high.  
Therefore, it is imperative that the energy market clearing price during emergency conditions be

energy offers of whatever dispatchable resources move in response to the load reduction.  Under 
high-stress conditions this price-setting resource will usually be some type of thermal generation

likely to be well in excess of $5,000 per MWh.  Thus, without intervention, the prevailing price 
signal that PJM’s economic dispatch software will produce when IRAS is deployed will be far 
below the VOLL of IRAS load, and thus far below the level needed to send the appropriate 
economic signals.  Put simply, absent changes to PJM’s economic dispatch software, curtailment 
of IRAS loads will suppress prices despite the existence of emergency conditions serious enough 
to warrant involuntary load curtailment, completely inconsistent with marginal cost pricing 
principles.

separate from and additional to standard RPM capacity.  Resources qualifying to supply BYONC

new large loads via bilateral BYONC arrangements that will be made outside of the RPM.  The 
value of the “newness” feature of BYONC capacity is likely to be quite high in the coming years

sufficient to cover their load may still interconnect with the PJM system, but PJM proposes that, 
starting with loads entering in the 2029/30 Delivery Year, these new large loads be excluded from 
RPM demand.  Under normal circumstances, when load grows or enters the system, it will be 
included in the RPM demand curve which in turn will send a signal to the market that additional 
supply is needed and existing supply should remain in the market.  This signal occurs through the 
demand parameters that PJM makes available to market participants prior to each auction, as well 
as through auction results themselves, including market clearing prices and quantities.  But under 
PJM’s Proposal, this will not occur because the load is excluded from the demand curve.  Despite 
this, the load in question will still be physically present when it comes to real-world operations, 
will be taking Network Integration Transmission Service on the same basis as other loads, and but-
for the IRAS curtailments will have the same right to be served as any other load. 
32. The implicit logic underlying PJM’s proposal is that because the excluded large loads can be 
called on to provide IRAS, the provision of IRAS offsets the resource adequacy need created by 
the physical entry of the excluded large load.  Thus, within the RPM framework, PJM’s proposal 
to remove large loads without BYONC arrangements or RBP allocations is effectively equivalent 
to incorporating the excluded large loads into the demand curve while also entering an equivalent 
amount of price-taker capacity supply that serves as a proxy for the IRAS curtailments that the 
excluded large load may be forced to provide.19

33. This implicit logic fails badly, for two reasons.  First, in any given delivery year IRAS will 
not exist at all if it is not triggered.    In this scenario, the excluded new large load will be physically 
present and will have the same service priority as other loads, but for RPM purposes PJM will 
simply pretend it is not there.  Furthermore, it will be uncertain whether or not IRAS will be in 
effect for a given delivery year until less than three months before the start of the delivery year 
because PJM proposes that the triggering conditions for the provision of IRAS are not to be 
finalized until the conclusion of the third Incremental Auction.  This lack of forward certainty as 
to whether IRAS will even be present at all further complicates PJM’s ability to plan for reliable 
operations both with respect to resource adequacy specifically as well as general transmission 
security in operations. 
34. Second, even when it is in effect, IRAS is not capacity.  Unlike its nearest capacity analogue, 
Demand Response, IRAS is not subject to any sort of rigorous accreditation process to verify that 
it can perform as required; it does not receive an Effective Load Carrying Capacity or UCAP award 
quantifying its capability; it is not subject to testing or performance measurement; and it is not 
subject to capacity performance incentives or penalties.  Instead, IRAS is implemented as a NERC 
Instruction to EDCs, which carries the weight of NERC oversight and potential enforcement, but 
is nonetheless far different from capacity.  Specifically, PJM will lack the same level of visibility 
and control it has with capacity resources because it cannot issue direct curtailment instructions to 
IRAS loads, because it cannot subject IRAS to the same precise, rigorous testing regime it places 
on capacity, because it cannot quantify expected IRAS performance via ICAP and UCAP ratings, 
and because it cannot assess or credit Non-Performance incentive payments to IRAS resources.  
But PJM’s proposed treatment of loads that are eligible to provide IRAS in the RPM effectively 
assumes that IRAS is equivalent to capacity resources that offset the incremental reliability 
requirement of the load in question on a one-for-one UCAP basis.  In mechanical terms, this is 
equivalent to assuming IRAS is a capacity resource with an ELCC equal to the forecast pool 
requirement, or about 92% in the upcoming 2029/30 BRA, which is better than Demand Response 
and most Generation Capacity Resource types. 
35. In addition to the reliability aspects, deployment of IRAS is likely to be very costly, and thus 
as a resource adequacy product it is likely to be much more costly than Demand Response or other 
capacity resources.  Excluding IRAS-eligible large load from RPM demand removes any pathway

to express these economic differences and replace costly IRAS with less costly capacity resources

36. The consequence of PJM’s exclusion of non-BYONC large loads from the RPM is that 
regardless of whether or not IRAS is triggered, the RPM will reflect a fictional reliability outcome 
that will be materially different from the real physical resource adequacy situation.  This is at odds 
with the purpose of the RPM, which is to conduct a competitive procurement for physical capacity 
supply sufficient to achieve PJM’s resource adequacy standard at the level of the RTO and Local 
Delivery Areas based on forecasted demand.  The amount of capacity procured, and the price paid 
for that capacity, will be inconsistent with the real underlying resource adequacy situation.

38. Thus, when PJM faces a need for new capacity (or retention of existing capacity) to support 
new demand from large load, there is no avenue for the RPM to reflect this need.  New large loads

fundamental need in reality.  PJM’s proposal creates the illusion within the RPM that no net new 
large load demand exists on the system, even if there is substantial need for new supply to serve 
new large load.

BYONC mechanism, which as discussed above occurs outside the RPM and does not result in a

will be effectively assumed to be served via the deployment of IRAS, which is both much more 
costly than actual capacity and subject to considerable uncertainty around its availability and 
performance. 
40. If it accepts the PJM Proposal, the Commission should order PJM to modify its proposal to 
include new large load in RPM demand under all circumstances, regardless of whether or not the 
new large load brings new capacity through a BYONC arrangement.  This treatment ensures that 
the RPM parameters and market clearing outcomes will be consistent with the actual underlying 
resource adequacy situation and will provide an accurate market price signal for resources to enter 
and/or remain in the market to support this incremental demand. 
IV. IRAS IS THE LATEST IN A SERIES OF RECENTLY-IMPLEMENTED OR PENDING ACTIONS BY 
PJM THAT WILL COLLECTIVELY DESTROY THE ABILITY OF RPM TO MEET THE RESOURCE 
ADEQUACY NEEDS OF PJM GOING FORWARD UNLESS CORRECTIVE ACTION IS TAKEN 
41. PJM’s IRAS proposal is the latest in a series of actions and proposed actions that collectively 
depress market prices in the RPM for existing resources while providing higher, discriminatory 
prices for new resources under specific conditions.  Without corrective action by the Commission, 
the collective weight of these proposals, coupled with the specific problems created by the IRAS 
proposal, will act together to effectively make it impossible for the RPM to attract new entry to 
serve any type of new demand, Large Load or otherwise, or to forestall resource exit. 
42. As discussed above, PJM’s proposal in this proceeding creates a special pathway for new 
resources to enter the market via bilateral BYONC arrangements with new large loads while 
requiring the capacity entering under such arrangements to offer into the RPM as price-takers and 
while excluding large loads that do not make BYONC arrangements from RPM altogether.  Both 
of these elements depress market prices in the RPM even when supply is needed to meet new 
demand.

resources exit the market, any apparent consumer cost savings will decline and ultimately reverse.

that are nonetheless needed for maintaining resource adequacy.  Under the emerging paradigm, 
the only viable way to replace these lost existing resources is to engage in further discriminatory

52. The forces moving PJM in the direction of the Credibility Trap have been operating for 
several years now and some of the interventions driving it have already occurred.  And PJM’s 
credibility as a steward of a stable, non-discriminatory, investable market for capacity has already 
been badly damaged.  Put simply, PJM is already deeply mired in the Credibility Trap.  Without 
modification, the IRAS proposal will drive substantial further market erosion for the reasons 
discussed above which will further undermine resource adequacy and increase costs in PJM.  This 
is particularly the case because the IRAS proposal will become a permanent part of the tariff and 
even if its implementation is suspended due to changing market conditions it can be repeatedly re-
activated later if market conditions again become tight.  Thus, the IRAS proposal is a permanent 
“heads I win, tails you lose” policy from the perspective of existing resources. 
53. But strong, prompt intervention by the Commission in the proceeding could put PJM on a 
path to ultimately escaping the Credibility Trap.  The modifications I discuss in other sections of 
this affidavit – namely moving IRAS curtailments back in the curtailment order and reflecting 
them in energy prices and reflecting all new large load in demand – are necessary to this but not 
sufficient in my view.  As discussed above, the BYONC mechanism is ultimately highly corrosive 
to markets and is a major contributor to the confluence of factors driving PJM towards the spiral 
of crisis.  Ideally the Commission should direct PJM to remove the BYONC mechanism from its 
proposal.  If the Commission declines to recommend a full removal of the BYONC mechanism 
from PJM’s proposal, it should consider mechanisms that limit its impact on the broader market, 
such as requiring resources entering the market under BYONC arrangements to comply with the 
Minimum Offer Price Rule which would preserve a new entry price signal for the broader RPM 
market.  The issues discussed in the PJM White Paper are broad and not limited only to the IRAS 
proposal, but strong action by the Commission to preserve markets in this proceeding will send a 
strong signal to investors that markets remain viable over the long term.
