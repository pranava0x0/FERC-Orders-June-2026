II. 
REPLY COMMENTS 
The California Utilities fully support the development of co-located load 
arrangements, which have the potential to create a number of benefits to ratepayers.2  For 
instance, co-located data centers may provide savings to ratepayers by reducing 
congestion charges, as these facilities are often drawn to locations with low-cost power, 
which could include areas with heavily curtailed renewables.  Co-located load 
configurations can reduce line losses, result in more efficient utilization of existing 
infrastructure, and free up delivery capacity on the grid.  Co-location arrangements may 
also provide a cheaper alternative to network upgrades.  However, without proper cost 
allocation of transmission costs, reliability services, and Resource Adequacy, traditional 
customers could be forced to subsidize co-located load, or suffer impaired reliability.  
Neither outcome is acceptable.   
In addressing issues related to co-located load, the Commission must ensure that:  
• States retain their exclusive authority to determine who may serve co-
located load and the rates and regulations for co-located load; 
• Co-located loads are adequately studied for Resource Adequacy and 
reliability impacts, including impacts relating to the co-located generation 
being off-line (momentarily, temporarily, or permanently); and  
• Co-located load connected to the transmission network pays a fair share of 
FERC-jurisdictional transmission costs, as determined by its state or local 
regulator.

A. 
Jurisdictional Issues 
In its Order to Show Cause, the Commission invited PJM and the parties to opine 
on the jurisdictional principles outlined in section VI.A.3 
The California Utilities appreciate the Commission’s clarification in the Order to 
Show Cause that “under the FPA, the states get to determine which entities are legally 
permitted to provide electricity to retail customers in co-location 
arrangements…irrespective of where the load interconnects (i.e., to the distribution 
system, the transmission system, or the generator itself).”4  The Commission also noted 
that although it “has not historically exercised jurisdiction over any and all transmission-
level interconnections of retail load,” the Commission “encourage[d] parties to address 
when and under what circumstances a co-located load is interconnected to the 
transmission system in interstate commerce”5 and “whether the Commission must, 
should, or can exercise jurisdiction over the interconnection of co-location arrangements 
to the transmission system in interstate commerce as an element of transmission service, 
including where the purpose is to facilitate a retail sale.”6 
Though PJM acknowledges that the various options it presents for co-location 
arrangements in Exhibit A “are subject to different state law requirements that could 
disqualify certain options,”7 PJM contends that the Commission has jurisdiction over 
PJM’s co-located arrangements “due to PJM’s provision of ancillary services and in light 
of the wholesale sales taking place over certain facilities. . . .Therefore, at a minimum, 
the Commission has jurisdiction regarding the transmission service associated with the 
ancillary services utilized in co-location arrangements.”8  The California Utilities agree 
with PJM that FERC has jurisdiction over the allocation of transmission, ancillary

reallocate wholesale costs among retail customers as long as they permit full cost

B. 
Any Tariff Revisions Should Adequately Address Resource Adequacy 
and Other Reliability Concerns 
The California Utilities appreciate the Commission’s recognition that large co-
located loads have the potential to negatively impact Resource Adequacy (i.e., capacity 
requirements) for existing customers.  Though FERC generally lacks authority to prevent

a generator from ultimately making direct or indirect sales to retail co-located load (even 
those that erode reliability), it is important for the Commission to recognize, when setting 
policy, that allowing co-located loads may reduce available capacity and accordingly, 
increase consumer electricity prices.  
The Commission does have authority, however, to require wholesale customers to 
procure adequate capacity.  If an ISO/RTO Tariff sets rules dictating which types of retail 
load that load-serving entities must purchase capacity for, at a minimum, the Tariff must 
be clear as to how co-located load will be treated and who will be responsible for 
procuring capacity for the load.  Cost allocation principles dictate that the entity directly 
selling at retail to the co-located load should pay capacity costs because of the grid 
reliability impact the load creates; how these costs are passed on to the co-located load is 
a matter between the load serving entity and its retail customer. 
 
Additionally, if existing generator capacity is diverted to co-located load rather 
than selling its energy into the market, this choice may cause power flow changes in a 
local area that need to be studied.  Thus, it is important for planners to know sufficiently 
far in advance if an existing generator is going to connect co-located load.  For generators 
in the queue, knowledge of intent to connect co-located load is similarly important for 
planning and reliability purposes. 
C. 
Co-Located Loads Must Pay Their Fair Share of Costs 
The Commission expressed concern that the absence of PJM Tariff provisions 
regarding co-located facilities “creates the potential that participants in a co-location 
arrangement may not be required to pay for wholesale services that they receive, as 
required by the cost causation principle.”19  Further, the Commission has reiterated that 
in designing its rates, PJM must ensure that costs are “allocated in a manner that is at

noted that “different co-location arrangements are likely to use or benefit from the 
transmission system in different ways depending on how they are configured and, 
moreover, that those different configurations will themselves likely have different 
impacts on the transmission system.”21   
As an initial matter, there is no such thing as fully isolated co-located load unless 
neither the source generator nor the co-located load is electrically connected to the 
electric transmission system.22  Thus, this configuration does not need to be addressed by 
the PJM Tariff. 
Assuming a state or local regulator allows a generator, whether directly or through 
a sleeving entity, to serve co-located load and the generator is synchronized to the electric 
grid, and the regulator does not require the co-located load to pay for retail transmission 
service in the same manner as retail loads served by front of the meter generators, there 
are a variety of cost allocation options that can be explored.  The Commission should 
recognize that there will be no uniformity in the PJM region, as states and local regulators 
may make very different decisions on retail transmission service.  The California 
Utilities’ thoughts on situations where the state/local regulator does not require that co-
located load be treated the same as front of the meter retail load are discussed below.   
First, the California Utilities do not support generators synchronized to the grid 
serving co-located load paying no ancillary services costs, as they do not agree that either 
the generator or the co-located load are fully isolated.  For example, synchronized 
generators utilize ancillary services (such as Regulation) and benefit from reliability and 
recovery services such as Spinning Reserve and Black Start.  In such cases, it is up to the

D. 
Regional Differences May Require Different Approaches 
The California Utilities submit these Reply Comments relating to the PJM Tariff 
recognizing that the Commission’s decision here may impact the conversation in future 
proceedings regarding other RTO tariffs.  Nevertheless, it is important to recognize that 
approaches to co-located facilities will need to be tailored to account for different state 
regulators, self-regulated non-jurisdictional utilities, and regions.  For instance, as 
relevant to the California Utilities, any future CAISO Tariff provisions relating to co-
located facilities must necessarily function within California’s unique regulatory 
environment.  The California Utilities respectfully request that the Commission’s Order 
on PJM’s Tariff acknowledge state/local regulatory rights to prevent confusion and 
disputes.  Moreover, even without the state and local jurisdictional differences, there is no 
“one size fits all” just and reasonable approach to this complex issue.
