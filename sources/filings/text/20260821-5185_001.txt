I. 
Introduction 
The PSEG Companies appreciate the opportunity to provide comments in this proceeding 
following the July 23, 2026 PJM Governance Reform technical conference (“July technical 
conference”).  These comments address the questions posed in the Commission’s July 30 “Notice 
Requesting Post-Conference Comment.”1  
As both a founding member and significant Transmission Owner (“TO”) in PJM, PSEG 
offers a unique and informed perspective on PJM’s flawed governance structure.  As we have 
emphasized in this proceeding, and as the Commission has recognized, the current effort to 
effectuate governance changes in PJM is not, and cannot be, an academic exercise or one that 
only addresses “low hanging fruit.”  In this regard, not all changes are created equal; for 
example, whether all or only certain PJM Board meetings are open to the public is not as 
significant from a governance reform perspective as deciding who should have filing rights or 
how votes should be taken or how to establish clear and workable rules around the future role of 
the states in resource adequacy decision-making. 
Given the magnitude of the resource adequacy problem facing PJM, governance changes 
must be meaningful and advance better outcomes across the RTO – measured by (i) long-term

system reliability for customers; (ii) stable and sustainable market rules; (iii) a bold, empowered

of a comprehensive effort to revise PJM governance rules: 
• Amending the Consolidated Transmission Owners Agreement (“CTOA”) and the PJM

Tariff, thereby empowering PJM to make regional planning decisions; 
• Revising the current voting rules to either (i) reflect members’ actual stake in, and 
accountability for, PJM outcomes or (ii) employ full advisory voting; 
• Establishing enhanced accountability for PJM senior management and for its Board, 
which may include clear and transparent performance metrics and third-party evaluations 
of Board and management performance conducted on a regular cadence; and 
• Granting states a clear, defined role in PJM resource adequacy decisions to reflect their 
primary legal responsibility in this area, which could entail “jump ball” filing rights on 
matters pertaining to resource adequacy.  
We look forward to continuing to participate in shaping PJM governance rules as part of 
this proceeding, including as part of the upcoming Commission Alternative Dispute Resolution 
(“ADR”) process.2  With the fundamental objective of advancing better long-term outcomes at 
PJM, we offer the following responses to the Commission’s questions:

II. 
QUESTIONS 
PJM Board Independence 
1) Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years? 
Answer: Yes 
Comments: With the caveat that, in our view, the length of Board member terms is not a root 
cause of the current problems PJM is facing, there is a benefit to giving Board members 
sufficient time in their roles to acquire necessary expertise so that they can effectively understand 
the challenges facing the PJM region and provide meaningful direction to PJM leadership.  
2) Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may serve (e.g., 10 
years)?  
Answer: Yes 
Comments:  Establishing a term limit for Board members should encourage bold, independent 
thinking and fresh perspectives on PJM’s ongoing challenges.  
3) Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a preference for some 
Board members that reside in the PJM region? 
Answer: Yes 
Comments: It is appropriate to consider a multitude of factors when evaluating candidates for the 
PJM Board.  These factors could include (i) residency; (ii) other “significant ties” to the region; 
(iii) knowledge of PJM operations and/or markets; and (iv) other requirements to help select 
Board members with requisite expertise and independent decision-making. 
4) Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off period for Board 
members from two years to one year?
--- PAGE 4 ---
4 
 
Answer: Yes 
Comments: The existing two-year cooling-off period may make it difficult to find qualified 
Board members.  Having rules in place that limit the available pool of Board candidates is 
counter-productive, particularly given the current challenges facing PJM.  
5) Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable more communication 
between the Board and stakeholders?  
Answer: Yes 
Comments: We support appropriately bounded enhancements that will enable stakeholders to 
better understand the PJM Board’s decision-making process and to bolster confidence in this 
process. 
6) In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the Board be 
required to explain its reasoning?  
Answer: Yes  
Comments: As explained in response to Question 5 above, it is important for PJM stakeholders to 
be able to understand Board decisions.  Therefore, at a minimum the Board should be required to 
explain its reasoning.  As part of the upcoming ADR process, PJM and its stakeholders should 
discuss whether making Board votes public would chill decision-making at the Board level or 
whether it instead makes sense to publicize these votes given that Members Committee votes are 
publicly recorded.  
7) In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or available to the 
public? 
Answer: Yes

and avoiding chilling independent Board decision-making.  As such, this question merits

8) Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the responsibility of 
PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest?  
Answer: This question requires further consideration as to whether adding this responsibility is 
needed on top of PJM’s existing, FERC-approved responsibilities and whether/how “serving the 
public interest” would be defined in this context.  
Comments:  In Order No. 2000, the Commission established the following requirements for an 
RTO: independence from market participants; a region of sufficient scope and configuration to 
permit the RTO to effectively perform its required functions and to support efficient and 
nondiscriminatory power markets; possession of operational authority for all transmission 
facilities under the RTO's control; and exclusive authority to maintain short-term reliability.3  
Significantly, Order No. 2000 did not include a “public interest” requirement governing RTO 
responsibilities. 
  
PJM, which became a FERC-approved RTO in 2001,4 is not a regulator but is instead a 
public utility under the Federal Power Act, subject to Sections 205 and 206 of the Federal Power 
Act.  Both statutory provisions require that public utility jurisdictional rates and services must be

“just and reasonable.”5  These provisions do not superimpose a “public interest” standard on top 
of the “just and reasonable” standard. 
Under the PJM Operating Agreement, PJM’s core responsibilities include (1) ensuring the 
safe and reliable operation of the PJM Region; (2) establishing and operating a “robust, 
competitive, and non-discriminator electric power markets in the PJM Region;” and (3) ensuring 
that “a member shall not have undue influence over the operation of the PJM Region.”6  Each of 
these core elements aligns with its responsibilities under Order No. 2000 and its FERC-approved 
governing documents.  
PJM’s May 2026 whitepaper on market design reforms, entitled “Powering Reliability 
Through Market Design,” illustrates the challenges that occur when PJM seeks to make decisions 
through a nebulous “public interest” lens rather than through a lens that focuses on its core 
functions.  In our view, it simply creates indecision.  Rather than presenting a recommended 
market design to address the ongoing and critical resource adequacy problem, PJM’s whitepaper 
states that decisions on future market design “belong to the people and institutions with 
democratic accountability for their consequences – to state regulators and legislatures, to FERC, 
to consumers and the advocates who represent them.”7  What this really means is that PJM is not 
focusing on discharging its core, FERC-approved responsibilities but is instead creating 
decisional paralysis by trying to ascertain what regulators, legislators, consumers and 
“advocates” believe is in the “public interest.”

There are a myriad of fundamental questions and challenges that would flow from 
overlaying an ill-defined “public interest” standard on top of PJM’s responsibilities.  A few 
examples: 
• Given PJM’s Order No. 1920-required 20-year transmission planning cycle, how would 
the “public interest” be measured in that context?  More fundamentally, what do “public 
interest” outcomes look like in the Regional Transmission Expansion Process (“RTEP”) 
process where there are significant upfront capital costs associated with infrastructure 
projects but long-term reliability and economic benefits? 
• What is a “public interest” outcome in the capacity or energy markets, and who gets to 
decide that and over what time frame? 
• Would the overlay of a “public interest” lens on planning, operations and markets 
decisions ultimately result in a less reliable system? 
We are not suggesting that acting in the “public interest” should be ignored, as it cannot 
be ignored.  From the Transmission Owner perspective, we are a fully regulated utility tasked 
with providing reliable customer service at just and reasonable rates.  We make reliability and 
cost effectiveness a central consideration in all of our decision-making.  However, we challenge 
the notion that PJM should be tasked with ownership of a “public interest” standard.  Asking 
PJM to morph from a public utility to a “quasi-regulator” is establishing a role for PJM that is 
outside of its FERC-determined authority and, most importantly, will not produce better 
outcomes at PJM.  Instead, it will bog down PJM decision-making even further, and inhibit bold, 
independent, timely and decisive action.  
9) Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the Nominating 
Committee?

Answer: Yes 
Comments: Given the states’ responsibility for resource adequacy, we support a more formal role 
for the states in PJM governance as pertains to resource adequacy.  While that role can take many 
forms, it must be linked to the broader goal of improved outcomes in PJM in the areas of reliable 
system planning and stable, well-functioning markets, an effective stakeholder process and

membership on the Nominating Committee is one avenue to offer the states a concrete “seat at

10) Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management compensation to their 
achievement?  
Answer: Yes 
Comments: As we explained in our pre-technical conference comments, it is important for PJM 
to be empowered to make independent, difficult and sometimes unpopular but necessary 
decisions.  We specifically noted the existence of corporate governance principles that PJM and 
the Board could adopt to foster accountability and responsiveness from PJM leadership to the 
stakeholders, and clear, transparent performance metrics should be part of the equation.  As an 
example, we recommend the use of a third party to regularly evaluate the performance and 
effectiveness of its senior leadership.  Figuring out all of the mechanics of establishing 
performance metrics and monitoring those metrics is less important at this juncture than 
engaging in an earnest effort to increase organizational accountability for PJM.  
Section 205 Filing Rights 
11) Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes only inform the Board?
--- PAGE 9 ---
9 
 
Answer: Yes.  Current PJM voting rules must change; however, our preferred approach would be 
to equate voting strength with membership responsibility for PJM outcomes, including system 
reliability.  Our alternative approach would be advisory voting, along with enhanced PJM 
accountability for its decision making. 
Comments: As highlighted in our pre-technical conference comments, PJM’s voting structure no 
longer works to produce effective outcomes.  The current voting structure should be revised so 
that votes reflect a member’s actual stake in PJM outcomes, and we have proposed various ways 
to revise the voting structure accordingly (see our response to Question 16 below).  Alternatively, 
we understand that other ISOs/RTOs utilize a full advisory voting construct and that one could be 
adopted by PJM.  Such an approach would shift the ultimate responsibility for decision-making 
onto PJM.  We are wary of the challenges PJM has faced with its capacity market rules, an area 
where stakeholder voting is already advisory.  However, we are willing to consider an advisory 
voting construct in PJM to encourage its independence and decisiveness.  However, this 
approach must be accompanied by enhanced accountability, including for the PJM Board, so that 
members have confidence in the decisions PJM is making.  
12) Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over the energy market and ancillary services 
unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members 
Committee?  
Answer: Yes 
Comments: If accompanied by measures to ensure bold, independent and reliability-focused 
decision-making by PJM, we could consider this change as part of a comprehensive, negotiated 
resolution.

13) Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over Regional Transmission Expansion 
Planning (RTEP) unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the 
Members Committee?  
Answer: Yes 
Comments: As we stated in our pre-technical conference comments, PJM is the only RTO that 
does not have independent Section 205 filing rights over transmission planning, and this 
undermines PJM’s independence to plan the grid.  Both the CTOA and the OA must be revised to 
empower PJM to make effective regional planning decisions.  In addition, the PJM Transmission 
Owners must have audit rights to hold PJM accountable for its performance under the CTOA.   
14) Should the Organization of PJM States Inc (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights over resource 
adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a supermajority of OPSI agrees on an 
alternative proposal? 
Answer: Yes, as pertains to resource adequacy.  No, as pertains to transmission cost allocation. 
Comments: Resource adequacy is the most significant problem currently confronting PJM and its 
stakeholders.  As we noted in our pre-technical conference comments, in contrast to the states 
PJM has only “indirect responsibility for resource adequacy through its use of market pricing 
signals,” and “(t)here is a pressing need for clarity on the respective responsibilities between 
PJM and the states on resource adequacy.”8  Because the states have primary responsibility for 
resource adequacy, in this limited situation, allowing OPSI to have “jump ball” rights or a 
comparable mechanism is a logical and appropriate step towards enhanced state accountability 
on the issue.

16) Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered from two-thirds 
sector-weighted voting? 
Answer: Yes 
Comments: PJM’s voting structure results in asset owners with responsibility for customer 
reliability having a significantly diluted vote. Sector-weighted voting must change to reflect the 
current membership composition, voting behaviors (including “sector blocking” that occurs 
when two of the five sectors vote together under the two-thirds construct) and the daunting 
challenges PJM is facing.  There are multiple approaches that may be considered, ranging from a 
realignment of voting interests to reflect member responsibilities/accountabilities12 to an 
advisory voting construct.  In addition, revising the current senior voting committee threshold to 
a lower value (e.g., NYISO employs a 58% threshold for Management Committee votes) would

17) Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing committees, 
also apply to lower-level committees?  
Answer: No 
Comments: Although PSEG’s vote constitutes only about 1.5% of the total vote in PJM senior

State Participation 
18) In addition to OPSI, should there be a second state representative group consisting of 
governors’ representatives and/or state legislators’ representatives?   
Answer: Yes, subject to comments below 
Comments: We are less concerned with the exact number of regional state committees that are in 
place and more concerned that any committee(s) established to address resource adequacy have 
clearly defined roles and responsibilities vis-à-vis those of PJM and the transmission owners. 
Absent that clarity, additional voices from the states in a new state representative group will lead 
to confusion and delay.

to certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional transmission cost allocation) in 
addition to the consumer advocate votes?  
Answer: Yes, subject to comments below

Comments: Noting the response to the prior question concerning clearly defined roles and 
responsibilities, we would support a formal voting role for state representatives in the PJM 
stakeholder process with respect to resource adequacy.  The PJM states have authority to address
