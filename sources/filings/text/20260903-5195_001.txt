COMMENTS OF THE CHIEF ENERGY OFFICER  
FOR THE GOVERNOR OF VIRGINIA 
The Chief Energy Officer for the Governor of the Commonwealth of Virginia respectfully 
submits these comments regarding PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) proposed Interim 
Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”) and Large Load Registry. 
Introduction 
Virginia’s central priority is straightforward: the Commonwealth must be able to meet 
growing electricity demand with an affordable, reliable, and clean energy system, while protecting 
households and businesses from costs driven by the rapid growth in large load demand. Achieving 
that outcome requires that large-load customers pay their fair share for the capacity and 
infrastructure needed to serve them, and that PJM’s market rules provide strong incentives for 
those customers to reduce their impact on the grid. 
PJM’s proposal is an important step, but the current IRAS design does not yet establish 
sufficiently clear, robust, technology-neutral pathways for large loads to pursue and receive credit 
for beneficial grid investments. We support refinements that ensure the program adequately 
rewards measurable peak-demand reductions, enables demand-side and distributed-energy 
resources to participate fully, and appropriately assigns curtailment risk to the loads associated 
with the identified resource adequacy concern. 
Areas of Support  
Virginia supports several important elements of PJM’s proposal.
--- PAGE 2 ---
First, large load customers that drive new system needs should bear an appropriate share 
of associated costs and reliability obligations. This principle is essential to protect households, 
small businesses, and existing customers in Virginia and throughout PJM from cost shifting as 
large load electricity demands grow. 
Second, Virginia supports PJM’s proposed approach to curtailment priority. In an 
emergency, curtailment risk should fall first on new large loads that have not adequately secured 
capacity or reduced their contribution to system peak demand, rather than on residential customers, 
small businesses, or demand-response participants that have already made firm and compensated 
reliability commitments. 
Third, Virginia supports PJM’s recognition that large loads can contribute to reliability by 
bringing new capacity, reducing peak demand, or deploying distributed and demand-side solutions. 
Distributed energy resources (“DERs”), demand response, energy storage, and other demand-
flexibility tools can be deployed more quickly and cost-effectively than traditional infrastructure. 
PJM should maintain and continue to enhance its technology-neutral rules that allow all resources 
capable of providing measurable reliability value to compete on fair terms. 
Areas of Concern  
The Commission should ensure that PJM’s final IRAS framework better reflects these 
principles. In particular, there are two areas that we encourage the Commission to address:  
1. Credit verified peak shaving  
PJM’s proposal does not reduce a large load’s IRAS curtailment exposure when that 
customer has secured verified demand reductions, including through the Peak Shaving Adjustment 
mechanism. Similarly, the proposal does not establish a clear mechanism through which 
measurable load reductions can reduce a large load’s allocated RBP costs. These gaps should be
--- PAGE 3 ---
corrected so that large loads receive appropriate credit for actions that reduce their impact on the 
grid. Properly valuing verified demand-side reductions will help address PJM’s resource adequacy 
needs, mitigate capacity-cost pressures, and advance regional reliability and affordability. 
A large load that secured verified demand reductions to reduce its impact on peak demand 
should receive a corresponding reduction in its IRAS curtailment exposure. Large loads that take 
measurable steps to reduce their impact on the grid—whether through verified demand reductions 
or by bringing new capacity online—should receive a corresponding reduction in curtailment 
exposure and be curtailed only after other large loads that have not taken such steps. 
This should also apply to reduction in Reliability Backstop Procurement (RBP) cost 
allocations, with large loads that secure verified demand reductions (such as through the Peak 
Shaving Adjustment) being appropriately credited for their reduction in impacts on the grid.  
Virginia has already acted to establish pathways for voluntary demand-flexibility programs 
that enable large loads to secure measurable and verifiable load reductions (see Virginia Code § 
56-596.7). PJM’s rules should provide a clear, timely, and administrable process through which 
these state-supported arrangements can be recognized in IRAS as well as the RBP cost allocation, 
with such reductions accounted for in a customer’s curtailment exposure and related obligations.  
This is an essential area for PJM to clarify to enable demand-side programs to be viable 
and valuable in supporting PJM’s capacity needs.  
2. Enable meaningful DER participation 
As we previously shared with the PJM Board,1 Virginia supports PJM’s effort to create 
pathways for DERs and demand-side resources to participate through peak-shaving mechanisms 
and through opportunities for large loads to bring new capacity. But eligibility on paper is not

enough. The rules must work in practice for the resources that can provide reliability value most 
quickly. 
PJM should avoid requirements that arbitrarily limit or disadvantage demand-side 
resources relative to traditional generation. In particular, PJM should ensure that its definitions of 
qualifying or “new” capacity recognize incremental capacity additions, including upgrades and 
reactivations, new investments at existing sites, or returning sites that have not recently

A technology-neutral structure that evaluates resources based on their demonstrated 
reliability contribution—not the particular technology or business model used to deliver it—will 
expand competition, strengthen reliability, and reduce costs for consumers. 
Conclusion 
Virginia is committed to continuing its leadership in addressing resource adequacy, 
protecting ratepayers, and advancing cost-effective energy solutions. Just this year, the 
Commonwealth acted to dramatically expand energy storage requirements for utilities,2 ease the 
path for large load customers to fund new zero-carbon dispatchable resources,3 support fair cost 
allocation,4 create a voluntary demand flexibility program for large loads,5 and other tools needed 
to meet this moment responsibly. 
PJM must now do its part. The Commission should ensure that the IRAS framework fairly 
assigns responsibility to new large loads, prioritizes those loads for curtailment when they have 
not sufficiently addressed their reliability obligations, and provides meaningful credit for verified 
peak shaving, DERs, and other demand-side resources in addition to bring-your-own-new-capacity
