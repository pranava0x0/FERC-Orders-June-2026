In response to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or 
“Commission”) Notice Requesting Post-Technical Conference Comments, the Financial 
Marketers Coalition and DC Energy, LLC (“Coalition”) submits these comments on the 
stakeholder and governance issues facing PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).  Members of 
the Coalition watched the July 23, 2026 Technical Conference on PJM Governance and 
Stakeholder Reforms (“PJM Technical Conference”) with interest and provide these comments 
to advance the conversation around these critical issues.  Overall the Coalition values PJM and 
the PJM markets and believes that PJM’s governance and stakeholder issues can be fixed with 
leadership and a willingness by all interested parties to engage with all stakeholders, states and 
other interested parties.  
I. 
Introduction  
 
In PJM, the Financial Marketers Coalition is a group of Other Supplier Sector market 
participants who participate in the Day-Ahead, Real-Time and FTR markets.  The companies 
represent a diverse range of market participants, from power marketers who import and export 
electricity between regions, to FTR market participants, to those who transact in virtual energy 
products.  DC Energy, LLC is a Member of PJM in the Other Supplier Sector.  In PJM, DC 
Energy, LLC and its affiliates primarily participate in the FTR market.  We are active 
participants in the PJM stakeholder process and look forward to being part of the solution to 
PJM’s longstanding governance problems.

II.

A. 
PJM Board Independence  
 
The Coalition believes that the PJM Board has a significant opportunity to increase its 
transparency and its engagement with PJM’s stakeholders. While the Board’s independence is 
important, the Board must also be well-educated on the PJM markets, the various types of PJM 
market participants and the concerns of those market participants.  Otherwise the Board is 
making critical decisions in a vacuum, advised only by PJM Staff which may bring to bear its 
own preconceptions.  
1. 
Board Member Terms and Qualifications  
 
The Coalition agrees with the many panelists who noted that a three-year term for a 
Board member is relatively short.  Each Board member’s term could be increased by a year or 
two, up to an individual term of no more than five (5) years.  Board members should also be 
permitted to be renominated for an additional term, allowing them to serve up to ten (10) years.  
Maximum terms any longer than 10 years would not result in sufficient turnover on the PJM 
Board to allow for new ideas and fresh leadership.  
 
We found Mr. Mills’ statement that PJM Board members often spend a year learning 
the acronyms to be troubling.  A Board member should be familiar with the PJM markets, and 
ISO/RTO markets overall. New Board members may need to undergo more extensive training 
so that they begin their tenure on the Board with basic familiarity with PJM’s markets.  
Residence in the PJM footprint by itself will not give an understanding of the PJM markets, 
nor will it give the Board member sufficient “skin in the game” beyond being a ratepayer and 
customer of one of the load-serving entities in the region. The Board member qualification 
rules should be revisited and revised to focus on Board members who are familiar with the PJM 
markets, understand the challenges and opportunities faced by PJM, and who bring particularly

2. 
Communications with the Board  
 
We believe that the PJM Board needs greater interaction with the stakeholders.  
Currently that interaction takes place through the quarterly Liaison Committee meetings, and 
annual meetings between the PJM Board and the individual sectors.  The Liaison Committee 
serves an important role and should not be eliminated, but by itself, it does not yield sufficient 
transparency or engagement.  The annual sector meetings can be difficult because they require 
a very senior level of engagement which may not be viable for some companies.  In this context, 
the Commission’s sunshine rules can provide a helpful example.  Companies may meet with 
individual Commissioners to discuss issues of broad policy concern or pre-filing concerns 
regarding something the Commission may be facing soon, though may not discuss pending 
matters pursuant to the ex parte rules.  Similar meetings should be permitted with Board 
members, with appropriate safeguards in place, such as participation by an attorney from the 
PJM Office of General Counsel or PJM’s outside counsel, who would serve a role similar to 
that of a Commissioner’s Assistant.  
3. 
Transparency  
 
The Coalition believes that the PJM Board needs to significantly increase its 
transparency.  Board votes should be made public.  Board members must have the leadership 
and strength of conviction to publicly own their decisions.  The Coalition notes that stakeholder 
votes in the Members Committee (“MC”) were previously public and published following each 
MC meeting.  PJM appears to have stopped publishing such votes in 2025, as the last voting 
report we found was from the November 19, 2025 Special MC on Large Load Additions.1

Similarly, some portion of PJM Board meetings should be made public. While the 
Coalition appreciates the need for open conversation within the confines of the Board room, 
the PJM Board could open part of its meetings to the PJM community. As a comparison, MISO 
holds a bi-monthly Board Week where the various committees of the MISO Board meet in 
open session, culminating in an open session of the MISO Board. On occasion, the MISO 
Board also meets in camera, closed to the public.  This model could be helpful to increase the 
PJM Board’s transparency.  
 
While we do not oppose the addition of a public interest standard to the governing 
documents for both PJM and the PJM Board, we note that both PJM’s and the PJM Board’s 
obligations are not limited to the public interest.  The public interest is clearly a critical element 
of PJM’s and the PJM Board’s obligations.  As directors of a corporation, the PJM Board 
directors also have obligations to the corporation itself.  They carry a Duty of Care, whereby 
they must make informed and active choices by being prepared prior to voting.  They carry a 
Duty of Loyalty, to put the good of the corporation ahead of their personal gain.  They also 
carry a Duty of Obedience and Good Faith whereby they must act in honesty and in compliance 
with the law and PJM’s governing documents. At bottom, their role is to hire a strong PJM 
CEO who will then build a management team and run the day-to-day management activities of 
the corporation.

5. 
Public Performance Metrics  
 
While the concept of tying PJM management compensation and bonuses to public 
performance metrics is promising, the idea requires far greater consideration and discussion.  
At bottom, any performance metrics must be public.  At times it has appeared that PJM Staff 
is advocating for a particular issue because it is tied to their bonus or compensation, or a Board 
directive to make a change – any change. Such nonpublic tying does not make for good 
decision-making as it gives PJM Staff a personal investment in a policy decision that may or 
may not be beneficial to PJM as a whole. Further, any such metrics must be vetted and 
considered to ensure that they are clear and attainable.

B. 
Section 205 Filing Rights  
 
By sharing 205 filing rights with the stakeholders, PJM has been a laboratory of 
collaboration and stakeholder rights.  While we recognize that making a change in the 205 
filing rights over issues in the energy markets might help expedite certain issues, as several 
panelists noted during the technical conference, PJM and the PJM Board both have tools in 
their toolboxes at this time to expedite resolution of critical issues.  At a time when bold 
leadership is necessary from both PJM and the PJM Board, turning the PJM stakeholder process 
into a purely advisory body may result in even less leadership and action.  We do hold a 
different position on filing rights related to RTEP, as PJM is charged with serving as the Order 
No. 1000 regional planning authority for the PJM region.  
 
We believe that the suggestion of a “jump ball” to be incorporated into PJM’s Tariff is 
a good idea.  OPSI should be one of the parties to have the right to file, or ask PJM to file, a 
second “jump ball” style proposal.  This right, however, should not be limited to OPSI, 
particularly at a time when OPSI itself may be changing or may be joined by a second state 
organization.  The jump ball concept should be expanded to include a majority of PJM 
stakeholders in a situation where the PJM Board decides to pursue an option contrary to that 
approved by the stakeholders, or that failed to receive the requisite two-thirds sector weighted 
vote but otherwise was supported by a significant percentage of stakeholders.

C. 
Stakeholder Process  
 
The Coalition is an active participant in the PJM stakeholder process, a member of the 
Other Supplier Sector (OSS) which has 321 members out of the current 509 PJM voting 
members. This sector is the “catch-all” for market participants who do not fit into any of the 
other sectors. It includes financial market participants, demand response providers, 
transmission and generation developers prior to their projects’ energization, competitive retail 
electric suppliers and others.  As such, each individual OSS sector member’s votes are far more 
diluted than the votes of market participants in other sectors.  The below chart sets out the

We do believe that changes are necessary to the PJM stakeholder process.  We do not believe, 
however, that changes to voting, whether to the sector-weighted voting in the Members 
Committee or the affiliate votes in the lower-level committees, by themselves will make 
significant changes to the problems faced by PJM.  These steps are more likely to 
disenfranchise non-transmission and non-generation owning PJM market participants without 
making meaningful changes to the problems bogging down the process.  
 
We do think that prioritization of issues to be addressed by PJM in the coming year 
would be very valuable.  Several ISO/RTOs, including the New York Independent System 
Operator (“NYISO”) and the California Independent System Operator Corp. (“CAISO”), 
undergo a roadmap process each year.  A similar roadmap could be very helpful to PJM.  
Broadly, in both ISO/RTOs, stakeholders, the ISO/RTO Staff, the states and the market 
monitors provide input on the issues to be prioritized for the following year.  The ISO/RTO 
then provides a workplan and a report at year’s end on where various issues stand.  In CAISO, 
CAISO kicks off the roadmap process in January for the following year.5  There are a number 
of meetings over the following year, providing stakeholders and other interested parties with 
the opportunity to provide input.  By the end of the year, CAISO has a Final Policy Initiatives 
Roadmap for the following year.6  Similarly, NYISO kicks off a project budget 
recommendation through its Budget and Priorities Working Group in August, with a goal to 
have a complete prioritization by the end of the year.7  PJM could similarly design a roadmap

D. 
State Participation  
 
In the PJM Technical Conference as well as conversations in the PJM stakeholder 
process and other fora, states’ desire for a greater voice and greater participation has been heard 
loud and clear. We therefore support the states’ desire for such a voice.  We defer to the states 
on whether that voice takes the shape of a second state representative group, an expansion of 
OPSI or another form. We note, however, that if multiple groups are formed, such groups must 
coordinate with one another so that PJM and its stakeholders do not need to balance divergent 
positions from multiple state groups.
