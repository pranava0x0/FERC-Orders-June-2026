BEFORE THE FEDERAL ENERGY REGULATORY COMMISSION 
Technical Conference on the PJM Interconnection Stakeholder Process and Governance 
July 23, 2026 
Written Statement of Suzanne Jaworowski 
Secretary of Energy and Natural Resources, State of Indiana Office of Governor Mike Braun 
Introduction 
Chairman and Commissioners, thank you for convening this technical conference and for the 
opportunity to testify. I serve as Indiana's Secretary of Energy and Natural Resources in the 
administration of Governor Mike Braun. Indiana sits at the seam of two regional transmission 
organizations: most of our state is served by MISO, while Indiana Michigan Power serves a 
substantial portion of northeastern Indiana within PJM. That dual vantage point, together with 
our responsibility for one of the fastest-growing large-load pipelines in the country, informs the 
perspective I offer today. 
Indiana's message is straightforward. PJM has served this region well for nearly a century, but 
the demands now converging on it — unprecedented load growth, resource interconnection 
backlogs, transmission expansion, and capacity price outcomes that have become politically 
intolerable in several states — have outpaced a stakeholder process designed for a slower era. 
Governor Braun joined fellow PJM governors in a Statement of Principles because the states' 
confidence in PJM's governance has eroded. Of the four multi-state RTOs, the states' voice in PJM 
is the most muted. That must change, and it can change through a focused package of reforms 
drawn from proven practice across the organized markets. 
My testimony draws substantially on the governance recommendations recently advanced by Pat 
Wood III, former Chairman of this Commission, which Indiana broadly endorses. They are 
grounded in a generation of experience across all seven U.S. RTOs and ISOs, and they answer the 
questions the Commission has posed for this panel. 
Strengthening the State Role (Question 3) 
The Federal Power Act establishes a federal-state partnership, and RTOs perform functions whose 
practical effects span both jurisdictions. Yet the structured opportunities for state participation in 
PJM today are inadequate. The Organization of PJM States (OPSI) communicates on a consensus 
basis, which leaves it ill-suited to convey state positions when states diverge, and states are 
otherwise left to write letters to a Board that has no obligation to act on them. Indiana 
recommends two structural reforms: 
• Establish bifurcated state engagement channels. Mirroring the New England model, 
PJM should complement OPSI's technical regulatory role with a PJM States' Committee on 
Electricity (PJMSCOE) focused on state policy and economic development perspectives. 
PJMSCOE should have formal standing at PJM, a defined right to bring issues directly to the 
Board, and the duty to appoint three state members to the current eight-member Board 
Nominating Committee, which includes the PJM CEO as a voting member. One of those 
appointees could be the OPSI President.

• Clarify the Regional State Committee's authority. Indiana further supports vesting 
the states, acting through their Regional State Committee, with Federal Power Act Section 
205 filing rights over the matters where state jurisdictional interest is clearest — resource 
adequacy program structure and the state-facing elements of regional transmission cost 
allocation. This is not a novel arrangement: SPP's Regional State Committee has exercised 
Section 205 authority over cost allocation for two decades, and it has produced durable 
regional compacts rather than serial litigation. Filing rights are what convert consultation 
into partnership. A state position that PJM may note and set aside is advice; a state 
position that reaches this Commission on its own Section 205 footing carries both voice 
and responsibility. Consistent with the Federal Power Act's federal-state design, Indiana 
urges that any reform of PJM's governing documents give the states that footing. 
 
Voting Thresholds and Committee Structure (Questions 1, 6, and 7) 
Sector-weighted voting has value, but the current design no longer strikes the right balance 
between consensus and timely action. A two-thirds supermajority across all matters gives 
incumbent coalitions an effective veto over reform, and independent analysis has documented the 
consequences: five voting sectors unchanged since 2000 despite dramatic membership growth, 
per-firm vote dilution in the oversubscribed supply sectors, average member participation of 
roughly 20 percent at the senior standing committees, and lower-level committee rules that allow 
affiliate voting to skew outcomes toward supply-side interests. The result is a process where 
proposals grind through lower committees only to die at the top, leaving the Board — and 
ultimately this Commission — to resolve what the stakeholder process could not. 
Indiana supports the following reforms: 
• Move to an advisory stakeholder model. Non-binding stakeholder voting is the 
approach taken in every other U.S. RTO and ISO — now including ERCOT — and it works 
well. It should be paired with a more structured engagement between the Members 
Committee and the Board, akin to that employed in SPP, so that stakeholder counsel is 
elevated rather than diminished even as the veto arithmetic ends. 
• If the current structure is retained, bifurcate the threshold. Leave the 
supermajority at two-thirds for governance issues, and apply a simple majority to everything 
else. This preserves stability where stability matters most while restoring the ability to act 
on market design. 
• Align lower-committee voting with the Members Committee. In either case, the 
voting makeup of lower committees should mirror the sector-weighted structure of the 
Members Committee, eliminating the structural bias introduced by unweighted affiliate 
voting at the working level. 
Transparency and Accountability (Question 2) 
Transparency reforms are the fastest way to rebuild stakeholder and public confidence in PJM's 
decision-making. Today, lower-level voting behavior is generally not disclosed, meeting minutes 
convey little substance to non-participants, and the Board rarely explains its reasoning. Indiana 
recommends that PJM:

• Amend the governing charter to make clear that Board members have a 
responsibility to the public interest, consistent with the Federal Power Act. Public 
legitimacy begins with a publicly stated duty. 
• Publish individual voting results for votes taken by stakeholders throughout the 
committee structure. 
• Adopt public annual and quarterly performance metrics and tie management 
compensation to their achievement, as MISO does. 
• Publish all Board ethics documents on PJM's public website, as ERCOT does, and 
prioritize selection of Board members who live in the PJM footprint. 
• Modernize Board ex parte practices to allow more direct discussion with customers, 
stakeholders, elected officials, and regulators, subject to appropriate disclosure. Insulating 
the Board from the region it serves has not produced better decisions — it has produced less 
accountable ones. 
Governing Documents, Expedition, and Lessons from Other Regions 
(Questions 4, 5, 8, and 9) 
Several provisions of PJM's governing documents impede timely decision-making, and 
implementing the reforms above will require amendments to those documents — the 
supermajority provisions, the lower-committee voting rules, and the informality of the Board's 
override authority all live there. Indiana takes no position today on the procedural vehicle. What 
matters is that the amendments be made, and made quickly, so that PJM, the states, and 
stakeholders can turn their full attention to the substantive challenges ahead. 
On expedition: the Board's exceptional override authority — the "critical issue fast path" 
designation — should be formalized with crisp procedures, including public articulation of the 
basis for its decisions and a final stakeholder consultation before filing. Fast-path tools are 
appropriate for high-importance topics, but they must be rule-bound and transparent so that 
speed does not come at the price of legitimacy or circumvent meaningful stakeholder deliberation. 
Finally, the Commission asked what PJM can learn from other regions. Each element of the 
reform package Indiana supports is drawn from practice that has worked elsewhere: New 
England's bifurcated state committees; the advisory stakeholder model that operates in every 
other RTO and ISO, now including ERCOT; SPP's structured engagement between its members 
and its Board; MISO's public performance metrics tied to compensation; and ERCOT's published 
ethics standards. None of this is experimental. PJM would simply be adopting the best of what a 
generation of RTO experience has already proven. 
A Closing Note on Affordability 
Governance reform matters because affordability is now the central energy question in every PJM 
state, including Indiana. Recent capacity-market price outcomes became politically intolerable in 
significant part because default-service retail load across the footprint was not adequately hedged. 
States will need to address that on their side of the jurisdictional line — but PJM retains a critical 
role in managing the residual capacity market and allocating its costs, and every state needs the

Principles and the ratepayer protections large customers have pledged. A PJM that hears its states

Big issues await action by PJM and the states. Indiana urges the Commission and the PJM 
community to resolve these governance questions quickly, so all of us can focus on the future. 
Thank you, and I welcome your questions.
