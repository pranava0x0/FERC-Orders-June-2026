Pursuant to Rules 211 and 214 of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC 
or Commission) Rules of Practice and Procedure,1 Corporate Energy Buyers Association 
(CEBA) respectfully submits this motion to intervene and protest. CEBA urges the Commission 
to reject the proposal by PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) to revise the Reliability Assurance 
Agreement Among Load Serving Entities (RAA) and the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff 
(Tariff) to establish Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS). In the alternative, and as 
detailed below, if the Commission accepts PJM’s proposal, it should: (1) condition that 
acceptance on a minor modification needed to fully recognize customer-sponsored Peak Shaving 
Adjustments as incremental new capacity within the IRAS construct; and (2) encourage PJM to 
work with stakeholders on reforms that unlock the full potential of Surplus Interconnection 
Service to bring incremental capacity to the region quickly and cost-effectively.

II. 
COMMENTS 
CEBA supports the broader policy objectives underlying PJM’s proposal: large 
customers that are driving significant load growth should be encouraged to support solutions that 
help solve regional resource adequacy challenges. However, PJM’s proposal is fatally flawed in 
three ways. First, IRAS fails to promote the certainty needed for large load customers to support 
investment in new capacity because PJM promises a benefit—exemption from curtailment 
during IRAS Actions—for which it lacks authority. Second, IRAS impermissibly conditions 
access to transmission service based on attributes of retail customers and, in so doing, blurs the 
line between Federal and state jurisdiction. Third, IRAS unduly preferences Capacity Resources 
over investments that reduce the underlying capacity need, such as customer-sponsored Peak

A. 
IRAS FAILS TO PROMOTE THE CERTAINTY NEEDED TO SUPPORT 
INVESTMENT IN NEW CAPACITY IN THE PJM REGION 
PJM asserts that IRAS will incent large load customers to help underwrite the cost of new 
capacity in the PJM region—either through long-term bilateral contracts or through the 
allocation of capacity costs from the Reliability Backstop Procurement (RBP).3 CEBA agrees 
that large energy customers can play an important role in advancing resource development 
through long-term contractual commitments that help make new generation projects financeable. 
Indeed, our members have a well-documented track record of doing that across markets 
nationwide.4 However, such commitments are only viable when the customer has a clear

IRAS fails that test. Under PJM’s proposal, a large load customer could enter into 
decade-plus commitments to support the development of new capacity resources that satisfy the 
requirements necessary to avoid taking IRAS. Yet, PJM cannot ensure that those commitments 
will result in any defined level of curtailment protection or service reliability for that customer. 
As PJM itself acknowledges, those are fundamentally terms and conditions of retail service, 
which are not governed by PJM’s tariffs but instead fall squarely within state jurisdiction.5  
More specifically, PJM issues load curtailment directives to Electric Distributors. 
Individual Electric Distributors then determine which retail customers, such as Large Loads, are 
curtailed in response to any PJM directive based on the specific conditions at the time, consistent 
with state laws and regulations. Under this split construct, PJM has no authority or ability to 
ensure that an individual New Large Load that brings its own new capacity and/or is allocated 
capacity through the RBP can avoid curtailment when PJM initiates an IRAS Action. That is 
because policies and procedures regarding curtailment of retail customers sit within each Electric 
Distributor’s retail tariff, which are subject to exclusive state jurisdiction.  
As a result, PJM’s proposal attempts to induce investment by promising a benefit that 
PJM itself lacks the legal authority to offer. In other words, a large load customer could secure 
new capacity to avoid curtailment under IRAS, have that new capacity recognized appropriately 
in the Large Load Registry, and yet the customer could still be curtailed during an IRAS Action

because of policy choices made by state regulators with respect to curtailment priority among 
retail customers. 
This deficiency undermines PJM’s central premise that IRAS will stimulate investment in 
new generation resources. Customers considering long-term commitments to support new 
capacity require confidence that the benefits associated with those commitments are clearly 
defined, enforceable, and durable over the life of the investment. Where the principal benefit 
remains uncertain and dependent on decisions outside PJM’s authority, customers face 
diminished incentives to undertake the long-term obligations necessary to finance and develop 
new generation resources. 
If policymakers wish to encourage large customers to support the development of new 
generation resources through long-term contractual commitments, the foundation for such a 
framework should instead be established at the retail level, where curtailment rights and service 
obligations are determined. It is the states that each must decide the specific benefits available to 
customers that make qualifying investments, including the extent of any curtailment protection 
or service rights associated with those investments.

SERVICE BASED ON ATTRIBUTES OF RETAIL CUSTOMERS  
PJM’s proposal, if accepted, would also set troubling precedent for gatekeeping access to 
transmission service6 based on attributes of individual retail customers. To implement IRAS,

PJM would classify a retail customer based on its size, location, affiliation, in-service date, ramp 
schedule, capacity arrangements, backup generation, and contracts or service agreements. That 
information would be used to determine whether the customer is an Eligible New Large Load 
(i.e., eligible to provide IRAS) and the amount of its load that may be subject to curtailment. 
PJM’s assertion that it is merely directing load reductions in the aggregate by Electric Distributor 
zone/area7 is a misdirection from the fact that those directives are calculated based on the 
identities and retail service characteristics of individual customers.8 This approach risks blurring 
the Federal Power Act’s division between Commission-jurisdictional transmission and wholesale 
service and state-jurisdictional retail service. 
The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Federal Power Act preserves a 
meaningful jurisdictional distinction between regulation of wholesale markets and regulation of 
retail service. In EPSA, the Court upheld the Commission’s demand response rules because they 
“address[] only transactions occurring on the wholesale market” and were directed at “enhancing 
the wholesale, not retail, electricity market.”9 By contrast, IRAS conditions wholesale treatment 
on customer-specific retail characteristics, raising the question whether the proposed tariff 
revisions remain targeted at wholesale market regulation or instead reach into matters 
traditionally reserved to the states. Likewise, Hughes and Oneok caution that regulators must

the other.10 While the Court has recognized that Federal and state jurisdiction are not 
“hermetically sealed from each other,”11 overlap is permissible only where the regulation

Simply stated, because curtailment of retail customers is not a practice affecting 
wholesale rates, the Commission is likely barred under this precedent from approving tariffs that 
attempt to regulate which retail customers are curtailed. Commission acceptance of PJM’s 
proposal would thus introduce unnecessary legal risk at precisely the wrong moment given the 
resource adequacy challenges facing the region. To the extent that the Commission supports the 
policy objectives underlying IRAS, those instead could be addressed in a more legally durable 
manner by calling on the PJM states to make the necessary reforms to retail tariffs.12 PJM could 
then work to recognize those retail arrangements in its own tariffs.

C. 
IRAS UNDULY PREFERENCES CAPACITY RESOURCES OVER 
INVESTMENTS THAT REDUCE THE UNDERLYING CAPACITY NEED  
CEBA supports PJM’s objective of ensuring that significant new load growth does not 
exacerbate existing resource adequacy challenges. But a framework intended to encourage large 
customers to support solutions should not unnecessarily dictate the form those solutions must 
take. Customers should be able to address the incremental resource adequacy needs associated 
with their growth through the most efficient available means—whether by supporting new 
generation, reducing the underlying capacity requirement through qualifying demand-side 
investments, or making efficient use of existing interconnection capability. 
Under IRAS, a New Large Load can seek to reduce its curtailment exposure by securing 
new Capacity Resources that qualify as Bring Your Own New Capacity (BYONC) or through an 
allocation of capacity procured through the RBP. But PJM provides no comparable credit for 
investments that directly reduce the amount of capacity the system needs. 
That distinction is difficult to justify within PJM’s existing framework. PJM’s rules 
expressly recognize qualifying Peak Shaving Adjustments as reductions to forecast peak demand 
and, therefore, to the capacity requirement PJM must satisfy.13 Indeed, PJM recognizes this 
resource adequacy benefit in its RBP proposal, where qualifying Peak Shaving Adjustments 
reduce the capacity need to be addressed through the centralized procurement.14 Both 
incremental Capacity Resources and incremental qualifying Peak Shaving Adjustments can

D. 
PJM APPROPRIATELY RECOGNIZES INCREMENTAL CAPACITY 
UNLOCKED THROUGH SURPLUS INTERCONNECTION SERVICE, 
BUT FURTHER REFORMS ARE NEEDED TO MAKE THIS PATHWAY 
COMMERCIALLY VIABLE 
CEBA supports PJM’s recognition that capacity enabled through Surplus Interconnection 
Service (SIS) may qualify as BYONC.19 SIS has the potential to facilitate incremental capacity

additions, particularly storage, quickly and cost-effectively by allowing new resources to make 
more efficient use of existing interconnection capability and, in so doing, avoid the need for 
network upgrades. In practice, however, existing PJM rules can limit the ability of SIS to fulfill 
that potential. Among other things, these rules prevent new surplus resources from participating 
independently as a co-located Capacity Resource, despite this being the most commercially 
viable pathway for many developers and their offtakers to add a new resource behind an existing 
point of interconnection. The only available alternative—pursuing a hybrid configuration—is 
unworkable in most cases due to the complexities of unwinding existing ownership, financing, 
and offtake arrangements.20 
These barriers are particularly relevant here because PJM is expressly relying on SIS as 
one pathway through which New Large Loads can secure new capacity necessary to qualify for 
BYONC. One relatively straightforward rule change would be to allow an existing resource with 
underutilized Capacity Interconnection Rights (CIRs) to allocate a portion of those rights to a 
new resource interconnecting through SIS. Doing so would maximize opportunities to repurpose 
underutilized interconnection capability to add incremental capacity resources in the near-term, 
all while preserving appropriate reliability, operational, and market-power safeguards.

III. 
CONCLUSION 
For the foregoing reasons, CEBA respectfully requests that the Commission reject PJM’s 
IRAS proposal as unjust, unreasonable, and unduly preferential. CEBA members stand ready to 
make the long-term commitments necessary to support investment in new capacity in the PJM 
region. But those investments require a regulatory framework that provides investor certainty, 
reduces legal risk, and preserves the ability of large load customers to procure new capacity 
through arrangements that best align with their commercial needs. If the Commission 
nevertheless accepts PJM’s proposal, it should: (1) condition acceptance on the modifications 
described above, including revisions necessary to ensure that customer-sponsored Peak Shaving 
Adjustments receive appropriate recognition within the IRAS framework; and (2) encourage
