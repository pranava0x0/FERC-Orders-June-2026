I. 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
The NYTOs submit this filing under section 205 of the FPA and their reserved filing rights 
under section 3.10(a) of the NYISO-TO Agreement7 to address the concerns the Commission 
identified in the Large Load Show Cause Order that fall within the NYTOs’ responsibilities.  This 
filing complements, and is submitted in parallel with, the NYISO’s section 205 filing establishing 
new Large Load Integration Procedures in Attachment LL of the OATT.  The substantial majority 
of the proposed tariff revisions submitted herewith are amendments to the currently effective 
NYISO OATT.  In addition, certain amendments submitted in exercise of the NYTOs’ unilateral 
filing rights propose to amend revisions to the NYISO OATT sponsored by the NYISO in its 
contemporaneous section 205 filing.8  While the NYISO’s filing establishes the study process, 
readiness requirements, and new Firm and Non-Firm Withdrawal Services for Large Loads, the 
NYTOs’ filing addresses the complementary elements necessary to recover the costs of facilities 
built to serve Large Loads, to protect other customers from cost shifts, and to integrate Large Loads 
reliably onto the NYTOs’ systems.

The Large Load Show Cause Order included preliminary findings that the NYISO’s Tariff 
appears unjust and unreasonable in five principal respects: 
1.  Transmission Service Study Process for Large Loads.  The Commission 
preliminarily found that the NYISO’s Tariff lacks sufficiently clear and consistent provisions 
addressing: (a) a definition of Large Load as a distinct category of load; (b) a mandatory 
application process, readiness requirements, and study procedures for Eligible Customers seeking 
transmission service on behalf of Large Loads; (c) study timelines of no more than 60-90 calendar 
days; (d) ongoing operational requirements – including telemetry, data reporting, remote 
disconnect capability, ramp rate and ride-through requirements, and protection systems – for 
transmission customers serving Large Loads; (e) evaluation of alternative transmission 
technologies as potential solutions in lieu of or in addition to traditional Network Upgrades; and 
(f) pro forma provisions in transmission service agreements to memorialize operational 
requirements.14 
2.  Cost Transparency and Cost Recovery.  The Commission preliminarily found that 
the NYISO Tariff lacks: (a) robust, centralized, publicly accessible data on its website regarding 
the Network Upgrades and associated costs needed to provide transmission service to Eligible 
Customers on behalf of Large Loads; and (b) a pro forma CRA between the NYISO, the relevant 
Transmission Owner, and the Eligible Customer taking transmission service on behalf of a Large 
Load, with a minimum financial contribution and credit support requirement sufficient to mitigate 
the risk of cost shifting among transmission customers.15 
3.  Co-Location Arrangements and Behind-the-Meter Generation.  The Commission 
preliminarily found that the NYISO’s Tariff lacks: (a) a definition of co-located load; (b) 
sufficiently clear and consistent provisions governing the rates, terms, and conditions of service 
applicable to interconnection customers serving co-located load, including required ancillary 
service charges (regulation and black start service on a gross demand basis); and (c) provisions 
addressing netting of behind-the-meter generation from gross load for purposes of calculating 
applicable Transmission Service Charges in a manner consistent with the cost causation 
principle.16 
4.  New Transmission Services for Eligible Load and Flexible Large Loads.  The 
Commission preliminarily found that the NYISO’s Tariff lacks transmission services that reflect 
the operational capabilities of Eligible Customers taking transmission service on behalf of Eligible 
Load (co-located load and load with behind-the-meter generation) and flexible Large Loads that 
are willing and able to limit their energy withdrawals from the transmission system under certain 
conditions, specifically:  (a) an interim Non-Firm network transmission service available while

The NYTOs present in this filing pro forma Large Load Facilities Study, Security, Cost 
Recovery, and Interconnection Agreements.  While they relate to the NYISO Large Load 
Integration Process as proposed in new Attachment LL of the NYISO OATT, they are not 
appropriate agreements for intermediaries between the Large Loads themselves and the NYTOs 
responsible for interconnection studies, cost recovery, associated security and interconnection, 
including certain operational reliability requirements.  In each of these contexts, the NYTOs 
have dealt directly with the Large Load Customers themselves, who are the financially 
responsible parties, and propose to continue this practice.  
 
Historically, in New York, energy service companies (“ESCOs”) have provided 
commodity supply unbundled from retail delivery services and have been Eligible Customers for 
purposes of the NYISO’s energy and capacity market settlements.  ESCOs have been Eligible 
Customers under the NYISO Tariffs for limited purposes, enabling retail access programs 
intended to introduce competition for supply, but not for purposes of retail customer 
interconnection studies and associated agreements to effect retail interconnections with grid 
owners.  
 
ESCOs or other intermediaries have not functioned as the primary interfaces or 
contracting parties in the interconnection process for new Large Loads.  This is not by accident; 
unlike serving as a supply source to enable retail access, ESCOs would serve no legitimate 
business function as an intermediary in the Large Load Interconnection Process.  In fact, their 
insertion would only serve to frustrate that process, making it riskier and, not insignificantly, 
costlier.  
Until recently, the interconnection process for loads was subject to NYPSC regulation 
and not FERC regulation.  The interconnection of retail loads has been to facilitate local 
distribution of electric energy which the Commission found to be involved in every retail 
transmission transaction33 and to facilitate retail sales of electric energy.  These functions are 
subject to State jurisdiction, and the NYPSC regulates such functions.

Accelerating depreciation of the assets to correspond to their repayment term, however, 
would raise accounting and rate-making issues that should be avoided.  For example, accelerated 
depreciation may conflict with how depreciation rates are established pursuant to the FERC chart 
of accounts.  Further, certain utility software that marks asset classes for a variety of purposes may 
not be able to implement separate project-specific depreciation rates.  Accordingly, rather than 
depreciating the upgrade over 15 years, the risk of cost shifts to other customers and avoids the 
pitfalls of accelerated depreciation.  The Large Load Upgrade Charge under the CRA compresses 
cost recovery into the CRA term but does not accelerate depreciation on the Transmission Owner’s 
books.  Rather, for accounting purposes, the Transmission Owner would depreciate each upgrade 
over its full economic life on a composite straight-line basis, as usual.  The Large Load, however, 
pays the full cost within the 15-year term rather than over the decades the equipment will be in 
service.  This substantially mitigates the risk of cost shifts to other customers and avoids the pitfalls 
of accelerated depreciation. 
 
As a result, the Large Load’s yearly payments during the term are higher than the 
equipment’s normal yearly straight-line depreciation cost.  The Transmission Owner will defer in 
its accounts that excess (the “Annual Deferred Revenue”).  Starting in Year 16 and continuing for 
the rest of the upgrade’s economic life, the Transmission Owner will credit the deferred amounts 
back to its cost of service in its bundled T&D rates, or, if unbundled, in its transmission rates, 
thereby protecting other customers from upgrade costs. 
 
The annual revenue requirement for each upgrade is calculated as if the upgrade were 
recovered over its Average Service Life, using the Transmission Owner’s approved depreciation 
rates, and the Transmission Owner earns its return on the depreciated, not the original, cost of the 
upgrade.  That is, the investment balance used in the annual calculation is the original cost reduced 
by accumulated depreciation and amortization.  As the equipment depreciates each year, the 
remaining balance on which the Transmission Owner earns a return reduces in amount, so the 
return portion of the charge declines over the term, while the depreciation portion stays level under 
straight-line depreciation.  That annual revenue requirement is then multiplied by the Average 
Service Life and divided by the 15-year contract term to produce the compressed charge.

The Transmission Owner calculates the charge in four steps: 
 
1. Start with upgrade cost.  The Transmission Owner uses estimated costs at first.  
After construction, it substitutes actual, prudently incurred costs and corrects all prior 
bills, with interest, back to the start of payments. 
 
2. Apply the carrying charge.  The annual costs associated with the Large Load 
Upgrade shall consist of two components:  (1) the Large Load Upgrade-specific 
return of and return on investment, including associated income taxes; and (2) an

2. How the Carrying Charge Is Adjusted Each Year 
 
The carrying charge percentage (the “Annual On-going Charge Factor”) changes from 
year to year.  It equals the Transmission Owner’s total yearly transmission and distribution costs, 
excluding depreciation, amortization, return, and associated income taxes, divided by total gross 
plant in service.  Those costs are municipal and payroll taxes, operations and maintenance, and 
administrative and general expenses, minus rents received.  Because these system-wide figures 
change, the percentage changes too. 
 
Annual update.  No later than August 1 of each year, the Transmission Owner 
recalculates the carrying charge percentage using the most recent rate data from NYPSC sources:  
the Transmission Owner’s NYPSC rate order, its annual and compliance reports to the NYPSC, 
and its books and records, with FERC Form No. 1 data used only if an input cannot be derived 
from those NYPSC sources.  All inputs must cover the same reporting period and be measured 
the same way.  The update calculation reflects changes in the Transmission Owner’s approved 
return on equity, capital structure, and depreciation rates.  The Transmission Owner then resets 
the yearly charge, the Annual Deferred Revenue, and the monthly billing schedule for the 
coming year.  The NYTOs propose using NYPSC data because the NYPSC establishes rates for 
all retail customer classes in utility-specific, comprehensive rate cases which review 
transmission, distribution, common costs and overheads, cost allocation and rate design on a 
regular basis.

bills are estimates of actual charges.  The next year, the Transmission Owner compares what it 
billed with what it would have billed using actual data for that period.  The Transmission Owner

can be accommodated under economic security constrained dispatch.  There also may be instances 
of bulk system reliability concerns in which the NYISO, including at the request of the CTO or 
ATO, may reduce the dispatched withdrawals of a Large Load in whole or in part.  For instance, a 
Large Load that takes Interim Non-Firm Service prior to completion of bulk upgrades may be 
dispatched by the NYISO to withdraw only a portion of its requested withdrawal due to conditions 
on the bulk system despite having requested firm retail service under the utility’s retail service 
tariff.  For example, if a Large Load requests firm service, and the CTO can provide firm service, 
but the NYISO requires Non-Firm Service pending new capacity additions because the Large Load 
fails a Resource Adequacy Screen, then the CTO may still be providing Firm Service on its system 
and treating the Large Load as a firm load so far as its system is concerned.  The NYISO would 
have the ability to interrupt the load if the NYISO system was approaching resource deficiency, 
but this has nothing to do with the CTO’s ability to provide Firm Service and the treatment of the 
Large Load for purposes of providing service. 
As another example, if a CTO can provide Firm Service to a Large Load, but an ATO 
requires an upgrade before Firm Service is available, then the CTO is still going to provide Firm 
Service on its system.  The pendency of the Firm Service awaiting another Transmission Owner’s 
non-local upgrade may be completely unrelated to the manner in which the retail utility provides 
service and plans its bulk or local system to be able to provide reliable service prospectively.  
Consequently, the reason that Non-Firm Service is required is relevant to which Transmission 
Owners are providing Non-Firm Service.  It is not a one-size-fits-all dynamic.

Interconnection Agreement to govern the interconnection of Large Load Projects following the 
Facilities Study because it implicates the costs the NYTOs are entitled to recover pursuant to their 
reserved rights under the NYISO-TO Agreement and directly affects retail access.  The pro forma 
Interconnection Agreement is a two-party agreement between the Transmission Owner and the 
Large Load Customer, which must be the same legal entity that executes the pro forma CRA and 
the separate pro forma Large Load Security Agreement, and it operates together with those 
agreements as Related Agreements. 
Among other things, the pro forma Interconnection Agreement:  (i) conditions construction 
and interconnection activities on completion of the System Impact Study and Facilities Study and 
other required engineering, design and special studies, execution of the CRA, the Large Load 
Security Agreement, any related construction or cost reimbursement agreements, and any required 
transmission service agreement, receipt of required deposits and advance payments, receipt of all 
required regulatory approvals, and completion of the initial operating documents; (ii) provides load 
interconnection service at the Point of Interconnection up to a Maximum Load Facility Peak 
Demand that may not exceed the amount studied in the System Impact Study and Facilities Study, 
and requires additional study and Transmission Owner approval before any Material Modification; 
(iii) requires the Large Load Customer to provide a monthly Load Ramp Projection and to update 
it every five years within limits, and sets out mechanisms to reduce interconnection rights where 
the load fails to meet a Minimum Committed Load and to address Exceedance Events in which 
the load exceeds its studied load limits; (iv) identifies the Attachment Facilities and Network 
Upgrades determined in the Facilities Study and addresses their engineering, procurement, and 
construction; (v) provides that the Large Load Customer’s responsibility for, and the recovery of, 
the costs of the Network Upgrades and the Transmission Owner Attachment Facilities will be 
governed exclusively by the CRA, and that the posting and maintenance of security will be 
governed exclusively by the Large Load Security Agreement; (vi) establishes conditions to 
energization and Permission to Operate, together with operational, metering, telemetry, insurance 
and cyber-security requirements and the Transmission Owner’s rights to interrupt or disconnect 
service for safety, reliability, and emergencies; (vii) establishes an initial term of fifteen (15) years

Customer triggers the Net Present Value Residual Charge under the CRA; and (viii) makes clear
