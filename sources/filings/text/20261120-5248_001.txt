COMMENTS OF OLABANJI JOHN OGUNYEMI
Olabanji John Ogunyemi submits these comments on the November 16, 2026 filing by 
Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc. (MISO) in Docket No. ER27-526-000, titled 
"Large Load Review, Cost Recovery and Transmission Services Filing" (Filing). The Filing 
proposes Tariff provisions responding to the Commission's June 18, 2026 order finding that 
MISO's Tariff appears to lack provisions on large loads, co-located loads and generation serving 
electrically proximate large load. Midcontinent Indep. Sys. Operator, Inc., 195 FERC ¶ 61,212 
(2026) (Show Cause Order); see Midcontinent Indep. Sys. Operator, Inc., 196 FERC ¶ 61,217, at 
PP 5, 9 (2026). Because the Filing responds to the Show Cause Order, these comments are 
submitted in both dockets.
My comments address one practical question: whether the proposed provisions will leave 
solar and storage generation in the MISO footprint, both new projects and those already operating, 
with terms that lenders and investors can underwrite. In several respects the Filing moves in that 
direction, and I offer suggestions on where clarity would help.
I.
INTEREST OF THE COMMENTER
I am a project finance professional based in Saint Paul, Minnesota. I work on the financing 
and asset management of solar projects in Minnesota, within the MISO footprint, and in New 
England. My work includes sizing construction and term debt, preparing the documentation 
lenders and tax credit buyers require before they commit capital, and preparing the reporting 
lenders rely on once projects are operating. I submit these comments in my personal capacity. They

II.
SUMMARY
Large loads in the MISO footprint will be served in part by new solar and battery storage, 
which can be built faster than other resources. Developers planned to add 43.4 GW of utility-scale 
solar and 24 GW of battery storage nationally in 2026, out of 86 GW of planned additions.[1] That 
generation is built only if it can be financed. The Filing's defined review periods and its expedited 
study for loads paired with nearby generation are steps toward financeable terms. I respectfully ask 
the Commission to (i) recognize the value of those defined timelines, (ii) ensure the paired-
generation study is open to, and workable for, financed solar and storage, (iii) confirm that a paired 
generator's interconnection service does not depend on the load remaining in place, and (iv) protect 
the interconnection service of existing generators.
III.
COMMENTS
A.
The Filing's defined review periods are valuable, because federal law now fixes a 
completion deadline for solar.
The Filing establishes the Large Load Project Review as the core study process, run in three 
study cycles each year with application deadlines posted at least 90 days in advance, and with 
stated periods for each step, including a fifteen-Business-Day review of applications and a defined 
cure period.[2] It also integrates the Large Load Addition Resource Study (LARS), proposed 
separately in Docket No. ER27-25-000, under which very large loads and the generation paired 
with them are studied on a coordinated timeline.[3] Defined periods of this kind matter to capital 
providers. Under Public Law 119-21, the clean electricity investment and production credits are 
not available for wind and solar facilities placed in service after December 31, 2027, where 
construction begins after July 4, 2026.[4] A solar project financed against the credit cannot absorb 
an open-ended study. The Commission has recognized the value of a comparable expedited

process in Southwest Power Pool's tariff.[5] I support the defined periods in the Filing and 
encourage the Commission to preserve them.
B.
The paired-generation study should be workable for financed solar and storage.
As described in the Filing, LARS is available to large loads of 200 MW or more that are 
formally paired with the generation that will serve them, with a commercial agreement of at least 
five years and a pairing of at least fifteen years.[3] Generation serving load at the same substation 
may instead use MISO's proposed zero injection interconnection agreement, filed in Docket No. 
ER26-3552-000, while MISO has said that generation serving electrically proximate load would be 
addressed through further work.[6] Three points would help financing:
•
Loads between 50 MW and 200 MW. MISO lowered the LARS threshold from 250 MW 
to 200 MW in response to stakeholder feedback.[7] Even so, a load of 50 MW to 200 MW 
that is served by nearby generation not at the same substation appears to have no 
coordinated path for studying the load and its generation together. I encourage the 
Commission to ask MISO whether a lower threshold, or a scaled coordinated process for 
smaller pairs, is feasible, since solar and storage projects sized to serve loads in that range 
would otherwise lack a coordinated path.
•
The generator's interconnection rights over the pairing period. Term debt for a solar or 
storage project typically runs longer than five years, and lenders treat the project's 
interconnection service as part of their collateral. The Tariff should state clearly what 
happens to a paired generator's interconnection service if the load is delayed, curtailed, 
defaults or exits before the fifteen-year period ends, so that the generator's lenders can 
assess that risk at financial close.
•
Storage as paired generation. I support the exclusion of load used exclusively for 
charging an Electric Storage Resource from the definition of Large Load,[8] and I note that 
the zero injection proposal allows an Electric Storage Resource to participate.[9] I 
encourage the Commission to confirm that battery storage, alone or with solar, may also 
serve as the paired resource in LARS.

C.
Cost certainty for the large load supports the financing of the generation that serves 
it.
The Filing proposes pro forma cost recovery agreements under which the large load 
customer provides security and makes revenue commitments for Large Load Network Upgrades, 
together with a Large Load Transparency Dashboard reporting the location and estimated costs of 
large loads.[10] I support this structure, which protects other customers from cost shifts. It also 
matters to generators: where a generator sells output to a large load, the generator's lenders look 
closely at the load customer's obligations. Cost estimates and true-up mechanisms that are 
predictable, and bounded where possible, make the load a more creditworthy counterparty and the 
generation serving it easier to finance.
D.
The interconnection service of existing generators should be protected.
This point is drawn from the operating side of my work. An operating solar project's debt is 
secured on the project's revenue, and that revenue depends on the interconnection service the 
project holds. I urge the Commission to confirm that the provisions the Filing adds, including 
deferral of service, interim service and the curtailment terms applicable to large loads, do not 
reduce the interconnection service of, or shift new upgrade costs onto, existing generators that are 
not party to a large load arrangement. Uncertainty on this point would affect the financing of 
generation that is already serving the grid.
IV.
CONCLUSION
I respectfully ask the Commission, in acting on the Filing and in the show cause 
proceeding, to preserve the Filing's defined review periods; to ensure the paired-generation study is 
open to, and workable for, financed solar and storage, including clear treatment of the generator's 
interconnection service over the pairing period; to support predictable cost terms for large loads; 
and to protect the interconnection service of existing generators.
