Persistence Analytics Group is a New Jersey-based infrastructure risk analytics firm focused on demand
durability, grid stress, load integrity, public-cost exposure, and decision-grade evidence for long-duration
infrastructure and energy decisions.
These comments are offered as general policy input. They do not address the merits of any specific
pending contested proceeding.
I. The Governance Question Is Also an Evidence Question
The agenda for Panel 1 asks how PJM governance mechanics may impede PJM's ability to address
operational and market needs, and what concrete, actionable solutions could better equip PJM's Board
and PJM to tackle immediate and future challenges.
That question is timely. PJM is facing simultaneous pressure from large-load growth, AI and data-center
demand, interconnection delays, capacity-market stress, state-policy conflict, transmission constraints,
storage deployment, resource adequacy concerns, affordability pressure, and public-cost exposure.
PJM governance reform should not be viewed only as a question of voting mechanics, sector
representation, filing rights, or document architecture. Those issues matter. But the larger governance
question is whether PJM can act quickly and credibly when operational reality changes faster than the
stakeholder process.
A faster process alone will not be sufficient if the process continues to rely on weak or incomplete
assumptions. Governance reform should also improve the evidence standard that informs PJM
planning, market design, interconnection, cost allocation, and reliability decisions.
Projected demand is not the same as durable demand.
II. Large Loads Require Better Classification Before Assumptions Harden
As large loads scale across the PJM region, including data centers, AI campuses, hydrogen
electrolyzers, heavy-duty charging depots, and industrial electrification projects, PJM's governance
structure should help distinguish among different categories of load before those assumptions become
embedded in planning, market, reliability, or cost-allocation outcomes.
• announced load;

• speculative load;
• contracted load;
• energized load;
• durable load;
• flexible load;
• controllable load; and
• verified operating load.
Those categories should not be treated as interchangeable. A large load that is announced but not
financed, contracted but not staged, energized but not flexible, or forecast but not durable can create
very different planning, market, reliability, and cost-allocation implications.
PJM governance should support a stronger evidence standard around the following questions:
• Is the load real?
• Is the load durable?
• Is the load staged?
• Is the load flexible or interruptible?
• Is the load controllable under grid stress?
• Is the power deliverable?
• What upgrades are actually caused by the load?
• Who pays if the operating profile changes?
• How are existing customers protected if the forecast fails?
• What evidence is sufficient before assumptions harden into planning, market, or cost-allocation
outcomes?
Without a stronger evidence standard, the region risks treating projected growth as if it were verified
system need. That can distort planning, increase public-cost exposure, weaken ratepayer protection,
and create stranded infrastructure risk if load forecasts, project timelines, financing conditions, or
operating behavior change.
PJM governance reform should therefore include an explicit focus on large-load validation and verified
grid integration.
III. Concrete, Actionable Reform Areas
The Commission asked for concrete, actionable solutions. Persistence Analytics Group / United Grid
respectfully suggests that PJM governance reform should consider mechanisms that strengthen both
process responsiveness and evidence quality.
1. Time-bound governance pathways. Establish a time-bound governance pathway for urgent
reliability, large-load, capacity, interconnection, and cost-allocation issues.
2. Standardized large-load evidence classification. Require a standardized framework
distinguishing announced, contracted, energized, durable, flexible, controllable, and verified

3. Cost-causation evidence. Require stronger cost-causation evidence before major infrastructure
costs are assigned to existing customers or socialized across broader zones.
4. Assumption sensitivity review. Require large-load-related planning assumptions to include
sensitivity analysis for delay, cancellation, utilization shortfall, operational flexibility, and demand
non-persistence.
5. State, utility, and stakeholder coordination. Improve coordination among PJM, states, utilities,
large-load customers, and public stakeholders before load assumptions are embedded into market
and planning outcomes.
6. Governance performance review. Periodically assess whether PJM's stakeholder process can act
fast enough when load, capacity, interconnection, or reliability conditions change materially.
7. Verified grid-integration review. Consider a verified grid-integration review process for large loads
that may materially affect reliability, capacity pricing, transmission needs, state policy, or public-cost
exposure.
IV. Dependable Demand Is Now a Reliability Issue
Reliability is no longer only about dependable supply. It is also about dependable demand.
PJM governance must be capable of verifying both sides of the system: generation performance and
load behavior. This is especially important as large-load development, storage deployment, capacity
market outcomes, and public affordability concerns converge across the region.
The next phase of grid governance should be built around a practical standard:
• Verify the load.
• Verify the power.
• Verify cost causation.
• Verify flexibility.
• Verify public benefit.
• Protect the grid and the public.
PJM does not simply need more process. It needs governance capable of supporting verified execution.
V. Conclusion
Persistence Analytics Group LLC / United Grid respectfully encourages the Commission to consider
PJM governance reforms that improve not only speed and accountability, but also the quality of
evidence used to make planning, market, reliability, interconnection, and cost-allocation decisions.
The region's operational and market challenges are increasingly tied to the durability, flexibility, and
deliverability of large-load assumptions. PJM governance reform should help the region distinguish
projected demand from durable demand before costs, reliability obligations, and public exposure harden
around assumptions that may not persist.
