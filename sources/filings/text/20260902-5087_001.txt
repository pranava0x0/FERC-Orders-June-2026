I. INTRODUCTION
Persistence Analytics Group LLC ("PAG") respectfully submits these limited supplemental comments in
Docket No. ER26-3380-000.
PAG previously filed a Motion to Intervene and Comments on August 17, 2026 addressing the need to verify
the assumptions underlying PJM’s proposed Reliability Backstop Procurement ("RBP") before long-duration
procurement and cost obligations are allowed to harden around modeled need.
These supplemental comments address one narrower issue that has been sharpened by subsequent filings:
whether the original determination of cost causation should remain fixed over a long-duration RBP
obligation when the underlying facts materially change.
The Attorney General of the Commonwealth of Kentucky has now raised concern that Load Serving Entities
that prudently planned for their own resource adequacy needs could nevertheless bear costs caused by
others, including where the responsible entity defaults. The Attorney General further notes that such costs
could continue over the 15-year term of the agreement. 
PAG agrees that this issue warrants particular attention, but approaches it from a distinct evidentiary
perspective.
The relevant question is not only who is identified as the cost causer at inception.
It is:
What evidence supports that cost-causation conclusion, what conditions must remain true for it to
continue to hold, and what material change should require the conclusion to be revalidated?

II. INITIAL COST CAUSATION SHOULD NOT BE TREATED AS
PERMANENT COST CAUSATION
A cost-causation determination may be reasonable when made and still become stale if the underlying
large-load, project, contractual, or operational facts later change.
Examples may include material changes in:
expected large-load magnitude;
project timing or energization date;
construction status;
customer or developer commitment;
bilateral supply arrangements;
responsible entity status;
default or credit conditions;
resource adequacy contribution;
withdrawal, downsizing, or cancellation of the underlying project; or
other conditions that materially alter the basis on which the original RBP need or cost allocation was
established.
Where one or more of those conditions materially changes, continued reliance on the original cost-causer
determination may no longer be decision-grade.
Accordingly, PAG recommends the following principle:
Initial causation is not necessarily permanent causation.
A long-duration cost obligation should not outlive the evidence supporting the original cost-causation
conclusion.

III. MATERIAL CHANGES SHOULD TRIGGER COST-CAUSATION
REVALIDATION
PAG respectfully recommends that PJM’s implementation framework identify specific material-change
events that require the cost-causation determination to be revisited before continued allocation or
reassignment of RBP-related costs.
The purpose of revalidation would not be to reopen every settled matter continuously.
Rather, it would ensure that where a material factual premise changes, the record is updated before a long-
duration financial obligation continues to rely on an assumption that may no longer remain valid.
A revalidation process should be sufficient to answer, at a minimum:
what fact or condition originally established the cost-causation relationship;
whether that fact or condition still exists;
whether another entity now owns the relevant obligation or downside;
whether the underlying project still contributes to the need the RBP was designed to address;
whether any residual or default-related cost remains attributable to the original cost causer; and
whether assigning such cost to another Load Serving Entity would reflect actual causation or merely
administrative residual allocation.
That distinction is important.
Residual allocation is not automatically equivalent to demonstrated causation.
IV. DEFAULT RISK SHOULD NOT AUTOMATICALLY TRANSFER COSTS
WITHOUT A FRESH EVIDENTIARY DETERMINATION
The Kentucky Attorney General has specifically raised concern regarding the potential for prudent Load
Serving Entities to bear costs following default by the responsible entity.
That circumstance makes revalidation especially important.
A default may change the identity of the party that can pay an obligation, but it does not by itself establish
that another Load Serving Entity caused the underlying need or should bear the resulting cost.

Before residual costs migrate to another party, the implementation record should support a fresh
determination of:
the remaining obligation;
the continuing causal relationship;
the responsible entity;
the basis for any reassignment;
and the extent to which the reassigned party receives a corresponding reliability benefit.
PAG therefore recommends that default-related cost transfer not occur solely as an administrative
consequence of nonpayment where the evidentiary basis for causation has materially changed or become
incomplete.
V. LONG-DURATION OBLIGATIONS REQUIRE LONG-DURATION
EVIDENTIARY DISCIPLINE
The RBP contemplates obligations that may extend for many years.
The longer the obligation, the greater the likelihood that material conditions will change.
That does not mean the original determination was wrong.
It means the original determination may require revalidation.
The appropriate principle is:
The duration of the financial obligation should not exceed the durability of the evidence supporting
the causation conclusion without a defined mechanism for revalidation.
This approach would improve ratepayer protection without displacing PJM’s operational authority or the
jurisdiction of state regulators.
It would simply require the evidentiary basis for long-duration cost allocation to remain aligned with the
facts on which that allocation depends.
VI. CONCLUSION
PAG respectfully recommends that the Commission ensure, through PJM’s tariff, implementation guidance,
manuals, or other appropriate mechanism, that:
the initial RBP cost-causation determination is supported by an identifiable evidentiary basis;
