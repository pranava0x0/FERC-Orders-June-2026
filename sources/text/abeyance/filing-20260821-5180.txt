Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or the “Commission”),1 Sierra Club and Natural Resources

upgrades, which will enable the RBP to better achieve its intended objectives.

II.
PJM’s Reliability Backstop Proposal should be accepted with modifications to better
achieve the RBP’s objectives.

PIOs generally support PJM’s Reliability Backstop Procurement proposal but urge the
Commission to exercise its authority to modify the proposal to better accomplish PJM’s
objectives to: (1) procure the correct amount of capacity to meet the RBP target; (2) procure new
capacity; (3) procure that new capacity on time; and (4) ensure that large loads that require new
capacity bear the costs of building and connecting that capacity. Specifically, the Commission
should accept PJM’s proposal, but only on the conditions that PJM either require RBP supply to
be located near to large loads, which will help ensure that new supply can come online timely,
and that reliability risks or costs associated with delivering new RBP capacity to large loads are
not shifted to other ratepayers, or require RBP resources to cover 100% of network upgrades.
Additionally, the Commission should encourage states to mandate take-or-pay agreements
between LSEs and large loads to mitigate the risks of cost-shifting if a data center fails to
materialize as expected.

A.
Objective One: Procure the Correct Amount of Capacity

The RBP’s quantity of procured megawatts is set by administrative decision, not by
market clearing. In the RPM auctions, the amount of capacity procured emerges from an
administratively established demand curve interacting with competitive offers. Because the RPM
is annual, the amount of capacity procured is revised annually and therefore responds to changes
in the market. The RBP has no comparable self-correction. PJM sets the target, reduces it for
verified Qualifying RBP Offsets and opt-outs, and procures the final number under fifteen-year
commitments.48 Any over- or under-procurement is therefore locked in for fifteen years. Under-
procurement of new capacity leaves a reliability gap the RBP is designed to fix, which leaves
customers exposed to increase loss-of-load risk. Over-procurement would mean that the
participating zones and LSEs are stuck with fifteen years of charges for capacity they don’t need.
PJM describes “the primary purpose of the RBP Target” as determining “how much UCAP MW
is needed to meet the reliability requirement shortfall without over-procuring,” and it built the
matching rules to “avoid[] over-procurement, under-procurement, and double-counting.”49
In evaluating the RBP and any modifications proposed in this protest and by other parties, the
Commission should ensure that the RBP procure the right amount of needed capacity. A

B.
Objective Two: Procure New Capacity

The second objective recognizes that the shortfall was due to a lack of steel in the ground
supply rather than a mispricing of existing megawatts of supply. The need for genuinely new
supply is recognized by the PJM Board, the Governors’ Principles, and the Ratepayer Protection
Pledge. The RBP, therefore, “is designed to attract new supplies of capacity (i.e., new ICAP), not
merely incremental resource efficiencies, to address the current capacity shortfall and a provide
stable, long-term source of capacity.”50 If, for example, the RBP merely allowed existing
resources to move from the RPM to the RBP, the entire process would be an exercise in wealth
transfers with no reliability benefits. Genuinely additive procurement, by contrast, protects
existing customers from paying for reliability problems they did not cause while also providing
new load with long-term sources of generation.
The Commission should therefore make additionality a core touchstone as it evaluates the
RBP Proposal and any proposed modification. The Commission should ask: does the resource
add installed capacity that the system did not have before? A modification that admits
demonstrably additive resources enlarges the pool of new supply and advances the objective
while increasing competition, improving speed to power, and lowering costs. A modification that
would relax the screen against reshuffled or paper capacity is contrary to the objective and
therefore should be dismissed by the Commission.

C.
Objective Three: Procure New Capacity on Time

The third objective recognizes that the need for new capacity is based on an existing
shortfall, not a mere trend or forecast. The 2027/2028 and 2028/2029 auctions have already
cleared short, and capacity is valuable for reliability only when it is installed. Supply arriving in
2035 does nothing for loss-of-load risk in 2029. The RBP Proposal recognizes this urgency in its
selection criteria. The RBP only allows projects that can demonstrate a feasible commercial
operation date of June 1, 2032, or earlier.51 Once it collects all eligible projects, PJM then selects
offers “in rank order from earliest to latest offered Delivery Year,” and only then by levelized
cost. This means that PJM is willing to pay a higher price for capacity that arrives sooner.52
Timeliness of projects is also important because PJM proposes to reset the trigger for the
Tariff’s pre-existing Reliability Backstop Auction so that no such auction can occur unless BRAs
clear short for three consecutive Delivery Years beginning with 2032/2033. PJM justifies this by
assuming that “all RBP Resources should have reached their respective commercial operational
dates” by that delivery year.53 The Commission should, therefore, recognize that significant
reliability risks hang on the ability of the RBP to actually procure new supply in the next few
years.
The Commission should therefore treat speed to power as a key objective when
evaluating the RBP Proposal and any proposed modifications. Where the Commission finds that
the RBP will not actually bring new supply online quickly, it should require changes. Where a
modification would improve speed to power, the Commission should consider adopting such

D.
Objective Four: Ensure That the Large Loads That Require New Capacity
Bear Its Costs.

PJM and its stakeholders recognize that new large loads, especially data centers, are
uniquely responsible for the failure of recent auctions to clear at desired reserve margins. This
failure to meet reliability standards puts everyone in the PJM footprint in danger of increased
loss-of-load events. The RBP is designed to shore up that shortfall through new supply. Because
these new costs are so clearly and uniquely caused by the massive expansion of data centers and
similar new large loads, cost causation principles require that the RBP allocate costs to those new
large loads. This is not a controversial position. The nation’s largest data center developers
pledged to pay the “full cost” of the new resources their demand requires.54 The thirteen PJM
governors pledged to direct these costs to “new data center loads that have not otherwise
procured capacity or agreed to be curtailable.”55 PJM promises that RBP costs “will not become
stranded costs, which would ultimately be socialized.”56 It is therefore crucial that the RBP fulfill
its objective of ensuring that new large loads actually pay for the new supply they need.
The Commission should apply this objective when evaluating the RBP and any proposed
modifications. Commission and PJM jurisdiction over ratemaking, however, extends only so far.
States set retail rates, which are actually charged to individual ratepayers—be they residential
customers or data centers—for capacity charges assigned to LSEs by PJM. It is therefore

The Commission should modify the RBP by requiring RBP supply to be located new
large loads in order to minimize delays and the risk of cost-shifting.

To ensure that PJM accomplishes its objectives to procure the (1) correct amount of (2)
new capacity (3) on time to meet the 2028/2029 shortfall and (4) allocate the costs of that
capacity to new data center load, PJM must include a locational deliverability requirement for
RBP Sell Offers. Prioritizing RBP resources that are closer to zones with large load growth will
ensure that RBP resources are not bogged down with large network upgrades that can cause
delays or high costs that could be passed onto other customers through the interconnection queue
or RTEP. Failing to include a locational requirement in the RBP imperils the RBP’s ability to
achieve its objectives in several ways. First, the absence of a locational requirement increases the
likelihood that high network upgrade costs may prevent RBP resources from showing up at all,
failing the objectives to procure (1) the correct amount of (2) new capacity. Similarly, because
the absence of a locational requirement makes it more likely that RBP resources may require
significant network upgrades, it also increases the risk of network upgrade delays, failing the
objective to bring resources online quickly to meet the 2028/2029 shortfall. Furthermore, the

A.

could lead to reliability risks.

PJM’s decision to exclude a locational requirement in the RBP will increase risks of non-
delivery or delayed delivery if generation resources procured through the RBP are outside of
zones with growing large load demand. As proposed, the RBP relies on the assumption that PJM
will be able to build network upgrades quickly enough to get RBP resources online in the next
three to six years.61 This assumption is overly ambitious at best and unrealistic at worst. Recent
analysis shows that most new power plants faced network upgrade timelines of at least three
years after those projects had already cleared the queue.62 As of April 2026, about half of the
projects in TC2 were delayed by baseline upgrades, with average construction timelines of about
5 years.63 Projects just entering the queue or without a signed interconnection agreement will
take much longer. Resources that require larger network upgrades, like resources that are farther
away from load, will likely take longer to interconnect due to network upgrade construction
delays.

network upgrade costs and long construction timelines affected several projects in PJM’s
Reliability Resource Initiative (“RRI”)—an interconnection fast-track that aimed to procure
purportedly “shovel-ready” projects that could come online quickly to meet PJM’s near-term
capacity needs. However, the RRI procured large projects that resulted in the need for large,

for large loads that are currently causing a shortfall in PJM’s capacity market and to facilitate the
allocation of costs to large loads while respecting state jurisdiction over retail rates. However,
PJM’s failure to include any requirement as to the location of RBP supply resources will increase
the RBP’s reliance on large, costly network upgrades that threaten to delay when supply can
become deliverable to the large loads that need it. The modification described above to require
RBP supply to either be located near to demand, or to be fully responsible for the cost of network
