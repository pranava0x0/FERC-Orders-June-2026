A. MISO’s Answer Does Not Cure Concerns with ZGIA’s Operational Requirements.
In their initial protest, Community Organizations and Clean Energy Advocates explained
that MISO’s proposal fails to actually limit ZG resources to zero injections due to imprecise and
permissive operational standards without adequate penalties and other incentives.9 MISO’s
Answer attempts to defend this design, noting that its proposal “imposes specific operational
requirements” to enforce ZGIS’s zero-injection limitation, including the required installation of
communications and monitoring equipment by the ZG resource enabling it to monitor demand of
the ZG Associated Load, as well as equipment to enable the Generating facility to reduce its
injections within the Transient Tolerance Window.10 Community Organizations and Clean
Energy Advocates do recognize that MISO’s proposal includes several measures facially
designed to ensure that resources with ZGIAs comply with the operating requirements.11 But as
Mr. Moaveni’s testimony further explains, these proposed measures leave several gaps.12
By way of analogy, MISO’s requirements amount to requiring a car to have a
speedometer, without imposing appropriate speed limits on the road, using radar detection for
independent identification of speeding, or posting clear penalties to de-incentivize speeding.

1. MISO’s Reliance on the Limited Nature of ZGIS Does Not Justify Socialization
of Upgrades to Address Cumulative Grid Impacts.
As Community Organizations and Clean Energy Advocates explained in Protest, the

through MTEP, where costs are broadly socialized.66 MISO does not contest the differences

limits inherent to ZG service – namely peak load limits and the requirement to be at the same

justify socialization of costs that may nevertheless arise to cover upgrades to cover cumulative
impacts on the  grid resulting from the addition of ZGIA resources.

MISO’s assertion that the defining limits of ZG resources will prevent grid strain from
these resources does not withstand scrutiny. As Mr. Moaveni explains, “a generating facility
affects the transmission system in ways that do not depend on whether it is exporting real
power.”69 For example, “[a]n energized generator can contribute fault current to nearby buses
even when it is not exporting real power.”70 This matters because the technology and grid
functions that would address such issues – breaker interrupting capability and protection
coordination margins at nearby substations – are “a finite and shared capacity on which pending
DPP projects also rely.”71 As a result, the double allocation of access to such resources between
ZGIA and DPP resources is not resolved by injection, substation or voltage requirements. And
this is just one example of potential cumulative impacts. As Mr. Moaveni’s testimony further
explains, merely “[p]reserving transfer capability therefore does not establish that ZG resources
omitted from a DPP model, or DPP projects omitted from a ZGIA model, have no short-circuit,
protection, voltage, or dynamic interaction with one another.”72
The Commission should reject the filing as deficient because both MISO’s filing and
MISO’s Answer fail to either refute such cumulative impacts or justify their resolution in MTEP,
which socializes costs to load more broadly.

3. MISO Cannot Justify Exempting ZGIS from FERC’s Emphasis on
Transparency to Prevent Large Load-Related Cost Shifts.
Community Organizations and Clean Energy Advocates’ Protest explained that MISO’s

Q. How does the proposed contractual remedy, without additional measures, function to
ensure compliance with the operating requirements?
A. The proposed contractual remedy functions only as a last-resort backstop. It offers no
consequence short of termination, and nothing that operates on the timescale of an injection event.
The Filing relies on the pro forma generator interconnection agreement’s general default and
termination framework rather than on a remedy designed to deter or correct injections by ZG
resources. To see why that matters, it helps to distinguish the ways in which a ZG resource can
inject power beyond its interconnecting substation.
MISO refers generally to “inadvertent” or “unintended” injections, but those terms cover three
different situations: ordinary tracking error, contingency-driven transient export, and control or
protection failure. In an ordinary tracking error, generation briefly exceeds contemporaneous load
by a small margin during normal operation. In a contingency-driven transient export, the associated
load trips or drops sharply and the generating facility has not yet reduced its output. In a control
or protection failure, the injection-limiting scheme does not arrest the export within the permitted
window. These situations differ in likelihood and consequence, as well as in how they are detected
and in the appropriate response. MISO’s proposed Transient Tolerance Window addresses
contingency-driven transient export, and the proposed offer administration process addresses, at
most, part of ordinary tracking error. No ZGIA-specific provision directly addresses the third
situation, in which the facility continues exporting beyond the permitted tolerance because the
mechanism intended to limit that export did not perform as expected.
The situation in which exports continue beyond the tolerance window because a control
mechanism fails is the situation in which a remedy matters most, for three reasons. First, it involves

--- PAGE 36 ---

11
the largest injection, because absent a working scheme the facility’s full output can flow onto the
Transmission System rather than the small margin of a tracking error. Second, it is not necessarily
self-limiting; a transient export ends when the controls act, while a control failure can persist
beyond the permitted window until the controls recover or corrective action is taken. Third, it is
the situation the studies did not evaluate, since the ZG Impact Assessment models a loss of load
followed by a reduction within the Transient Tolerance Window, not a sustained injection beyond
it.
Yet the Filing does not establish a ZG-specific monitoring, testing, or remedial framework for
control failures. The ZGIA provisions contain no automatic or graduated curtailment, suspension,
or financial consequence tied to an unauthorized injection that would give ZG customers a
continuing incentive to maintain and correct their injection-limiting controls. The only contractual
backstop is the agreement’s general breach-and-cure framework, which operates on a thirty- to
ninety-day timescale suited to contractual defaults and, if the breach is not cured, leads to
termination. Nor does Article 9.7.4.7(c) fill that gap. Its obligation to cease injection is triggered
by the trip of a transmission facility in the delivery path; it is an operating requirement that
responds to a system event, not a remedy for non-compliance.
Termination, standing alone, is not a workable remedy for operating non-compliance. A ZG
resource is by design dedicated to serving its associated load, and the Filing requires the associated
arrangement to state an expected term of at least five years. Terminating the interconnection
agreement would eliminate the generator’s interconnection rights and leave the associated load’s
full demand dependent on network service. Without intermediate measures, termination provides
no means of correcting an isolated or remediable control failure, and its consequences fall on the
associated load as well as on the party whose equipment failed to perform. The injection-limiting

--- PAGE 37 ---

12
scheme must act whenever the load’s withdrawal falls, not only when the load trips entirely, and
termination removes the load’s dedicated supply for the remainder of the term, long after the event
that triggered it.
Termination can also create an immediate planning and operating challenge. Because the
associated load remains firm network load, termination requires its full demand to be supplied
from the Transmission System; where facilities have been deferred in reliance on the ZG resource
and are not yet in service, MISO may need interim operating measures or accelerated construction,
as discussed in Section IV. Termination may well be the appropriate response to persistent or
repeated failure, but the Filing offers nothing before that point. Intermediate remedies should be
available and applied proportionately to an isolated protection-scheme misoperation, repeated non-
compliance, and persistent failure. Those intermediate measures could include a ZGIA-specific
cure period during which operating restrictions apply, mandatory load-rejection retesting after an
injection event, temporary suspension pending correction, financial consequences for
unauthorized injections, and escalation for repeated events, with termination reserved for the end
of that sequence.

event; if it is treated as a Breach that is not capable of being cured, Article 17 provides that the
non-breaching Party “shall terminate” the agreement.16 Either way, Article 17 does not impose a

IV.  Modeling Assumptions and Cost Allocation
Q. How could MISO’s proposal affect responsibility for transmission costs?
A. Two features of MISO’s proposal bear on who bears transmission costs and when. The first is
reliance on a ZG resource as a substitute for transmission. This does not necessarily change which
customers pay for load-serving upgrades, but it changes when those upgrades are identified and
leaves responsibility for them unresolved if the resource is lost. The second is the set of study
assumptions used to approve ZG resources, which can cause interconnection-related issues to be
addressed under planning cost-allocation rules rather than generator-interconnection cost-
allocation rules. I address each in turn.
Q. What cost-responsibility issue arises when MISO relies on a ZG resource as a substitute
for transmission?
A. MISO attributes much of the value of a ZGIA to transmission planning. MISO’s witness states
that only resources with an accepted interconnection agreement are eligible for inclusion in MISO
Transmission Expansion Plan (“MTEP”) studies. MISO identifies this as a principal source of the
ZGIA’s value. According to MISO’s witness, the ZGIA process will provide an agreement within
ninety days, which permits the resource to be modeled as an input to the subsequent MTEP or
Expedited Project Review load study for its associated load, creating “the potential for reduced

total cost of transmission network upgrades needed to integrate the new load.”38 Attachment FF
permits the resource to serve as a Non-Transmission Alternative that offsets upgrades that would
otherwise be required to serve the load. Transmission upgrades that would otherwise be identified
for the associated load may therefore be deferred or avoided in the planning case that includes the
ZG resource.
Now consider what happens if the ZG resource fails to materialize, retires early, or becomes
permanently unavailable. The associated load is firm network load taking Network Integration
Transmission Service on a gross basis, so a transmission need that had been reduced, deferred, or
avoided in reliance on the ZG resource may re-emerge and be identified in a later MTEP cycle.
Any resulting transmission solution would be classified and allocated under the applicable MTEP
cost-allocation rules. The Filing does not assign that cost to the ZG interconnection customer,
establishes no ZGIA-specific true-up, and does not trace later planning costs back to the
arrangement on which the earlier planning assumption depended.

The ERAS experience is relevant here as well, although the analogy is only partial. The ERAS and

nearby substations. The cumulative effects I am describing include short-circuit duty, protection
coordination, voltage performance, and dynamic behavior. Ultimately, a zero-export restriction
does not eliminate those effects, although their magnitude depends on technology, operating
condition, controls, and system topology. However, the absence of a cap or sunset means the
potential cumulative exposure is less bounded under ZGIA. ERAS was capped and time-limited,
and ZGIA is neither.

V.  Eligibility and Limitations on the ZGIA Process
Q. Is the ZGIA process equally functional for all resource types?
A. MISO states that the requirements are facially neutral, fuel-agnostic, and open to any resource
regardless of technology.70 That is accurate as a matter of eligibility, but it does not follow that the
ZGIA process is equally workable in practice for all resource types. A paired generation-and-
storage configuration is eligible to apply for a ZGIA. Because a Generating Facility may consist
of “one or more generating unit(s) and/or storage device(s),” such a facility can proceed through a
single Interconnection Request, occupy one quarterly study slot, and be subject to a single service
cap under Section 3.11(v).71 MISO has also confirmed that a resource with ZG Interconnection
Service is considered deliverable to its associated load for accreditation purposes, with the
reference to “firm interconnection service” in the accreditation provisions referring to
deliverability.72
How storage charging would be treated in such a configuration, however, remains unresolved.
MISO has stated in the stakeholder process that charging by a storage resource with a ZGIA “will
follow the same process as other storage resources – storage charging is not considered during the
interconnection queue process; instead, it is studied as an obligation to secure transmission service
as a load.”73 That approach may be workable for a stand-alone storage resource, but it leaves four
questions open in a co-located configuration, where the defining constraint is the relationship
between generation output and a specific load: (1) whether energy used to charge the storage

Q. What are the likely adverse consequences of not limiting the ZGIA process beyond the
ten-project quarterly cap?
A. There are two principal consequences. First, the Filing does not examine how a growing share
of generation dedicated to specific loads affects resource adequacy for the rest of the system. A
ZG resource may not serve any load other than its associated load, and its capacity credits must be
used in a Fixed Resource Adequacy Plan or Reliability-Based Demand Curve opt-out rather than
offered into the Planning Resource Auction.79 Whether that matters depends on whether the
pathway displaces generation that would otherwise have interconnected through the standard
process with the ability to serve other load, or supports generation that would not otherwise have
been built. The Filing does not address that question or explain how MISO’s loss-of-load modeling
and planning reserve methodology account for ZG resources.80
Second, a permanent construct reduces the opportunity to evaluate actual operating experience
before the pathway becomes an enduring feature of the Tariff. This is a largely new construct, and
MISO acknowledges having accommodated zero-injection arrangements only in limited
circumstances in the past.81 A pilot with an initial term and an aggregate cap would permit the
Commission and stakeholders to observe how many resources actually use the pathway, whether
the injection limits hold in operation, and whether upgrades are later identified in transmission
planning that were not anticipated in the interconnection studies, before the service becomes a
permanent feature of the Tariff. Nothing about a sunset would deny the pathway to projects with
near-term needs; it would simply require MISO to return to the Commission with an operating
