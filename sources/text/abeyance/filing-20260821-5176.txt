I.
INTRODUCTION

As described by PJM, the Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) is a “one-time
procurement of new capacity to address resource adequacy challenges in the PJM Region arising
from insufficient commitments of capacity in the 2027/2028 and 2028/2029 Reliability Pricing
Model (‘RPM’) Auctions, primarily driven by the unprecedented addition of large loads, most
notably data centers, in the PJM Region.”2 As proposed by PJM, “the RBP will commence upon
Commission acceptance of this filing with the goal of obtaining commitments of new RBP
[Unforced Capacity (‘UCAP’)] MW from eligible resources, called RBP Resources, for a fixed

II.
BACKGROUND
PJMICC is a coalition of large energy users in the PJM Region. Coalition membership
includes many large manufacturers and other entities that require significant volumes of electricity
to support their operations—they include manufacturers of paper, cement, chemicals, metals,
pharmaceutical products, and consumer goods; institutional customers such as large hospitals and
universities; and other energy-intensive retail electric customers. PJMICC members represent the
backbone of American manufacturing and core services that support the United States’ competitive
strength in a global economy.
As a threshold matter, PJMICC wishes to express appreciation to PJM’s Board, Staff, and
stakeholders for the intensive communication and collaboration over the past many months leading
up to the RBP Proposal. As an ad hoc organization representing many traditional large energy
users in the manufacturing and institutional sectors, PJMICC emphasizes the importance of
securing stable, reliable, and cost-effective power sources during a period of rapid load growth.
While PJMICC does not oppose the concept or general approach of the RBP, PJMICC
offers concern that the RBP, as proposed, is incomplete—primarily insofar as its design leaves

III.
PJMICC COMMENTS
A.
The RBP, as proposed, is incomplete because it lacks key protections and
components to enable alignment of wholesale and retail cost allocation.
PJM rightly recognizes that meeting the moment requires “cooperative federalism” – that
is, both state-regulated and federally-regulated spheres.5 Like a large jigsaw puzzle, the picture
will only be complete when the necessary pieces are joined.
Here, PJM has proposed an approach that, if successful, will result in a substantial increase
in capacity to address the resource adequacy challenges it faces. However, procuring the capacity
is only part of the picture. The addition of this capacity is intended to be paid for by new large
loads; however, as currently proposed, PJM’s filing lacks the tools needed to ensure that retail
costs are, and will be, allocated in a just and reasonable manner at the state level.
While PJM appropriately recognizes that states oversee retail cost allocation, PJM neglects
to recognize that the structure of how the wholesale rates are charged directly and substantially
impacts the practical ability for states to assign retail costs. Put simply, while RBP seeks to fill a
resource adequacy need, the proposal should be amended to ensure it fills key “gaps.” Below,
PJMICC addresses three interrelated but critical gaps: cost allocation, regulatory accountability,
and information-sharing. Bridging these gaps is critical to ensuring both just and reasonable
wholesale rates and just and reasonable retail rates that are consistent with the Ratepayer Protection

In PJM’s urgency to respond to the pressures of the PJM Governors and other stakeholders

B.
Gap #1: Cost Allocation Alignment: Effective cooperative federalism must
position states and retail customers for success by providing appropriate
alignment between wholesale and retail costs.
PJM is correct that states are responsible for retail cost allocation. Yet meeting the moment
requires a cooperative federalism that reflects a reasonable alignment between wholesale and retail
pricing. Without appropriate alignment, states could be frustrated in their efforts to establish just
and reasonable retail rates.
Under PJM’s proposal, each Load Serving Entity’s (“LSE”) specific share of costs within
a zone is determined by its RBP Obligation. From there, PJM explains that states are responsible
for allocating these costs.6 Yet, PJM does not recognize that its proposed cost allocation has a
direct impact on the states and their ability to allocate at the retail level.7
Wholesale costs must be designed with retail allocation in mind; otherwise, simply
claiming that states can allocate costs to retail customers may prove inadequate. States will only
succeed if the rules in the wholesale market include sufficient tools to assign the costs of RBP to
the retail customers that are prompting the RBP procurement, consistent with the Ratepayer
Protection Pledge and the principles of just and reasonable rates.

This is a critical point. Just and reasonable federal/wholesale rates are connected to just
and reasonable state/retail rates. This is logically apparent because both are connected to cost
causation, which is a bedrock principle in the Federal Power Act and many state laws.8 Just as an
LSE purchasing capacity on behalf of its retail customers will be allocated costs based on its
proportionate use of capacity in each zone, a retail customer should also be allocated costs similarly
at the state level.
This connection between wholesale and retail rates can be illustrated as follows:

If a wholesale food distributor sells barrels of apples for $100 and barrels of oranges for
$500, a grocery store will not buy a barrel of each and then price apples and oranges the
same on the store shelves. Such an approach would result in very expensive apples and
extremely cheap oranges. Assuming a normal competitive environment, such an approach
would result in apples going bad on the shelf, while oranges would sell out quickly without
generating sufficient cash to re-order more oranges.


Similarly unrealistic would be a grocery store ordering a barrel of each fruit without seeing
itemized pricing to understand the 5x price difference. A grocery store that treats both fruits
as a “bundle” but does not distinguish the costs of each item will not be able to sustainably
replenish its supply, make a profit, or charge market-based rates to its customers.


Bottom line: the grocery store can only set its retail price by reference to the wholesale
price. While the grocery store has latitude to set retail prices and allocate its costs, it cannot
sustainably act in a way that is contrary to the wholesale edifice on which it depends.

Similarly, the RBP creates a carveout from the normal capacity marketplace process. If
certain (present and future) retail customers are driving the need for more supply, PJM’s RBP must
operate so that wholesale costs flow through. As a practical matter, this means that wholesale rates
must be developed with retail allocation in mind. Currently, this is facilitated by a cost allocation

C.
Gap #2: Regulatory Accountability: Assignment of costs to retail customers by
utilities must be on file before a state commission or FERC.
Currently, many utilities in the PJM region include their cost allocation methodologies in
the Attachment M-2 on file at the FERC (as an attachment to the PJM Open Access Transmission
Tariff). However, this is not a universal process. To PJMICC’s knowledge, the FERC has not
broadly required utilities to file such methodologies. The Attachment M-2s address both federal
and state jurisdictional cost allocation. For example, a typical Attachment M-2 format includes
both (1) calculation of LSEs’ respective shares of responsibility for system-wide transmission and
capacity charges and (2) how individual accounts are allocated responsibility.
Attachment M-2s stand at the border between state and federal regulatory responsibility.
While the FERC may not direct retail cost allocation, it has jurisdiction over rules and regulations
“affecting or pertaining to [jurisdictional] rates and charges”10 Utilities must be required to set

This transparency is also important because of the relative complexity of the RBP. To take
a straightforward example, the RBP Proposal states, “Capacity obtained through bilateral matching
or other voluntary arrangements for new capacity, if reported to PJM within the RBP procurement
window and supported by sufficient evidence, may be determined by PJM to be Qualifying RBP
Offset UCAP MW.”11 Then, PJM may, as appropriate, “reduce the Final RBP Target by the
Qualifying RBP Offset UCAP MW and by the UCAP obligation value of load that have
successfully requested and obtained an opt-out pursuant to proposed Tariff, Attachment DD,
section 18.1(d).” In this case, a state retail authority must be able to determine which load has met
this requirement to appropriately allocate (or not allocate) costs. There are a variety of options and
carveouts present in the RBP Proposal. These must be clear to consumers at both a wholesale and
retail level. While not mandating retail pricing, the FERC should require that methodologies be on
file with the FERC or a relevant state commission.
D.
Gap #3: Information-Sharing: Clear tagging of RBP participation and relevant
carveouts.
The wholesale costs alluded to above can only be appropriately allocated at the state level
if there is proper “information flow” from wholesale to retail. As the central coordinator of the
region’s wholesale markets, PJM alone is situated to provide the necessary tools and data to state-
regulated entities, enabling states to fulfill their respective just and reasonable mandates at the
retail level.12 Unfortunately, under the RBP Proposal, it is not clear if states will have the necessary

To appropriately equip states and state-regulated authorities for this purpose, each
substantive component of the RBP must be “tagged” so that information sharing reaches the state

have access to as they design cost allocation at the retail level.
Currently, there is a lack of clarity at the customer level regarding which loads are causing
RBP-related costs and which are not. Such information must be clear. The following practices, at
minimum, should be followed by PJM as an operational matter:

Essential data needed for forecasting and cost assignment or allocation must be accessible
to PJM, LSEs, and electric distribution companies (“EDC”).


PJM Settlement should track critical data on PJM invoices to LSEs. PJM invoices already
include a variety of zonal- and LSE-based charges. PJM should ensure that key costs for
new large retail loads impacting the RBP are tracked through a subaccount-like structure.


PJM should create a new billing line item (similar to how PJM separately reports
Transmission Enhancement, Generation Deactivation, or other itemized costs) tagged
specifically as “Reliability Backstop Procurement” costs, broken out by the load growth
that caused the procurement (see below). This approach is relevant to RBP, IRAS, and
other proposals affecting wholesale capacity and transmission costs. For example, such
billing line items should include itemized exemptions/credits for Bring Your Own New
Capacity (“BYONC”) status (including co-located load additions).


Under each billing line item related to RBP, PJM should add subaccounts identifying each
individual large load customer by interconnection point.
