I.
Introduction
As the Commission has recognized, integrated planning processes can help ensure that
generation and transmission can be arranged to supply nearby large customers reliably, at lower
cost, and with faster engineering study processes, ultimately resulting in more just and
reasonable rates without harming other existing or future customers or suppliers. Better load and
generation interconnection processes are clearly needed. MISO’s LARS proposal does not
achieve that end, nor does it improve other interconnection processes. Instead of improving the
coordination of generation and transmission planning, MISO’s LARS proposal is a new,
permanent parallel interconnection study process that will shift costs from large loads to other
existing and future transmission customers and resource owners, create and worsen reliability
concerns, and allow for unchecked and harmful anti-competitive discrimination. The
Commission should reject LARS because it is not just and reasonable and is unduly
discriminatory or, in the alternative, issue a deficiency letter requiring MISO to demonstrate how
LARS can be made just and reasonable.
II.
Background
A. Large Load Growth and Interconnection
MISO estimates that annual load growth will be 1 to 3 percent per year from 2025
through 2044.5 MISO integrates new generation and load through separate processes on different
schedules.6 New generation enters either through MISO’s cluster-based Definitive Planning
Phase (“DPP”) or its temporary, serialized Expedited Resource Addition Study (“ERAS”). The
DPP operates on a 365-day timeline and ERAS operates quarterly. New load is accounted for

C. The LARS Proposal
On October 2, 2026, MISO filed proposed revisions to Attachment X (the Generator
Interconnection Procedures, or “GIP”), Appendices 1 and 6 to Attachment X, and Attachment FF
to establish the Large Load Addition Resource Study, with a requested effective date of
December 2, 2026 and a first study period in the first quarter of 2027.21 MISO described LARS
as “expedited review of the addition of 200 MW or greater loads together with new generation

LARS creates at least two major changes to how generation interconnection and

D. The Stakeholder Process
MISO developed LARS in eleven weeks. It first presented the concept to its Large Load
Working Group (“LLWG”) on July 16, 2026.55 After presenting revisions at different meetings,
MISO presented final language on September 23 and filed on October 2.56
Stakeholders raised, and MISO declined to address, several of the concerns presented in
this protest. The Environmental Sector objected to the Transmission Customer’s gatekeeping
role, to the LRZ-wide scope of the pairing requirement, and to LARS’s failure to meet the Show
Cause Order’s electrically proximate standard, and asked that generators and loads be jointly
liable for upgrade costs if a pairing ends early. Energy Futures Group presented the Generator
and Load Interconnection Process (“GALIP”), which would study large loads and their
generation together within the DPP, with full cost-causation-based allocation of upgrade costs
and both firm and non-firm service options for load.57 MISO declined to pursue GALIP, stating
that it “does not believe in a ‘one size fits all’ approach to interconnection” and that GALIP
“aimed to shoehorn a parallel generation/load study process into the existing DPP process.”58

III.
Arguments
A. LARS creates a cost shift from large loads and their generation to other
customers
It is a long-standing principle of Commission ratemaking that “all approved rates reflect
to some degree the costs actually caused by the customer who must pay them,” and that
compliance is measured “by comparing the costs assessed against a party to the burdens imposed
or benefits drawn by that party.”59 In the Show Cause Order, the Commission preliminarily
found that MISO’s Tariff violates that principle because it “lacks adequate mechanisms to
mitigate the risk of cost shifting among transmission customers” when large loads drive network
upgrades.60 LARS does not supply those mechanisms. By MISO’s own account, LARS adopts
no cost allocation methodology, and “[t]he cost shifting issues raised in the Commission’s Show
Cause Order will be addressed in future filings.”61
Instead, LARS builds cost shifting into the process in four ways. First, it assigns the
network upgrades that a generator-load pair requires to the transmission pricing zone rather than
to the pair. Second, it studies LARS generators in models that exclude the DPP queue, and
resolves the resulting double-allocation of transmission capacity through MTEP projects paid for
by zonal load and through curtailment of DPP generators that paid for their own upgrades. Third,
it allows either half of a pair to leave the system after fifteen years and the generator to fail at any
time, leaving zonal load to pay for whatever transmission must then be built. Fourth, it defers the
cost recovery agreement, financial security, and transparency measures the Commission
identified as necessary. The Commission should reject LARS or, at a minimum, decline to make

1. LARS assigns upgrade costs to the pricing zone
As explained above, LARS sorts network upgrades identified in its load and generation
studies into three categories: violations in only the load study, violations in only the generation
study, and violations in both studies. Violations that occur only in the load study or in both the
load and generation studies are assigned the BRP or Other project types and their costs are
allocated to all loads in the Transmission Owner’s pricing zone. Only when a violation occurs in
the generation study alone will MISO assign the costs of the mitigations to the Interconnection
Customer as a GIP upgrade.62 MISO calls this a “cost allocation hierarchy—transitioning from
GIP to BRP when appropriate.”63 This hierarchy represents impermissible cost shifts.
When the costs of mitigation are assigned to BRP or Other project types, those costs are
allocated to the Transmission Owner in whose pricing zone the project is located. Those costs are
then recovered through the Transmission Owner’s rates, paid for by every transmission customer
in the zone. MISO explains that the costs will fall on “the load within the transmission pricing
zone where the transmission upgrade is required.”64 In contrast, when the same generator
interconnects through the DPP, the Interconnection Customer funds the Network Upgrades its
interconnection requires. LARS, therefore, takes a category of costs usually assigned to
generators and assigns them to existing load. It is not as though the beneficiaries are unknown or
diffuse. Rather, the beneficiaries are simply the generator and the large load, for which the
generator is the “alternative transmission solution.”65 MISO has failed to show how the LBA or

2. LARS shifts costs to interconnection customers in the DPP
LARS shifts costs to interconnection customers in the DPP, to the disadvantage of
generators in the DPP compared to those related to LARS projects. In the DPP, MISO models
the system headroom by assuming that all higher-queued generating facilities will enter
operation.78 By contrast, in LARS, MISO proposes to model only DPP projects with a signed
GIA.79 These differing approaches risk MISO allocating transmission headroom twice.80 Rather

1. MISO’s LARS proposal is unduly discriminatory
Under the FPA, a rate design must be non-discriminatory and non-preferential as well as
being just and reasonable.96 As the D.C. Circuit has observed, the Act “bristles with concern for
undue discrimination.”97 The Commission and courts have consistently found that
“[d]iscrimination is undue when there is a difference in rates or services among similarly situated
[entities] that is not justified by some legitimate factor.”98 Different treatment is only justified if
the record “evidence show[s] factual differences to justify” it.99
As Commissioner See has previously explained, when a “proposal’s goal is to get some
interconnection requests through the study process faster than others, the Commission is by
definition asked to greenlight discrimination.”100 Here, LARS sets up a generation and load study
process that will offer an unlimited number of generation projects and loads preferential access
to the transmission system. LARS generators also obtain the additional significant benefit of
access to MISO’s Planning Resource Auction (“PRA”). LARS accomplishes this in a way that is
unrelated to any technical or operational limitation or purported system need, and allows self-
interested vertically integrated utilities the ability to determine which projects can access the
process without any mechanism for Commission, or even MISO, oversight. The result is a study
process that violates the FPA’s prohibition against undue discrimination, and its longstanding

harm to other interconnection customers that could not participate in the expedited process.113 In

E. LARS threatens reliability and efficient planning
LARS would be at least the fifth separate path onto the MISO transmission system,
alongside the DPP, ERAS, the pending Zero-Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement
(“ZGIA”) process, and EPR for load. A sixth, the Large Load Project Review (“LLPR”) is
currently under development.150 MISO’s addition of another parallel interconnection process
takes the region further away from efficient transmission planning. MISO’s statement that any
constraints that are not addressed in the LARS study due to overallocation would be reconciled
in the MTEP process does not promise that these solutions would be minimal upgrades.151 It
promises only that they would be reconciled through the MTEP process. There is a risk under
MISO’s current proposal that unresolved reliability contingencies identified in a subsequent
MTEP process could require mitigation through backbone upgrades or other high-cost solutions
resulting from the cumulative impacts of LARS and other interconnection requests, the costs of
which non-LARS transmission customers would ultimately have to pay. Due to the permanent
nature of MISO’s LARS proposal, there is a risk that non-LARS transmission customers could
be permanently disadvantaged by the process and bear the costs of transmission investments
needed to resolve issues caused, at least in part, by a LARS request, or series of interconnection
requests. Each new path to interconnection takes MISO further from efficient planning.

1. Parallel independent studies produce unreliable results and reliability risks
Interconnection studies assign network upgrades on the basis of cumulative impacts. By
putting some generators in one model and other generators in another, MISO cannot capture
those impacts. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation’s (“NERC”) model
validation guidance states that future-year planning cases “should contain the same component
representations as the most recently validated model, unless there is a specific reason for the data
to be different,” and should represent “the cumulative planned changes to the system” through
the period studied.152 By excluding the DPP, LARS does the opposite of NERC’s procedures.
Neither set of studies sees the other until MTEP, which is after LLGIAs have been executed and
DPP customers have been assigned their upgrades.
The consequences of this mutual blindness of the models are not limited to the cost shifts
described above. A constraint that appears only when the two sets of projects are combined
might be a contingency for which no mitigation was identified and no upgrade was built, leaving
real-time operators without the reconfiguration options the planning process assumed. In the
worst case, cascading blackouts could result. A more moderate negative outcome might be load
shed as the only tool available to manage unstudied conditions. MISO does not appear to
describe the dispatch and case assumptions for the LARS load study. MTEP-based load studies
and DPP interconnection studies in MISO are built on different cases and different dispatch
assumptions. Without an explanation of which assumptions LARS will use, the Commission has
no basis to find that a load study built upon MISO’s existing EPR process will identify the
constraints that a DPP study of the same generator would identify.

3. Integrated planning processes are available and MISO should adopt them
There are viable alternatives to siloed transmission planning, which is inefficient and
costly. In March, the Commission accepted SPP’s Consolidated Planning Process (“CPP”),
which, in Commissioner Rosner’s description, “eliminat[es] the silos between the transmission
planning process and the generator interconnection process” by “synthesizing and streamlining
them into one recurring three-year planning cycle” that “recognizes that generation and load
share one transmission system, and plans for both of their needs holistically.”160 Commissioner
Rosner “strongly encourage[d] other transmission providers to take note of the innovative
approach the CPP promises, and to consider developing similar integrated solutions tailored to
meet the pressing needs within their own footprints.”161 Commissioner Rosner noted that SPP
expected to deliver interconnection agreements within nine to ten months of the close of its
application window, to cut study costs by millions of dollars, and to avoid more than $100
million in duplicative transmission costs each planning cycle.162 PJM, responding to its own
show cause order, has proposed to “[c]onsolidate generation and merchant transmission
interconnection along with long-term firm and large load transmission service studies into a
singular 12-month process.”163 Transmission planning becomes more efficient the more future
transmission needs can be considered together in the planning process. This is one of the reasons

Conclusion
For the foregoing reasons, the Commission should reject all portions of MISO’s LARS
proposal and direct MISO to engage with stakeholders to advance a different proposal.
Alternatively, Clean Energy Advocates and Community Organizations request that the

course of action should focus on an integration of planning processes that does not undermine
