AFFIDAVIT OF CHRISTOPHER PILONG

1.
My name is Christopher Pilong and my business address is 2750 Monroe Blvd.,
Audubon, Pennsylvania 19403.  I am the Senior Director, Operations Planning at
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).  In that role, I am responsible for functions
that support the reliable planning and preparation of PJM’s bulk electric system for
real-time operations.  My responsibilities include oversight of operational studies,
processes, tools, and coordination activities with Generation Owners, Transmission
Owners and neighboring entities, as well as gas-electric coordination with the
natural industry.
2.
I am submitting this affidavit in support of PJM’s proposed revisions to the PJM
Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) filed August 17, 2026, to comply with
the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“Commission”) directives in its
June 18, 2026 order,1 including the directive to reconsider the proposed effective
date for PJM to offer the new transmission services for Eligible Customers taking
service on behalf of Co-Located Load and load with Behind the Meter Generation.
Because PJM has to date only proposed Tariff language for Interim Network
Integration Transmission Service (“NITS”), my affidavit is limited to addressing
implementation of that service.
3.
In this affidavit, I explain:
• The operational complexities associated with implementing Interim
NITS on an automated basis and scope of changes on PJM’s systems
that will be needed to accommodate automated provision of Interim
NITS and the anticipated timing of implementing those changes.
• Why the timeline PJM proposed for Interim Resource Adequacy
Service (“IRAS”) implementation is not comparable to Interim NITS.
4.
This affidavit addresses implementation rather than the legal merits of any
particular Tariff design.  Because the Commission has not yet issued an order and
final Tariff language, implementation requirements will depend on the terms of the

5.
As explained below, PJM should not treat the implementation of Interim NITS as

of demand, there is significant value in incorporating Interim NITS into PJM’s

settlement, and customer-facing processes.

I.
OPERATIONAL DESIGN AND PROCEDURE CHANGES
6.
Once the Commission approves proposed Tariff language, PJM first would need to
convert the Commission’s requirements into objective and auditable operating
rules.  Those rules would need to define when service is available, the transmission
capability that supports it, the events that permit or require curtailment, the
sequence and priority of curtailment, restoration criteria, customer notification, and
the treatment of partial curtailments.  The rules also would need to address conflicts
among
reliability
directives, transmission service priorities, generation
commitments, and market outcomes.
7.
Existing operating agreements, manuals, desk procedures, emergency procedures,
training materials, operator displays, alarm protocols, and shift turnover practices
would require review and, in many cases, revision.  PJM would need clear decision
rights for declaring a qualifying condition, approving a curtailment, communicating
the instruction, monitoring compliance, and restoring service.  Procedures must
work during normal operations, constrained operations, emergencies, restoration,
and periods when communications or applications are degraded.
8.
Interim NITS presents a number of implementation challenges.  Network service
ordinarily is embedded in network modeling, designation of network resources and
load, transmission planning assumptions, scheduling rights, market participation,
and long-term reliability obligations.  An “interim” status therefore must be defined
consistently across time horizons and must not inadvertently create rights,
priorities, or planning assumptions beyond those authorized by the Commission.
9.
Implementing this construct for Interim NITS would require exception
management.  Examples include late or inaccurate data, a customer’s failure to
curtail, a change in topology after the applicable study, overlapping transmission
reservations, generator or transmission outages, load forecast error, restoration
following a contingency, and a service period that crosses operating days or
settlement intervals.  Some manual workarounds may exist, but they are not
preferable or durable substitutes for controlled and auditable production processes.

II.
COORDINATION WITH TRANSMISSION OWNERS, GENERATION
OWNERS, AND CUSTOMERS
10.
Implementation of Interim NITS would require substantial coordination with PJM
Transmission Owners. Transmission Owners would need to understand and support
the service definitions, study assumptions, facility ratings, topology inputs, local
operating procedures, switching implications, notification paths, and field
execution responsibilities.  Where a curtailment is intended to relieve a local or
regional constraint, PJM and the affected Transmission Owner must agree on the
relevant monitored facilities, contingencies, expected distribution factors,
validation method, and restoration criteria.
11.
Transmission Owner systems and procedures may require corresponding changes.
These may include energy management system displays, outage coordination
practices, control-center instructions, operator training, customer communications,
and evidence retention.  Differences among Transmission Owner footprints, legacy
systems, and local procedures will require testing by affected zone rather than
reliance on a single generic implementation.
12.
Coordination with Generation Owners and other market participants also is
necessary because new service states can alter commitment, dispatch, reserve
procurement, congestion management, and outage assumptions. PJM would need
to explain how a curtailment instruction interacts with generation dispatch
instructions, must-offer or availability obligations, bilateral arrangements, and any
generation or storage serving the affected load.
13.
Customers receiving the service would need defined telemetry, metering,
communications, scheduling, and response obligations.  PJM would need validated
points of contact available at all times, test protocols, escalation paths, and
consequences for non-performance.  Meter-data latency and granularity must be
sufficient to determine whether the requested relief occurred and when it occurred.
14.
A further implementation uncertainty is the identity and operational role of the new
“Eligible Customer” that would take service on behalf of the underlying load.
Depending on the final tariff construct, that entity may not be the load-serving
entity, end-use customer, Transmission Owner, or market participant with which
PJM currently exchanges operating instructions.  PJM therefore would need to
verify the entity’s legal and operational authority to act for the load; define its
responsibilities relative to those other parties; establish secure, continuously staffed
primary and backup communication channels; identify authorized contacts and
escalation paths; and develop protocols for issuing, acknowledging, executing, and
documenting curtailment and restoration instructions.  PJM also would need to
determine required telemetry, metering, scheduling, outage, cybersecurity, identity-
management, and data-sharing arrangements; execute any necessary agreements or
registrations; conduct communications and failover testing; train the parties; and
confirm that an instruction to the Eligible Customer can be translated into timely,
measurable action at the affected load.  If the identity, authority, technical

IV.
MARKET CLEARING, RELIABILITY AND SETTLEMENT IMPACTS
18.
Market clearing requires a consistent representation of which load is firm, which
load may be curtailed, when curtailment is available, and whether the curtailment
is a pre-contingency control, a post-contingency action, or an emergency measure.

--- PAGE 15 ---
5
That representation can affect constraint formulation, load distribution,
commitment, dispatch, reserve requirements, congestion, losses, prices, and
interchange feasibility.
19.
If the market engines assume curtailment that operators cannot execute with
sufficient certainty or speed, the resulting solution could understate reliability
needs.  Conversely, excluding available curtailment from all market and reliability
analyses could produce inefficient commitments or congestion outcomes.  PJM
therefore would need validated rules for when the service may be modeled, the
confidence assigned to it, and any conservative operating margin required.
20.
PJM also would need to determine whether and how curtailment affects locational
marginal prices, make-whole payments, uplift, transmission charges, congestion
rights, reserve or emergency compensation, deviation charges, and other settlement
outcomes.  The treatment must be internally consistent and understandable to
market participants.  PJM would need to update its settlement calculation and
reporting systems to account for proposed differences between the settlements
treatment for NITS and Interim NITS services.  The systems Electric Distribution
Companies (“EDCs”) and Load Serving Entities (“LSEs”) use to submit revenue
quality metered load data will also need to be updated to allow differentiation of
the metered load served under NITS service from the load served under Interim
NITS service given the differing settlements treatment. Further, EDCs and LSEs
will need to develop their own procedures to track and report the load of end-use
customers taking the new transmission service to PJM distinctly from those using
regular NITS service.
21.
Reliability studies would need to evaluate contingencies in which the curtailable
load does not respond, responds only partially, or is unavailable because of
communications, equipment, or customer conditions.  Operators would need
alternative actions for such failures.  The design also should avoid dependence on
a single point of failure and should define how service is suspended during tool
outages or uncertain system conditions.
22.
Implementation may require revisions to model-development schedules, case-
building processes, seasonal and near-term studies, operator drills, and reliability
assessments.  The interaction with generator deactivations, new resource
interconnections, load additions, transmission upgrades, and temporary operating
guides must be evaluated so that interim service does not obscure an emerging
reliability need.

VI.
DIFFERENCES FROM IRAS IMPLEMENTATION
27.
Constellation Energy Generation, LLC (“Constellation”) incorrectly characterizes
Interim NITS implementation as comparable to the implementation of the IRAS
framework.  The two initiatives differ materially in scope, operational complexity,
required market system modifications, transmission service administration impacts,
and reliability implementation requirements.

28.
While IRAS primarily establishes a planning and accreditation framework that
relies upon existing market and operational constructs, Interim NITS requires PJM
to develop entirely new processes for administering, tracking, limiting, and
curtailing transmission service associated with individual large load customers.
Accordingly, the implementation timelines are not analogous.

29.
Interim NITS requires PJM to manage transmission service at an Eligible Customer
and transmission service request level, as opposed to zonal level.  PJM’s current
operating and emergency procedures are primarily designed around zonal load
forecasting, zonal load management, and system-wide reliability actions.  Under
existing arrangements PJM forecasts load at the transmission zone level.  In
addition, capacity obligations are assigned at the zone and LSE level.  Emergency
load management actions are generally executed through zonal or broader
reliability directives.  Market systems clear generation against aggregate load
forecasts rather than individual transmission customers.

30.
Interim NITS introduces an entirely different construct requiring PJM to identify,
monitor, and potentially curtail transmission service attributable to specific large
loads.  Unlike a zonal curtailment approach, customer-specific transmission service
curtailments require various actions directed at the individual loads being served
by Eligible Customers.  This will require PJM to have the capability to identify the
individual affected loads in the operations systems and track the transmission
service rights associated with those loads.  PJM will then need to have the capability
to determine the customer-specific curtailment quantities, which will require
further development of priority and allocation methodologies.  PJM will also have
to coordinate this implementation with the Transmission Owners.  PJM will also
need to have protocols to validate customer-specific exception conditions and take
customer-specific actions in real time.  These capabilities do not exist today within
PJM’s operating platform and must be newly developed.

31.
Implementation of Interim NITS requires significant modifications to several
existing systems and business processes used to administer transmission service.
Among other changes, PJM must develop capabilities to register and maintain
Eligible Customers’ individual large loads, associate those loads with transmission
service reservations, and track load-specific transmission service limits.  PJM will

--- PAGE 18 ---
8
also need to establish curtailment eligibility criteria, create notification and
communication protocols, and integrate transmission service information across
planning, operations, and settlements systems.  PJM will also need to develop
auditable compliance records for transmission service administration.

32.
These requirements represent a substantial departure from PJM’s current
transmission service framework, which was not designed to administer
transmission service limitations on individual end-use customer loads.  By contrast,
IRAS largely leverages existing planning, capacity market, and resource
accreditation processes.

33.
PJM will have to modify the market-clearing engines to implement Interim NITS
on an automatic basis.  Constellation understates the degree to which Interim NITS
affects PJM's market systems.  To implement Interim NITS, PJM must evaluate
modifications to both Day-Ahead and Real-Time market operations.

34.
Day-Ahead Market Impacts.  For implementation of Interim NITS in the Day-
Ahead Market, PJM must make significant modifications to the current system.
Moreover, to do so, PJM must determine a range of implementation details
including how Interim NITS will be represented in the Day-Ahead load forecast,
whether curtailment limitations affect demand bid treatment, and how transmission
service restrictions impact commitment decisions.  PJM will also need to determine
how reliability commitments should account for potentially interruptible
transmission service.  The Day-Ahead market engine currently assumes load
forecasts consistent with existing transmission service obligations.  Introducing
customer-specific transmission service constraints may require system design
changes and extensive testing to ensure appropriate unit commitment outcomes.

35.
Real-Time Market Impacts.  The Real-Time implementation of Interim NITS
presents additional complexity.  To implement Interim NITS in Real-Time, PJM
must determine various parameters for including Interim NITS in the Real-Time
Market.  For example, PJM must determine when customer-specific transmission
service limitations become binding and how transmission service curtailments
interact with dispatch and reserve commitments.  PJM must also establish how
operators will recognize and model curtailed load and how real-time telemetry and
operational visibility requirements will be satisfied.  PJM must also determine how
reliability actions are coordinated with transmission owners and affected
customers.  Ultimately, the interaction between customer-specific transmission
service restrictions and real-time security-constrained economic dispatch in
implementing Interim NITS requires detailed design, testing, and validation to
avoid unintended reliability consequences.

36.
Reliability Risks.  The reliability and operational risks associated with Interim
NITS differ materially from those of IRAS.  IRAS primarily affects resource
accreditation and resource adequacy planning.  Interim NITS directly affects real-
time operational execution.  Specifically, Interim NITS requires PJM to establish

--- PAGE 19 ---
9
customer-specific curtailment methodologies and reliability-based criteria to
trigger those curtailment methodologies.  Once those methodologies and criteria
are established, PJM also must establish operator procedures and communication
protocols for implementation, along with compliance and audit processes.
Moreover, PJM must establish processes for coordination with the transmission
owners and load-serving entities.  As part of this effort, PJM will need to ensure
appropriate dispute resolution mechanisms for transmission service administration
because any errors in these processes could result in improper curtailment of
transmission customers.  Because Interim NITS directly impacts operational
reliability actions, PJM must ensure comprehensive testing and validation prior to
implementation.

37.
Software Development and Testing.  The software development needs and testing
requirements to implement Interim NITS are also significantly greater than IRAS.
Implementation of Interim NITS requires coordinated modifications across
multiple platforms, including the transmission service systems, load forecasting
systems, and customer registration systems.  PJM will also have to modify its
market clearing applications, operator tools, settlements systems, reporting and
compliance systems, and the data interfaces with transmission owners.  Each of
these modifications requires requirements development, software design, coding,
integrated system testing, market participant testing, operator training, and
production deployment.

38.
Unlike IRAS, which predominantly leverages established planning and capacity
market constructs, Interim NITS introduces entirely new operational functionality
that must function correctly during emergency grid conditions. Consequently,
extensive development and testing are necessary to avoid reliability risks.  Any
incremental benefits associated with implementing Interim NITS do not justify
accelerating implementation at the expense of reliability.

39.
PJM agrees that large load growth presents emerging reliability challenges.
However, implementation speed cannot come at the expense of operational
accuracy and reliability.  The Commission has consistently recognized that RTOs
must ensure that major operational, market, and transmission administration
changes are fully designed, tested, and validated before implementation.  The
complexity associated with customer-specific transmission service management
warrants a deliberate implementation schedule that ensures reliable, transparent,
and non-discriminatory administration.

40.
Accordingly, the implementation timeline proposed by PJM reflects the substantial
operational, software, market, and transmission service changes necessary to
implement Interim NITS successfully and cannot reasonably be equated to the
implementation requirements associated with IRAS.

VII.
CONCLUSION
41.
In my opinion, implementing Interim NITS safely and reliably requires coordinated
changes across PJM operations, Transmission Owners, Generation Owners,
customers, market systems, transmission-service systems, settlements, training,
and compliance processes.  The work must proceed from a stable service definition
and must include integrated testing under normal, constrained, emergency, and
degraded-tool conditions.
42.
A Commission-authorized transition period of approximately 18 to 24 months
would provide a reasonable implementation window under the assumptions stated
above.  That period is necessary to reduce the risk of inconsistent service treatment,
non-executable market assumptions, unreliable curtailment, settlement errors, and
inadequate operator or stakeholder readiness.
