Dear Chairman Swett:

The Natural Gas Supply Association (NGSA) appreciates the opportunity to comment on the
Informational Reports directed by the Commission’s June 18, 2026, orders in the above-
captioned dockets.

As industry participants who produce, ship, and deliver U.S. natural gas, NGSA members view
these reports as vital tools for tracking resource adequacy across regional operators. However, to
ensure a complete picture of grid reliability, the Commission should direct regional transmission
organizations (RTOs) and independent system operators (ISOs) to supplement these reports with
an assessment of whether existing natural gas infrastructure and fuel supply arrangements are
sufficient to support anticipated growth in electricity demand.

I.
Natural Gas is Critical to Supporting Large Load Growth

Because of its scalability, reliability, and rapid dispatchability, natural gas will remain a
significant fuel source to support much of this new electrical demand. As noted in an article by
Data Center Dynamics, natural gas is uniquely suited to meet the immediate needs of data

centers because it is the most dispatchable fuel source available, can run at capacity factors
exceeding 80 percent, and can be ramped up within minutes to meet demand. 1

In fact, there has been a dramatic uptick in new gas generation in the interconnection queues
across the country. For example, the amount of natural gas generation in PJM’s interconnection
queue has increased by a staggering 1,458% (surging from approximately 6,800 MW to 106,000
MW, where it now represents 48% of the total queue volume) over the past two years. Similarly,
there has been an incredible shift in MISO, where gas requests have spiked over the last 24
months to now dominate the fast-track Expedited Resource Addition (ERAS) queue, capturing
75% of the new expedited capacity requests. The need for new natural gas infrastructure built in
tandem to support this magnitude of growth in gas generation cannot be ignored.

The United States is uniquely positioned to meet this demand as the largest net producer of dry
natural gas, supported by a vast pipeline network spanning more than three million miles. The
upstream natural gas system possesses ample productive capacity to supply this growing market.
NGSA member companies are already actively partnering with data center developers and power
generation entities to create tailored commercial solutions underpinned by natural gas services to
support this evolving market.

II.
Infrastructure Constraints Threaten Reliability Efforts

While much attention has been focused on ensuring adequate power generation to meet rapid
demand from data centers and other large loads, and rightly so, we are concerned that the
magnitude of new pipeline capacity to support that growing load may not be fully appreciated.
Despite the robust gas supply, regional energy security is increasingly limited by the delivery
infrastructure "straw"—rather than the wellhead. Multiple interstate pipeline mainlines currently
run near maximum capacity during peak periods.

If left unaddressed, localized infrastructure shortfalls will directly limit a region's ability to
develop and dispatch new gas generation when and where it is needed most, particularly during
extreme winter weather. Furthermore, the interdependence between the gas and electric sectors
continues to grow as upstream and midstream operations become increasingly electrified,
requiring more transmission. Failing to account for the full energy ecosystem—including
necessary pipeline infrastructure—limits the regional operators’ ability to achieve full success in
these critical resource adequacy initiatives.  Simply put, generation cannot be separated from fuel
assurance.

III.
Conclusion and Specific Request for Supplemental Reporting

NGSA does not ask the Commission or RTOs to reprioritize their current, complex workloads
regarding large load integration. Rather, we urge the Commission to ensure that fuel availability
is explicitly recognized as a core component of the total grid reliability equation.

To that end, we ask the Commission to issue a directive requiring regional operators to
supplement their informational filings with a dedicated section detailing:

1. How they assess whether regional natural gas infrastructure can support the expected
growth in natural gas for power generation.
2. What regional initiatives or stakeholder processes are underway to address any identified
natural gas infrastructure shortfalls.
3. Their efforts to support generator fuel adequacy, such as ensuring power market reforms
that encourage strong natural gas contracting practices.

At a minimum, such information should be mandated in any updates or future Informational
Reports required by the Commission.
