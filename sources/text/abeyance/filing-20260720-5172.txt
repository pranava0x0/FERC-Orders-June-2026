II.
The Burden Under FPA Section 206 Has Not Been Met: The Existing Rate Has
Not Been Shown To Be Unjust And Unreasonable.

The FPA requires that the rates and charges of a public utility “in connection with the
transmission or sale of electric energy subject to the jurisdiction of the Commission, and all rules
and regulations affecting or pertaining to such rates or charges shall be just and reasonable.”9
“[A]ny such rate or charge that is not just and reasonable is . . . unlawful.”10  Under FPA section
206, the Commission may, by its own motion or upon complaint from a third party, remedy any
“rate [or] charge” or “any rule, regulation, practice, or contract affecting such rate [or] charge”
found to be “unjust [or] unreasonable.”11  “The burden of proving an unjust or unreasonable rate
rests with the party that initiated the proceeding—that is, the Commission or the third-party
complainant.”12  In other words, a respondent to a FPA section 206 complaint is not required to
make an affirmative showing that its existing rate is still just and reasonable.
For the reasons below, neither FERC nor any other party have met the burden of
demonstrating that PJM’s current rate is unjust and unreasonable so as to require new forms of
transmission service for flexible large load or other large load customers.
A. There is No Evidence In The Record That Flexible Large Loads or Other
Large Loads Are Unable to Obtain Reliable, Timely, And Reasonably Priced
Service Using The Existing Network Service Construct.  Therefore, the
Commission Fails To Satisfy The Requirement Under The APA That Its
Findings Be Supported With Substantial Evidence.

can “potentially defer or reduce the need for Network Upgrades, among other benefits.”16

The states, not the Commission, have jurisdiction to decide when and in what
circumstances retail load will be curtailed, and how utilities should charge and treat retail
customers in those circumstances.  The Commission has jurisdiction over “the transmission of
electric energy in interstate commerce and to the sale of electric energy at wholesale in interstate
commerce,” and over “all facilities for such transmission or sale of electric energy.”62  Except as
otherwise provided in the FPA, the states retain jurisdiction over “any other sale of electric energy”
and “over facilities used for the generation of electric energy or over facilities used in local
distribution or only for the transmission of electric energy in intrastate commerce, or over facilities
for the transmission of electric energy consumed wholly by the transmitter.”63
States decide how the retail obligation to serve should be met.  For example, states might
require the provision of backup service to retail customers who lose supply from their contracted
supplier, would regulate the distribution system costs associated with such service that would be
charged to the retail customer, and would regulate the costs and terms of the backup service.
Rather than honoring the states’ freedom and flexibility to exercise their jurisdiction over such
questions, the Commission’s new rule appears to create a new federal construct that defines the
extent and terms of service in such situations.  That is, the Commission’s new rules leave it entirely
up to the customer to specify a contract demand service level that it will purportedly not exceed,
even if state law mandates that the local utility provide the customer with backup service in the
event of an outage.  The Commission’s conclusion that the customer will not take additional
service lies in direct conflict with the states’ backup service mandates.  The Commission does not
have authority under the FPA to dictate the terms of retail service in that fashion.

Unlike wholesale demand response programs, which “allow[] any state regulatory body to
prohibit consumers in its retail market from taking part in wholesale demand response programs,”
thereby “not requir[ing] any “‘action[ ] that would violate State laws or regulations,’” the
Commission’s new forms of transmission services provide no such limitation.64  As the Supreme
Court has explained, however, “if indirect or tangential impacts on wholesale electricity rates
sufficed, FERC could regulate now in one industry, now in another, changing a vast array of rules
and practices to implement its vision of reasonableness and justice,” a result that the Court could
not “imagine . . . was what Congress had in mind.”65  That is the problem here with FERC’s
proposal to extend the new transmission services that it devised in the PJM Co-Located Load
Proceeding to flexible large load.
As the Supreme Court explained, the FPA
“limit[s] FERC’s sale jurisdiction to that at wholesale,” reserving
regulatory authority over retail sales (as well as intrastate wholesale
sales) to the States.  FERC cannot take an action transgressing that
limit no matter how direct, or dramatic, its impact on wholesale
rates.  Suppose, to take a far-fetched example, that the Commission
issued a regulation compelling every consumer to buy a certain
amount of electricity on the retail market.  Such a rule would
necessarily determine the load purchased on the wholesale market
too, and thus would alter wholesale prices.  But even given that
ineluctable consequence, the regulation would exceed FERC’s
authority, as defined in § 824(b), because it specifies terms of sale
at retail—which is a job for the States alone.66

V.
Conclusion
Because neither the Commission nor any other complainant have satisfied their burden
under FPA section 206 to show that PJM’s existing Tariff is unjust and unreasonable with respect
to flexible large load (or any other type of load) and the Commission has not engaged in reasoned
decision making in the Show Cause Order the Commission should grant rehearing and revoke its
preliminary finding in the Show Cause Order that the new transmission service offerings that it
devised in the PJM Co-Located Load Proceeding should be extended to flexible large loads, or
any other large loads.
