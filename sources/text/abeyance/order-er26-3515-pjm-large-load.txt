1.
On August 13, 2026, pursuant to section 205 of the Federal Power Act (FPA),1
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) submitted proposed revisions to its Open Access
Transmission Tariff (OATT) and Reliability Assurance Agreement (RAA) (collectively,
Tariff) to establish:  (1) a definition of Large Load; (2) a Large Load Registry; (3) a new
Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS); (4) a requirement for Load Serving Entities
(LSE) serving New Large Load to designate new capacity resources in an amount at least
equal to the New Large Load’s peak load to avoid the New Large Load being subject to
IRAS; (5) rules for Bring Your Own New Capacity (BYONC) arrangements; and (6)
rules to exclude Eligible New Large Load from the Variable Resource Requirement
(VRR) curve beginning with Delivery Year 2029/2030.
2.
In this order, we accept PJM’s proposed Tariff revisions to establish a definition of
Large Load and a Large Load Registry, effective October 12, 2026, as requested, and
require PJM to submit a compliance filing within 30 days of the date of issuance of this
order.  We reject PJM’s remaining proposed Tariff revisions, as discussed below.

B.
Substantive Matters
12.
PJM’s proposal consists of several elements that, when combined, are intended to
address the capacity shortfall that, absent the entrance of new capacity resources, will
prevent PJM from satisfying the PJM Region Reliability Requirement starting in the
2027/2028 Delivery Year.  The first element of PJM’s proposal is to establish definitions
of Large Load and New Large Load.  The latter is designed to capture the Delivery Year
when PJM’s capacity shortfall, which PJM contends is the result of data center load

growth,11 first emerges.  Next, PJM proposes to develop a Large Load Registry to foster
transparency regarding Large Load additions.  With that foundation, PJM proposes to
adopt the IRAS framework, under which LSEs that seek to use Network Integration
Transmission Service (NITS)12 to serve New Large Loads must designate sufficient new
capacity resources, referred to as BYONC, to serve the New Large Load.  If an LSE
using NITS to serve New Large Load fails to designate sufficient capacity resources,
such New Large Load will be “subject to load reduction requests” when there are
insufficient resources to reliably meet demand in real-time operations.13  In essence, the
requirement that these New Large Loads must curtail under certain emergency conditions
is designed to allow PJM to address its capacity shortfall.14
13.
As outlined in greater detail below, we accept PJM’s proposal in part, effective
October 12, 2026, as requested, reject PJM’s proposal in part, and direct PJM to submit a
compliance filing within 30 days of the date of this order.15  Specifically, we accept
PJM’s proposal to establish a definition for Large Load and a Large Load Registry.16  We
find that those two elements of PJM’s proposal are just and reasonable and not unduly
discriminatory or preferential.  Given PJM’s consent to sever or modify its proposal, we
do so here.  We find that the elements of PJM’s proposal to establish a new definition of
Large Load and a Large Load Registry are severable from the remainder of the filing and
therefore may be accepted even though we reject the remainder of the proposal.

Moreover, because the elements of PJM’s proposal other than the Large Load
definition and Large Load Registry represent an integrated package that must be viewed
as a whole, we reject the remainder of PJM’s proposal.  PJM, while consenting to
severance and modification of the proposal, acknowledges that the RAA and OATT
revisions are “designed to work in lockstep and provide complementary pieces.”17  We
find that the remaining elements of PJM’s proposal, such as the proposed rules for
BYONC arrangements and the exclusion of Eligible New Large Loads from the VRR
curve, are not simply complementary, but instead are inextricably linked.  For example,
the new obligation for LSEs seeking to use NITS to serve New Large Load to bring new
capacity to avoid curtailment under IRAS is foundational to both the IRAS and BYONC
frameworks.  It also establishes the need for the rules for BYONC arrangements and
facilitates excluding Eligible New Large Loads from the VRR curve.  As such, we
conclude that, contrary to PJM’s statements in its transmittal, the only elements of its
proposal that are severable are the proposed definition of Large Load and the proposed
Large Load Registry, which can be implemented independent of the IRAS and BYONC
frameworks.

27.

not unduly discriminatory or preferential.49  First, we find that establishing the threshold
to qualify as a Large Load at a cumulative peak of 50 MW strikes a just and reasonable
balance between capturing the larger loads into which PJM needs visibility to ensure the
reliability of its system and excluding smaller loads that do not raise the same reliability
concerns in PJM.50  We acknowledge protesters’ arguments that PJM has not explained

Determination
77.
We find that certain aspects of PJM’s proposal appear to present an impermissible
intrusion on retail rate regulation and are not subject to the Commission’s authority
pursuant to section 205 of the FPA.174  We find that several provisions of the proposed
Tariff appear to place obligations directly on Eligible New Large Loads, which are, for
the most part,175 retail customers; that certain other provisions appear to dictate which
retail customers taking retail service will be curtailed; and that the proposed
compensation framework appears to establish a retail rate.176  Therefore, we reject PJM’s
proposed IRAS framework.

78.
The FPA authorizes the Commission to regulate “rates, terms, and conditions” and
any “rule, regulation, practice, or contract” directly “affecting” such transmission or
wholesale rates.177  But the law places beyond the Commission’s jurisdiction, and leaves
to the states alone, the regulation of “any other sale”—most notably, any retail sale—of
electricity.178  Section 201 of the FPA further limits the Commission’s jurisdiction to
“those matters which are not subject to regulation by the states.”179
79.
In EPSA, the U.S. Supreme Court stated that although “the wholesale and retail
markets in electricity are inextricably linked,”180 the Commission “may not regulate . . .
retail sales of electricity.”181  The Court further stated that “no matter how direct, or
dramatic” a proposal’s impact on wholesale rates, the Commission may not regulate retail
electricity sales.182  The Court also clarified that the FPA limits the Commission’s
jurisdiction to wholesale sales, reserving regulatory authority over retail sales to the states
alone, and that any regulation that “specifies terms of sale at retail” exceeds the
Commission’s authority under the FPA.183
80.
In the instant proceeding, protesters argue that PJM is proposing, via a
Commission-jurisdictional tariff, terms of retail service for Eligible New Large Loads.
These protesters contend that PJM is doing so by placing obligations on Eligible New
Large Loads, which are, for the most part, retail customers taking retail service; by
dictating which retail customers an Electric Distributor must curtail; and by establishing
the compensation for Eligible New Large Loads.184  PJM’s proposed construct appears to
regulate the terms and conditions of a retail customer’s service in ways that are beyond
the Commission’s authority.

appears to be unjust and unreasonable because it does not include transmission services
that reflect Eligible Customers taking transmission service on behalf of flexible large
loads that are willing and able to limit their use of the transmission system under certain
conditions.255  The Commission directed PJM to explain whether its Tariff remains just
and reasonable without Non-Firm Contract Demand Service for flexible large loads.256  It
appears that a non-firm transmission service similar to Non-Firm Contract Demand
transmission service, with appropriate adjustments, might be considered as a potential
avenue to achieve some of the aims of PJM’s proposed IRAS framework.  However, we
note that Non-Firm Contract Demand transmission service is affirmatively a voluntary
option, while the product or service here is not.  Should PJM choose to refile its IRAS
proposal, we encourage PJM to clearly identify the product or service IRAS is intended
to provide and ensure its proposed Tariff revisions consistently reflect that
characterization.  We also encourage PJM to consider providing a description of how it
will transition from any proposed types of service back to its traditional market structures
and how it will avoid a long-term bifurcation of loads in the PJM market.  Although we
are rejecting PJM’s proposal, PJM may consider refiling an alternative proposal under
FPA section 205 that considers the guidance provided herein.

The Commission orders:

(A)
PJM’s proposal is hereby accepted in part, effective October 12, 2026, as
requested, and rejected in part, as discussed in the body of the order.
(B)
PJM is hereby directed to submit a compliance filing within 30 days of the
date of this order, as discussed in the body of this order.

ROSNER, Commissioner, concurring:

1.
I agree with the decision to reject the bulk of PJM’s filing for the reasons
articulated in today’s order.  I write separately to emphasize that, if PJM chooses to refile
its proposal, it must justify its proposal to curtail IRAS load on a mandatory basis before
load that has voluntarily enrolled in a demand response program.  I also urge PJM to
carefully consider the consequences any refiled proposal might have on its capacity
market.
2.
Customers in a demand response program allow a utility to reduce their demand
without resorting to the extreme measure of manually pulling breakers on the utility’s
distribution network.  Demand response customers also receive payments in exchange for
volunteering to reduce their usage when the grid is stressed.  This structure is designed to
protect consumers and their equipment from the draconian measure of being involuntarily
forced off the system, as well as to send efficient market signals based on actual system
conditions.
3.
PJM’s filing flips this paradigm, proposing to force IRAS load off the system in
the earliest stages of emergency conditions,1 before PJM deploys demand response.2
4.
Moreover, PJM’s proposal would force IRAS load off the system in lieu of
sending capacity price signals that are needed to get new infrastructure built.  Because
PJM would forcibly curtail IRAS load before other firm load, PJM excludes IRAS load
from its capacity market demand curve.  The result could be a capacity market signaling
that PJM has more than enough capacity while the region sheds thousands of megawatts
of demand on an involuntary basis.  That’s not only economically inefficient, but also
likely to prevent supply and demand from coming back into balance.

2 PJM cleared 7,017 MW of demand response in its most recent capacity auction.

5.
To be clear, in an emergency, data centers not serving critical functions should be
