I.
BACKGROUND
DCRT is a Participating Transmission Owner (“PTO”) and thus a TO under the CAISO
Open Access Transmission Tariff (“CAISO Tariff”).  DCRT owns and manages the 125-mile, 500
kilovolt Ten West Link Transmission Line (“Ten West Link”), which runs from Arizona Public
Service Company’s (“APS”) Delaney Substation to Southern California Edison’s (“SCE”)
Colorado River Substation.  In 2015, DCRT won the CAISO’s competitive solicitation leading to
the development of Ten West Link.  Ten West Link entered commercial operation in June 2024,
and is currently one of the few interconnections between Arizona and southern California.
Approximately 100 miles of Ten West Link are in Arizona.
The entirety of Ten West Link, including the portion located in Arizona, is part of the
CAISO Balancing Authority Area (“BAA”) and is subject to the CAISO’s operational control,
with transmission-related service over the line provided under the CAISO Tariff and DCRT’s TO

Tariff.  Therefore, Ten West Link is integrated into the CAISO transmission system, and, in fact,
extends the CAISO footprint into the retail franchise territory of APS, a state-franchised utility
that is not a PTO and that operates its own BAA and is regulated by the Arizona Corporation

DCRT’s facility would sit within the retail service territory of a state-franchised utility, outside the
CAISO BAA, while interconnecting to a facility that is part of the CAISO controlled grid.
As the Commission understands, the OSC raises a wide range of issues for industry
participants, especially transmission-only companies (“Transcos”) like DCRT.  Tariff provisions
that presume a traditional franchised public utility model (e.g., a retail tariff, traditional native load
customers, a retail regulator) do not properly apply to Transcos.  DCRT is a prototypical example:
DCRT is not a Load Serving Entity (“LSE”), has no network customers, and every mile of its
transmission facility is part of the CAISO controlled grid, even though most of its facilities are in
Arizona, across a state boundary and within the retail franchise territory of APS.  APS is not fully
integrated into the CAISO footprint.3
DCRT’s differences from a traditional utility with a franchised service territory are a
structural feature of DCRT’s Commission-approved role as a transmission-only PTO, not a defect
in DCRT’s existing TO Tariff.  It does mean, however, that the potential tariff provisions DCRT
develops in response to the OSC may not necessarily be adapted from the integrated-utility model;
they must be drafted for the transmission-only context, and this distinction is what primarily drives
DCRT’s requested abeyance.  DCRT’s TO Tariff revisions must not only address DCRT’s
obligations under the CAISO framework but also interface with the retail framework of a state-

II.

A.
The OSC Requires Substantial Coordination by Multiple Stakeholders and
Time to Address DCRT’s Unique Circumstances
DCRT understands the Commission is focusing on the integration of large loads into the
bulk power system and the national and regional economies.  DCRT commends the Commission
for focusing on this issue to promote the greater national interest.  DCRT believes that the
interconnection and provision of transmission service to Eligible Customers on behalf of large
loads in the CAISO region can be faster, more cost-effective, and ultimately better for transmission
customers.  DCRT appreciates the Commission’s desire for prompt action and the invitation to be
the “first wielder of the pen.”4

days, which should “include both (1) a robust description of the content of a potential future filing

B.
Cost Shifting Risk Among Transmission Customers
The OSC  states that the CAISO and/or TO Tariffs may be unjust and unreasonable because
of “the apparent lack of (1) transparency regarding the assignment of network upgrades, and their
associated costs, that are needed to provide transmission service to Eligible Customers on behalf
of large loads; and (2) a pro forma cost recovery agreement between CAISO, the relevant
transmission owner, and the Eligible Customer taking transmission service on behalf of large loads
that help ensure that Eligible Customers bear the risk and are ultimately responsible for costs
incurred to provide transmission service, including the cost of Network Upgrades.”26  DCRT will
also address these cost-shifting concerns with proposed TO Tariff changes in its section 205 filing.

C.
Co-Location Arrangements; Flexible Transmission Services; Electrically
Proximate Large Load and Co-Located Load
With respect to co-located loads, flexible loads, and large loads that are electrically
proximate to new generation, the Commission has identified the following issues with respect to
the CAISO Tariff, as opposed to the TO Tariffs.  First, the Commission noted that the CAISO
Tariff appears to be unjust and unreasonable without “clear and consistent provisions addressing
the rates, terms, and conditions that apply to co-location arrangements and for transmission
services that reflect an Eligible Customer taking transmission service on behalf of Eligible Load
that is willing and able to limit its energy withdrawals from the transmission system under certain
conditions.”29  Second, the Commission found that the CAISO Tariff appears unjust and
unreasonable because it does not include “transmission services that reflect Eligible Customers
taking transmission service on behalf of flexible large loads that are willing and able to limit their
use of the transmission system under certain conditions.”30  Third, the Commission found that the
CAISO Tariff appears to be unjust and unreasonable because it does not contain provisions
“allowing an interconnection customer to seek the generator interconnection service(s) that reflects
the operational dynamics of serving either: (1) electrically proximate large load or; (2) a co-located
load . . . that has a high peak load.”31
