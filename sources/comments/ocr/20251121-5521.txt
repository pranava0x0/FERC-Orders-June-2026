interconnection policy is now directly tied to America's energy security, economic leadership,

II. About DCC
The Data Center Coalition (DCC) is the membership association for the U.S. data center
industry, representing leading data center owners and operators as well as companies leasing
large amounts of data center capacity! DCC's member companies provide the digital
infrastructure that supports the applications, capabilities, and essential services that enable our
modern economy. Our members are making significant, multi-billion dollar investments in
America's infrastructure that benefit and provide employment opportunities to states and local
communities. These investments support hundreds of thousands of quality jobs across the nation
and contribute billions of dollars in local, state, and federal tax revenue, providing consistent
funding for important community priorities like public safety, education, and transportation.
Between 2017 and 2023, the data center industry's total contribution to U.S. GDP was $3.5
trillion. In 2023, the U.S. data center industry directly employed more than 600,000 workers and
supported 4.7 million jobs in total. The sector generated $404 billion in total labor income and
contributed $162.7 billion in federal, state, and local taxes in 2023. Notably, each direct data
center job sustains more than six additional jobs elsewhere in the economy, underscoring the
sector's broad multiplier effect.?

IV. Support for FERC's Attention and a Clear, Workable Framework
DCC appreciates DOE's and the Commission's leadership in initiating an examination of
the framework governing the interconnection of large loads. The current reality is clear: across
the country, interconnection timelines for large loads have lengthened, processes differ
substantially across utilities and states, and the treatment of hybrid facilities that combine load
and generation remains inconsistent and, in many cases, underdeveloped. These conditions create
uncertainty, delay investment, and complicate grid planning at a time when the nation can least

The Commission's attention to this challenge is both timely and appropriate. A more
transparent, predictable, and uniform approach to large load interconnections, particularly at the
transmission level, would provide substantial benefits in terms of coordination, system reliability,
and economic development. This is especially true where new load and new generation seek to
interconnect contemporaneously or as part of a single, integrated project.
As the Commission evaluates whether and how to exercise its authority, DCC
respectfully urges a measured approach that recognizes the historic balance of federal and state
roles embedded in the Federal Power Act. States remain indispensable partners in regulating
retail service, overseeing siting and permitting, and supporting local and regional economic
priorities. Their engagement and support will be critical to the success of any final framework.
We support the Commission's exploration of legally durable potential pathways that
could provide a clear, nondiscriminatory, and workable structure for large load interconnections
that complements state authority, preserves space for regional innovation, and gives customers
and utilities more certainty and efficiency.
The status quo is not serving the nation's economic or reliability needs. DCC welcomes
the Commission's effort to consider solutions that are carefully designed, responsibly
implemented, and grounded in the legal, operational, and jurisdictional realities that define the
modern electric grid.
V. Transition Provisions: Regulatory Certainty as a Prerequisite to Reform
Reforms intended to improve timeliness and predictability should not inadvertently
disrupt projects that have already made significant financial, contractual, and engineering
commitments. DCC therefore strongly recommends clear and legally enforceable transition

--- PAGE 5 ---
provisions, including:
1. Respect for Existing Agreements
Projects with fully executed binding electricity service agreements, tariffs, construction
service agreements, and/or transmission security agreements (or similar such structures)
must be allowed to continue under their existing terms. These arrangements reflect
bargained-for rights and obligations and should not be retroactively disturbed
2. Protection for Projects in Advanced Stages
Projects that have completed feasibility or system impact studies should be permitted to
complete their studies under the framework in place at the time they entered the study
process. Forcing restudy or process migration could create material delays, cost increases,
and uncertainty for utilities and customers alike.
3. Transparent and Time-Bound Transition Rules
As any new framework is implemented, FERC should establish clear, time-bound
transition rules. Applicants should know with certainty which process applies based on
the date of their interconnection request or the stage they have reached.
This proceeding should acceleratenot pause critical load and generation projects already
advancing under existing frameworks. DCC believes these transition protections are critical to
maintaining industry confidence and ensuring that reforms deliver additional speed and clarity
without disrupting progress already underway.
VI. Hybrid Load-Plus-Generation Facilities
DCC supports DOE's emphasis on hybrid facilities. The ability to study new load and
new generation together, when physically proximate and electrically significant, presents
meaningful system benefits. Joint study of paired facilities can reduce the need for network
upgrades, shorten energization timelines, improve resource adequacy, minimize congestion and
curtailment, and expand siting options. These efficiencies are increasingly relevant as load and
supply additions occur on more compressed timelines and in more diverse configurations.
At present, however, the treatment of hybrid load-plus-generation facilities remains
highly inconsistent across regions and transmission providers. This variation introduces

--- PAGE 6 ---
uncertainty, delays investment, and undermines the potential reliability and cost benefits that
these paired configurations can provide. A clear, Commission-directed framework could help
resolve this bottleneck by establishing consistent principles for studying hybrid facilities, while
still allowing for regional variation and respecting state authority. The Commission already has a
complete record on co-location issues in PJM. With that proceeding fully briefed and ready for
decision, the Commission can deliver immediate clarity by acting in that docket.?
To be clear, such a framework should not mandate hybrid development or require load to
be paired with on-site generation. Rather, it should recognize hybrid configurations as a valid
and efficient option within the interconnection process, and ensure that customers pursuing them
are able to do so transparently and without procedural disadvantage.
VII. Stand-Alone Load and Market-Based Procurement
Although hybrid configurations offer meaningful benefits in many circumstances, it is
essential that the interconnection process continue to support stand-alone load as a viable and
efficient option. Large customers must retain the ability to interconnect load without being
required to co-locate new generation or modify the manner in which they procure energy.
Commercial flexibility has long been a hallmark of the competitive wholesale markets that the
Commission has fostered and overseen for decades.
Customers may serve their load through a variety of lawful arrangements, including
bilateral power purchase agreements, participation in organized wholesale markets, utility default
service, or in some instances, a portfolio that blends these options. The Commission has

and expand resource diversity, all to the benefit of consumers. In RTO regions, competitive
procurement is not merely a financial preference; it is the mechanism through which new load
can be served by the most efficient mix of resources across the entire RTO footprint.
An interconnection framework that preserves market access is therefore essential. Not all
new load can—or should-be expected to build generation behind the meter or at the point of
interconnection. In many cases, it is more economically efficient and system-optimal for load to
rely on the regional market, especially in areas with diverse supply portfolios, well-developed
transmission, or liquidity in capacity, energy, and ancillary service markets. Market-based
procurement allows the full fleet of competitive suppliers not just those co-located behind the
fenceto compete to serve new demand, thereby maintaining price discipline, supporting entry
of new resources, and ensuring that the most cost-effective solutions rise to the top.
Timely, predictable, and nondiscriminatory access to interconnection service for
stand-alone load is therefore critical, not only to the customers seeking service, but to the
continued functioning of the market itself. If customers are forced into specific commercial
structures, the result would be less competition, reduced commercial flexibilities, and slower
demand growth at a moment when the grid requires both investment and innovation.
A durable framework must therefore allow load to interconnect efficiently, whether or not
that load is paired with on-site supply, and regardless of the commercial structure through which
the customer procures energy. This is fully consistent with the Commission's longstanding
commitment to open access, competition, and customer choice, and with the principle that load
should be able to rely on the same competitive markets whose design the Commission has spent
more than two decades refining.

VIII. Interconnection Reform Is Fundamentally an Infrastructure Challenge
The Commission's responsibility to ensure reliability under Section 215 of the Federal
Power Act cannot be separated from the physical limits of the transmission system. The core
challenge facing the electric system today is not merely procedural delay in generator
interconnection studies, but the lack of sufficient transmission infrastructure and forward
planning to accommodate new load and new generation in a timely manner.*
Generator interconnection queues have grown into multi-year bottlenecks not because
customers are unwilling to invest, nor because generation is unavailable, but because the
transmission system lacks sufficient unencumbered headroom to integrate these resources. In
many cases, projects cannot proceed because needed network upgrades were not planned,
permitted, or built in advance. The interconnection backlog is thus a symptom of a deeper and
more structural issue: the grid was not expanded or modernized at the pace that changing system
needs require. The lack of supply to meet today's demand is not a failure of market
interest-rather a failure of infrastructure.
The Commission has long recognized that transmission access, transmission planning,
and resource adequacy are inseparable. Order No. 888 opened the grid to competition,
establishing nondiscriminatory access as a foundational principle.' Order No. 2000 advanced that
framework by authorizing and encouraging regional transmission organizations to facilitate
regional planning and more efficient use of transmission assets.' Order No. 1920/1920-A now

IX. Complementary Commission Authorities Should Be Exercised in Parallel
While this rulemaking provides an important forum for considering reforms specific to
large load interconnection, the Commission already possesses substantial authority to improve
the timeliness and efficiency of load and generator interconnections, ensure nondiscriminatory
access, and support the infrastructure necessary to maintain system reliability and economic
competitiveness. These tools should be advanced now, in parallel with this proceeding.
First, the Commission should reinforce the need for forward-looking transmission
planning that reflects real and foreseeable load growth. Load growth on the scale now
materializing cannot be served through reactive planning frameworks. Lagged interconnection
times for both generation and load are due to a lack of headroom on the system. It is critical that
the Commission focus on the infrastructure challenge through appropriate long-term
transmission planning. Further, there is broad agreement that interregional transfer capability
increases system resilience and reliability. The Commission has the necessary information to
take the next steps to ensure we have prudent amounts of transfer capability.? Finally, the

X. Conclusion
The Data Center Coalition appreciates the Commission's leadership in examining the
interconnection of large loads at a moment when the nation's electric system is undergoing rapid
and unprecedented change. The ability to interconnect new load and new generation is no longer
a narrow operational matter; it is now central to maintaining reliability, fostering economic
growth, strengthening domestic industry, and preserving American leadership in advanced
technologies.
To succeed, the Commission's approach must provide clarity, timeliness, and
predictability for customers and transmission providers alike. It must also respect the
foundational balance of federal and state roles that has long defined the Federal Power Act, while
ensuring that the transmission system remains open, competitive, and capable of supporting
emerging national priorities.

DCC supports the Commission's exploration of a framework that improves transparency
and coordination, enables both hybrid and stand-alone load configurations, and aligns
interconnection processes with the physical realities of the grid and the timing needs of modern
industry. At the same time, the Commission should continue to advance the complementary tools
already within its authority, including forward-looking transmission planning, improved
generation interconnection processes, defined interconnection service options, customer
self-build opportunities, and the deployment of grid-enhancing technologies.
The stakes are clear-if the United States is to maintain resource adequacy, economic
competitiveness, and technological leadership, the grid must be capable of interconnecting both
load and supply at the pace required by today's economy. DCC looks forward to working with
the Commission, states, utilities, RTOs, and other stakeholders to help develop an approach that
meets these challenges while upholding the principles of reliability, nondiscrimination, and open
access that remain at the core of federal energy regulation.
