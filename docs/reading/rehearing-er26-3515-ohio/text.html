I.
INTRODUCTION 
The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC”) October 9, 2026 Order1 in this 
case fails to protect consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) 
from the risks and costs that will result from acceptance of PJM’s Large Load Registry proposal 
in these dockets. In particular, that order allows PJM to exclude state consumer advocate offices 
from the list of entities entitled to access confidential information to be collected and maintained 
in the Large Load Registry.
This decision will prevent state agencies directly charged with representing consumers 
from being able to access critical information related to large load data centers with “billion-
dollar implications for consumers.”2 Consumer advocates have a statutory responsibility to 
protect residential and other utility consumers from unjust and unreasonable rates. That

II.
BACKGROUND 
PJM proposed in this case to implement an Interim Resource Adequacy Service 
(“IRAS”), effective with the June 1, 2027 Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) Delivery Year.  
That proposed service would interrupt, or curtail service to New Large Loads that fail to bring 
sufficient capacity to satisfy their capacity obligations.7 PJM represented in the filing that it 
“hosts the largest concentration of data center load in the world,”8 and its load forecast “predicts 
peak load growth of 32 gigawatts [(“GW”)] from 2024 to 2030, 30 GW of which is projected to 
be from data centers.”9 PJM explained that the 2027/2028 RPM base residual auction cleared 
over 6,600 MW short of the region’s reliability requirement, and that the 2028/2029 RPM 
capacity auction cleared over 6,800 MW short of that requirement.10 In other words, PJM 
represented that the addition of new supply has not kept pace with rapid large load demand

B.
FERC’s exclusion of state consumer advocate offices from the confidential 
information in the Large Load Registry unduly discriminates against these 
statutorily mandated consumer representatives. 
In Duke Energy, FERC found that it is unreasonable to limit access to confidential 
information and Critical Energy Infrastructure Information (“CEII”) in the transmission planning 
process only to certain entities that obtained authorization from the Commission to access CEII 
contained in Form 715 reports.35  FERC stated that “these limitations unreasonably restrict the 
ability of affected stakeholders to participate fully in transmission planning meetings and that 
transmission providers may develop mechanisms, such as confidentiality agreements and 
password-protected access to information, in order to manage confidentiality and CEII 
concerns.”36
Notably, the confidentiality concerns in Duke Energy did not involve an adjudicatory 
proceeding, but rather involved access to confidential information in a transmission planning 
process. That process is not unlike the situation here, where state consumer advocate offices need 
access to confidential information that will affect generation and transmission planning, rates and 
curtailments for retail consumers, including the 67 million consumers who ultimately pay the 
rates under discussion.

C.
FERC’s ruling is an unlawful unexplained departure from its rulings in the 
RTO show cause proceedings recognizing the need to provide critical 
transmission planning and cost allocation information to state regulatory 
commissions and state consumer advocate offices. 
As noted previously, FERC recently issued Show Cause orders against the six RTOs in 
the nation. These orders find that the existing tariffs of these RTOs are unjust and unreasonable 
because they provide insufficient transparency for state regulatory commissions and state 
consumer advocate offices regarding the effects of large load data centers on transmission 
planning and allocation of transmission costs.42
FERC specifically noted in PJM’s Show Cause Order that “the lack of centralized 
information on the associated transmission costs in PJM hamper the ability of the Commission 
and stakeholders (including transmission customers and state regulators) to understand and 
accurately assess the relationship between the addition of large loads to the transmission system 
and wholesale transmission costs.”43 Citing the “speculative nature” of requests for transmission 
service for large data center loads, FERC found that “[t]he speed and scale with which Eligible 
Customers are seeking transmission service on behalf of large loads makes it difficult for 
interested parties, including transmission customers and state regulators, to accurately assess

confidentiality of that information under regulatory rules and orders.  FERC should reverse its 
ruling on this issue, and direct PJM to extend access to the Large Load Registry confidential 
information to state consumer advocate offices, subject to confidentiality obligations.
VI.
CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF 
WHEREFORE, OCC respectfully requests that FERC reconsider its ruling in the October 
9 Order.  FERC should afford consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM the full protections of 
just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory and preferential rates.  FERC should reject PJM’s 
Large Load Registry proposal with directions to refile the proposal with a provision that includes 
state consumer advocate offices in the list of entities entitled to access the confidential 
information that will be collected, maintained and made available in that Registry.
