I. INTRODUCTION
The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC”) October 9, 2026 Order1 in this case fails to protect consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) from the risks and costs that will result from acceptance of PJM’s Large Load Registry proposal in these dockets. In particular, that order allows PJM to exclude state consumer advocate offices from the list of entities entitled to access confidential information to be collected and maintained in the Large Load Registry.

This decision will prevent state agencies directly charged with representing consumers from being able to access critical information related to large load data centers with “billion- dollar implications for consumers.”2 Consumer advocates have a statutory responsibility to protect residential and other utility consumers from unjust and unreasonable rates. II. BACKGROUND
PJM proposed in this case to implement an Interim Resource Adequacy Service
(“IRAS”), effective with the June 1, 2027 Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) Delivery Year. That proposed service would interrupt, or curtail service to New Large Loads that fail to bring sufficient capacity to satisfy their capacity obligations.7 PJM represented in the filing that it “hosts the largest concentration of data center load in the world,”8 and its load forecast “predicts peak load growth of 32 gigawatts [(“GW”)] from 2024 to 2030, 30 GW of which is projected to be from data centers.”9 PJM explained that the 2027/2028 RPM base residual auction cleared over 6,600 MW short of the region’s reliability requirement, and that the 2028/2029 RPM capacity auction cleared over 6,800 MW short of that requirement.10 In other words, PJM represented that the addition of new supply has not kept pace with rapid large load demand

OCC seeks rehearing of FERC’s failure to direct PJM to allow state consumer advocates access to the Large Load Registry confidential information. B. FERC’s exclusion of state consumer advocate offices from the confidential
information in the Large Load Registry unduly discriminates against these
statutorily mandated consumer representatives.
In Duke Energy, FERC found that it is unreasonable to limit access to confidential information and Critical Energy Infrastructure Information (“CEII”) in the transmission planning process only to certain entities that obtained authorization from the Commission to access CEII contained in Form 715 reports.35  FERC stated that “these limitations unreasonably restrict the ability of affected stakeholders to participate fully in transmission planning meetings and that transmission providers may develop mechanisms, such as confidentiality agreements and password-protected access to information, in order to manage confidentiality and CEII concerns.”36

Notably, the confidentiality concerns in Duke Energy did not involve an adjudicatory proceeding, but rather involved access to confidential information in a transmission planning process. That process is not unlike the situation here, where state consumer advocate offices need access to confidential information that will affect generation and transmission planning, rates and curtailments for retail consumers, including the 67 million consumers who ultimately pay the rates under discussion.

C. FERC’s ruling is an unlawful unexplained departure from its rulings in the
RTO show cause proceedings recognizing the need to provide critical
transmission planning and cost allocation information to state regulatory
commissions and state consumer advocate offices.
As noted previously, FERC recently issued Show Cause orders against the six RTOs in
the nation. These orders find that the existing tariffs of these RTOs are unjust and unreasonable because they provide insufficient transparency for state regulatory commissions and state consumer advocate offices regarding the effects of large load data centers on transmission planning and allocation of transmission costs.42

FERC specifically noted in PJM’s Show Cause Order that “the lack of centralized
information on the associated transmission costs in PJM hamper the ability of the Commission and stakeholders (including transmission customers and state regulators) to understand and accurately assess the relationship between the addition of large loads to the transmission system and wholesale transmission costs.”43 Citing the “speculative nature” of requests for transmission service for large data center loads, FERC found that “[t]he speed and scale with which Eligible Customers are seeking transmission service on behalf of large loads makes it difficult for interested parties, including transmission customers and state regulators, to accurately assess
