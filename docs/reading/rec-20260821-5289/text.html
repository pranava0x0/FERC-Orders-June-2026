II. 
GENERAL COMMENTS 
 RESA asks that the Commission take note of the need for market certainty and stability 
and the importance of granular cost allocations consistent with cost causation principles.2  In light 
of today’s ever increasing capacity prices, rate shock caused by mis-allocated costs will cause 
significant harm to those entities who are not causing the costs to increase.   
RESA members purchase services in the RTO/ISO markets such as energy, capacity and 
ancillary services, but they offer more than just electricity service to their customers.  Competitive 
retail suppliers may provide  retail energy to end-use customers at contracted fixed or variable 
prices which are tied to wholesale electricity prices.  Retail suppliers may offer demand response 
services – assisting customers in reducing peak demand and receiving benefits for reducing 
consumption at peak times.  Retail suppliers may offer energy efficiency services to assist 
customers in reducing their electricity consumption.  Retail suppliers may offer electricity with a 
certain component of renewable energy – or all renewable energy.  There is no one model –

competitive wholesale markets and the introduction of retail choice in states such as Ohio, Illinois, 
Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and Maryland, have allowed significant innovation, which 
has reduced consumption of energy, lowered prices, and provided valuable services to consumers.   
Competitive retail suppliers operate in an environment of risk.  Retail suppliers are unlike 
traditional utilities in several important ways.  For example, retail suppliers are not cost-based 
regulated by state public utility commissions (although they comply with a myriad of state 
requirements to be authorized to provide energy-related products and services to retail customers).  
Retail suppliers do not have a mechanism to defer costs, like traditional utilities, and thus bear the 
risk of wholesale market volatility and uncertainty.  Retail suppliers do not have a specified service 
area.  They contract with customers bilaterally.  Generally, these contracts are long-term – of a 
duration of one to five years. As a result, when a contract expires, it may or may not be renewed.  
Thus, a customer today may not be a customer tomorrow.  As a result, charges allocated to a utility 
service area or load zone must be properly allocated to the appropriate retail end use customer, 
even if the customer has changed suppliers or returned to default service (i.e. the customer is served 
by different LSEs).   
To offer valuable and innovative services at competitive prices, retail suppliers 
participating in competitive wholesale markets require market certainty, transparency and stability.  
Market certainty, transparency and stability allow retail suppliers to offer products and services 
that they know they can deliver during the term of the applicable contract.  The importance of 
having confidence in the competitive wholesale market cannot be overstated.  As with products 
and services offered by retail suppliers, the contracts vary by company, by service, by customer 
type, by term and other conditions.  If competitive retail suppliers are participating in default 
service programs, those programs often require winning suppliers to agree to a standard form

 III. 
PROTEST 
A. The Commission should Deny PJM’s Default Allocation Proposal and Require 
PJM to Use Sub-Accounts to Allocate Costs to Large Load  
RESA supports PJM's RBP Proposal to the extent that it assigns RBP costs and collateral 
obligations to the LSE serving the Large Load Customers that cause those costs. PJM’s proposal 
to facilitate the allocation of costs to cost causers, however, is undermined to the extent that PJM’s 
proposed default methodology applies. Under PJM’s RBP, if an Electric Distributor does not report 
Reliability Backstop Obligations (“RBO”) for the Large Load Customers in its zone, PJM will 
instead allocate RBP costs to all load in that zone based on each LSE’s existing Peak Load 
Contribution assignments.  The result is that the cost of capacity procured specifically to serve 
new, not-yet-interconnected Large Load through the RBP would be borne by residential, 
commercial, and traditional industrial customers who did not cause those costs and who receive 
no corresponding benefit.  
This is not a remote contingency.  As PJM acknowledges, neither the Commission nor PJM 
can dictate how RBP costs are allocated among the customers and LSEs behind each Electric 
Distributor. Ensuring allocation of RBP costs to cost causers will require each Relevant Electric

causation, but by the pace of administrative implementation in individual states, RERRAs and 
Electric Distributor service territories. 
PJM touts its cost allocation proposal as supporting the Statement of Principles Regarding

allocate to end-use customers in their respective states in accordance with their laws and 
policies, supports the NEDC/PJM Governors’ Principles, which include the principle that 
the cost of new capacity should be allocated to data centers who have caused the need for 
additional capacity in the PJM Region.4

B.
If the Commission Accepts PJM’s Default Allocation Proposal, PJM Should be 
Ordered to Take Additional Steps to Protect Residential, Commercial and 
Traditional Industrial Customers from Cost Shifts  
If the Commission accepts PJM’s Tariff proposal to, as a default, allocate RBP costs to 
LSEs based on the sum of their customers’ Peak Load Contributions, states, RERRAs and Electric 
Distributors will be under significant pressure to timely implement RBO.   Accordingly, RESA 
respectfully requests that the Commission facilitate discussions among and between the states, 
RERRAs, Electric Distributors and interested Market Participants to assist in implementing the 
RBP Proposal and RBO.   
RESA hereby emphasizes the urgency of state action and collaboration between the states 
and PJM in implementing RBO. If the RBP Proposal is accepted substantially as filed, every PJM 
state — and particularly those open to retail competition — will need to move immediately to 
identify Large Load Customers and to assign RBP costs and collateral obligations to those 
customers and their LSEs.  RESA intends to participate in those proceedings, and the Commission 
should recognize in any order approving the RBP that the reasonableness of the cost allocation

C. 
The Commission Should Require Certain Commitments and Advance 
Disclosure from PJM to Reduce Risk and Provide Greater Certainty to 
LSEs.   
PJM’s RBP injects significant uncertainty to competitive wholesale markets to the 
detriment of wholesale and retail customers. In the short amount of time to analyze this filing, 
RESA has identified a number of concerns that are identified below. 
First, PJM should publish RBO determinations and resulting cost allocations expeditiously.  
Retail suppliers such as RESA members are currently submitting bids into default service supply 
solicitations to provide service to retail customers for long-term obligations of between one and 
five years.  Price certainty and the RBO is critical information in responding to these procurements

for future Delivery Years.  The central RBP auction is proposed to run from September 30 through 
October 21, 2026, while the facilitated bilateral matchmaking process is expected to continue for 
another six to nine months.  Thus, RESA asks that the Commission require PJM to publish 
Reliability Backstop Obligation determinations and cost allocations sufficiently in advance of

Second, PJM offers no guarantee regarding timing of resettlement of RBP charges.  RESA 
asks the Commission to confirm that RBP charges will not be subject to retroactive resettlement 
outside PJM's standard resettlement window. Retail suppliers cannot recover charges retroactively 
imposed after a contract has been priced and served, and the magnitude of the obligations at issue 
here makes that exposure materially different from ordinary settlement variance. 
Third, RESA further notes that excluding Large Load growth from future Reliability 
Pricing Model auction demand until that load is covered by new supply is directionally consistent 
with cost causation and may reduce capacity costs for all LSEs and the customers they serve. To 
realize that benefit, however, RESA asks that PJM be directed to re-release the 2029/2030 Base 
Residual Auction planning parameters promptly upon any change to the load forecast so suppliers 
can reflect a demand adjustment of this significance in their estimate of future capacity costs 
procured through the RPM. 
Fourth, PJM’s proposal appears to lack specificity around how RBP costs will be assigned 
to LSE’s serving Large Load Customers as those customers switch from one LSE to another 
throughout the period the RBP will be in effect.  For example, each state that permits retail access 
has rules and guidelines governing switching LSEs.  Under the RBP, how will PJM be notified of 
the transfer of the Large Load and how will appropriate collateral be collected from the transferee 
LSE and returned to the transfer LSE?  Will PJM deadlines interfere with the switch and leave the

Finally, while PJM’s proposal provides detailed steps to address LSE default on RBP
