I. Introduction 
LS Power Development, LLC (“LS Power”) appreciates the opportunity to 
participate in this examination of the efficacy of PJM Interconnection LLC (“PJM”) 
governance structure.1 Founded in 1990, LS Power is a premier development, 
investment, and operating company focused on the North American power and 
energy infrastructure sector, with leading platforms across generation, transmission 
and energy expansion solutions. Since inception, LS Power has developed or 
acquired 50,000 MW of power generation, including utility-scale solar, wind, hydro, 
battery energy storage, and natural gas-fired facilities. Through its transmission 
business, LS Power Grid, the company operates 7 transmission utilities, has built 
780+ miles of high-voltage transmission with another 400+ miles currently in 
construction or development. LS Power actively invests in and scales businesses that 
are meeting the growing needs of the energy expansion, including electric vehicle 
charging, demand response, microgrids, renewable fuels and waste-to-energy 
platforms. Over the years, LS Power has raised more than $85 billion in debt and 
equity capital to support North American infrastructure. LS Power has been an active 
participant in PJM for three decades.

II. The Board and PJM’s Jurisdictional Authority 
Our comments are grounded in the jurisdictional and regulatory limits that apply to 
the Commission, PJM, and the states. The Federal Power Act does not speak in terms 
of “affordability.” Instead, it requires FERC to judge whether a rate is “just and 
reasonable” — a standard that turns on the relationship between a seller’s costs and 
market position (market power), and the price that seller charges.3 “Affordability,” 
by contrast, is a qualitative judgment about the impact of a rate on the buyer, and 
turns on the relationship between a customer’s income and its retail bill. 
This distinction is why it is critical that the PJM Board focuses on producing 
wholesale market outcomes that are just and reasonable, while the states focus on 
how those market outcomes are then allocated — equitably or otherwise — to their 
retail constituents. Keeping these two distinct responsibilities straight and keeping 
them with the entities who jurisdictionally manage them, is foundational to 
everything that follows.4  Addressing the present significant load growth challenge 
will require hyperscalers, state and federal regulators, PJM, the PJM Board, 
transmission and generation asset owners and developers, end users, transmission 
dependent utilities and consumer advocates to work cooperatively to implement 
rules and regulations in the RTO, at the federal and at the state levels to manage this

to PJM’s success; any action that significantly tilts this balance in order to meet the 
moment must be considered judiciously so that it does not undermine PJM’s overall 
success. 
III. Board Independence 
Section 7.7 (i) of the Operating Agreement places the duty on the PJM Board to 
ensure “that a Member or group of Members shall not have undue influence over the 
operation of the PJM Region.”   
Note that the discussion around who has 205 rights applies only to the Operating 
Agreement.  With respect to resource adequacy, PJM has exclusive 205 rights under 
the Reliability Assurance Agreement (“RAA”).  Under the Operating Agreement, 
on the other hand, for matters that pertain to the energy market and regional 
transmission planning protocols, when the Members Committee reaches a 2/3 sector 
weighted vote, PJM is expected and indeed, required, to respect that vote and file

Given this structure, Board independence means that when the Board reaches a

agreements. PJM Board independence is not only a governance imperative for just 
and reasonable rates, but also an imperative to the long-term health of the market.  
Some are concerned with Board independence and transparency and have proposed 
that the Board hold open meetings.10 For the PJM Board to continue to enjoy 
independence requires them to continue to exercise their role with the highest ability 
to conduct fulsome deliberations which can only be afforded by maintaining closed 
Board sessions. LS Power is concerned that honest deliberation will be muted if 
Board members believe they are being judged for the appearance of favoring a 
particular position, and that open sessions risk becoming performative rather than 
substantive. The same reasoning counsels against disclosing individual votes.

IV. Enhancing State Participation in the Stakeholder Process 
LS Power has long supported expanded Section 205 rights for PJM states related to 
cost allocation for new transmission and supports ensuring the states have a 
meaningful way to participate in Board, PJM and stakeholder deliberations.  Given 
that there are 13 states and the District of Columbia, there is no single policy to 
incorporate into the PJM markets, unlike single-state RTOs.  It would undermine the 
independence of PJM and the Board to integrate a single state policy goal into the 
market rules of PJM.   
The states were intimately involved in the discussions involving the formation of 
PJM.  At that time, states did not want to be voting members because they did not 
want to be constrained by the rules governing stakeholders.  They wanted the ability 
to interface with PJM management and the Board outside of the stakeholder process.  
Some were also concerned that voting would give the appearance of prejudicing their 
primary role of ensuring wholesale prices are appropriately incorporated into state 
retail tariffs.  The concern was that it could prejudice the record if they were involved 
with the creation of these costs at the wholesale level.  They also did not want to 
participate directly in the RTO through a voting mechanism so that they could retain 
jurisdictional distinctions between the Commission and the states.15   
This reasoning still stands today.  Indeed, providing the States with a formal role in 
running PJM would add to the policy uncertainty that exists today.  As noted early 
in these comments, making multi-billion-dollar investments with several decades of 
useful life will require market structure durability and contract sanctity.  Both of 
those drivers of investment can be undermined by political pressure, which runs in 
four-year cycles and is not primarily focused on long-term market viability.   
To that point, PJM’s job is to run a market which in its basic terms matches supply 
to demand and produces a just and reasonable cost for the service.  The allocation of 
that cost then becomes a rate-making issue for the states, who rightly manage 
affordability constructs.  To a great extent, the states already can control those costs: 
for example, states control the physical siting for hyperscalers from building data 
centers in their states limiting demand, they can develop tariffs that ensure the 
hyperscalers pay their fair share for interconnection costs and retail service, and they

V. Making the Stakeholder Process More Efficient  
LS Power observes that the stakeholder process needs more discipline generally. LS 
Power thinks the specifics of that discipline are best left to stakeholders themselves, 
and FERC should not be distracted by delving into the details of such a process.  The 
Commission has in fact done exactly what is necessary:  issued a Show Cause Order 
under FPA section 206 “in order to ensure that PJM’s ongoing processes generate 
reforms that are sufficiently timely and comprehensive to address the concerns we 
identify today.”21 More direct involvement could distract the Commission from its 
primary role as an economic regulator and create unnecessary ex parte concerns, 
actually limiting the ability to informally discuss proposed changes with the 
Commission.  The Members have imposed such discipline before — the creation of 
the CIFP process is a good example22 — and can do it again. In fact, the focus of the 
PJM Liaison Committee meeting on July 13, 2026, was PJM governance.23 
There are guidelines to impose more discipline worth considering: 
• Limiting the number of educational sessions. Educational sessions should 
continue to be taped so that no one can claim they were unable to attend. (PJM 
does not currently record meetings, and we support continuing that approach 
for non-educational meetings).  
• At the MRC and Members Committee, attendees should arrive empowered to 
make final decision votes, including the ability to vote even if a proposal is 
amended on the floor — every time a representative must “go back” to their 
organization, that adds a month of delay to the process.

V.  What the Commission Can Do 
LS Power commends the Commission for its proactive efforts and orders to ensure 
that each region of the country can meet the changing electricity demands.  That 
said, LS Power believes that it is not just a cumbersome governance problem that 
has caused PJM to struggle to meet these demands, but a confluence of a number of 
challenges, including the need to revise retail tariffs, to better manage 
interconnection and transmission upgrades, to expedite supply chains, and the need 
to have forcing mechanisms that enable bilateral contracts between hyperscalers and 
new generation in order to obtain financing.  None of these challenges are unique to 
a competitive market such as PJM.  Vertically integrated utilities cannot bypass the 
physical realities of the current grid and supply chains, and moreover, their business 
model shifts the risk of cost and over-procurement back to ratepayers.  This reality 
should not, however, impede the ability to make enhancements to PJM’s governing 
process, as we have suggested here. 
The Commission also has a critical role to play: it must act under its statutory 
authority as an economic regulator.  It, too, is an independent organization staffed 
by industry experts.26 This expertise enables the Commission to be the ultimate 
backstop in allowing the Board and PJM to make politically unpopular decisions in 
order to carry out their mission of ensuring reliability through competitive markets.
