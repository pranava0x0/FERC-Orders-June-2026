Chairman Swett and Commissioners Rosner, See, Chang, and LaCerte;

Thank you for convening this Chairman and Commissioner-led Technical Conference in the above-captioned proceeding to discuss PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) governance and stakeholder processes, with a particular focus on identifying and evaluating concrete, actionable reforms to improve PJM’s ability to address operations and market needs in a timely and efficient manner.1 My name is Brian George and I serve as Sr. Lead, U.S. Energy Markets at Google, LLC (“Google”). My job responsibilities include establishing and executing Google’s energy strategy across the RTO and ISO regions, including PJM, and at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”), to ensure access to adequate power supply and infrastructure required to serve our data centers in the United States. I welcome this Commission’s proactive leadership on governance and stakeholder reforms in PJM, and appreciate the opportunity to submit this pre-filed statement and participate in the Technical Conference.

I offer these comments, observations, and recommendations in the interest of improving collaboration, fostering innovative thinking, and ultimately producing tangible results that lead to a more reliable and efficient grid that can simultaneously accommodate the integration of new large loads while also protecting residential consumers from unintended reliability or affordability issues. These pre-filed comments address the questions raised by the Commission in the Second Supplemental Notice of Commission-Led Technical Conference for Panel 2: PJM Stakeholder Process – Design, Transparency, and Decision-Making, and are informed primarily by my experience participating in the recent Critical Issue Fast Path (“CIFP”) process on behalf of Google and as a member of the Data Center Coalition (“DCC”).

I.​
PJM’s Stakeholder Process is a Robust, Open Forum, But Cannot Succeed Without Consistent, Active, and Transparent Leadership From PJM Executive Staff and

Board of Managers.

The PJM stakeholder process is a public forum, and with limited exceptions, open to participation from any individual or organization, including member states. In general, any person, including non-members, can participate in meetings, ask questions to PJM staff or other stakeholders regarding their respective packages, propose new packages or changes to existing packages, lobby other stakeholders, and build coalitions. Over the last year, during the course of PJM’s two CIFP stakeholder process to address Large Load Additions (“CIFP-LLA”) and the Reliability Backstop Procurement and Connect-and-Manage reforms (“CIFP-RBP/C&M”), several members of the PJM Board of Managers (“Board”) were present, providing all participants with the opportunity to directly appeal to the members of the Board.

Google is not a PJM member, and we do not have voting rights in the PJM stakeholder process. However, Google was deeply engaged in both CIFP processes. We participated in nearly every meeting, including in-person, when offered by PJM. In August 2025, at the outset of the CIFP-LLA, Google offered detailed public feedback to PJM and the Board, including proposed reforms of PJM’s large load forecasting process, PJM’s demand response rules to better align with Google’s demand response capabilities, and a Bring Your Own New Generation (“BYONG”) proposal to provide large loads a pathway to bilaterally contract to offset new load with new generation.2 In September, Google worked with a cross-sector coalition to develop, refine, and advance a multi-issue package that contained reforms of PJM’s load forecasting process, demand response rules and emergency procedures designed to protect residential consumers from blackouts in an emergency, and a proposed reliability backstop auction to address PJM’s supply shortfall.3 Google was also proud to help lead, and join, via the DCC, a multi-sector package that included four bipartisan governors and two major transmission owners in PJM.4

As a result of our efforts, and many others within, and outside of, the traditional stakeholder body, PJM and the Board had several strong options to address and manage large load additions. While many viewed the outcome of the CIFP-LLA as a failure because all twelve packages presented to the Board failed to receive the required sector-weighted approval, most the twelve

All things considered, the CIFP-LLA process worked quite well in identifying core issues and proposing conceptual reforms. It is not unreasonable to posit that with a narrowed and targeted focus, additional efforts to build consensus, including, critically, from PJM leadership, and transparency from the Board regarding their desired outcomes from the stakeholder body, and a little more time, consensus could have been achieved. Even if consensus could not have been achieved, the Board certainly would have had clear options for potential reforms.

PJM has a tremendous, diverse, and intellectually engaged stakeholder body, comprised of subject matter experts, process veterans, and the wonkiest of energy policy wonks, advocating for a broad range of perspectives and interests. However, if asked to solve basic arithmetic, such as “what is the answer to one plus one?,” the PJM stakeholder body would undoubtedly spend a significant amount of time to produce multiple answers. Given the open nature of PJM’s stakeholder process, on top of over 1,100 formal PJM members, it can easily become unwieldy to manage and balance these naturally conflicting interests; this only becomes more difficult as the complexity of the issues facing the stakeholder body increases. Left to their own devices, stakeholders cannot and will not achieve consensus, particularly on foundational market and operational issues. This is not a criticism of PJM stakeholders, but a reality of a large and diverse stakeholder body. At the end of the day, market administration and operation is PJM’s responsibility; actions or changes have to be justified by PJM and approved by this Commission. Therefore, active, consistent, and transparent engagement and leadership from PJM, including the Board, is essential to producing tangible outcomes in the stakeholder process.

Throughout the CIFP-LLA process, however, PJM’s position on the various packages or conceptual reforms was not consistently known, nor was there a visible public effort focused on stakeholder alignment on key reforms, or direction from PJM on acceptable (or unacceptable) pathways forward. As an example, in response to 197 pages of stakeholder feedback on the CIFP-LLA scope in August 2025, PJM offered only a high-level 19 page presentation (including no acknowledgement or feedback on Google’s BYONG comments or questions).6 Given the strict time boundaries of the CIFP process, and the volume of proposals on the table, stakeholder meetings felt rushed and disjointed, rather than focused attempts to identify and build consensus. The process was simultaneously substantively open-ended and bound by a rigid timeline, so the outcome was not unexpected. This outcome could have been avoided by the Board and/or PJM

These are relatively easy-to-implement changes that require more affirmative and vocal stances from PJM leadership and the Board, if necessary.

The subsequent CIFP process focused on the RBP and C&M benefitted from more affirmative PJM engagement and detailed proposals, yet suffered a lack of transparency into PJM’s decision-making process, which resulted in last-minute, often significant changes in PJM’s proposals. For example, PJM’s initial RBP proposal included a six-month window for large loads to bilaterally contract for new capacity, a position supported by an overwhelming majority of stakeholders. As Google articulated extensively, BYONG is the best pathway to ensure the costs and risks of new generation required to serve large loads are appropriately borne by the large load, and not passed on to residential consumers, consistent with the principles of the Ratepayer Protection Pledge that Google and six other leading technology companies signed in March 2026. Despite overwhelming support for PJM’s position, in May, PJM abruptly shifted to a September RBP, forgoing the six-month window essential to allow loads to fully explore the BYONG alternative.

As another example, consider PJM’s C&M proposal. Despite significant implementation and jurisdictional questions, which were raised early and often in the CIFP process, PJM pressed forward on a C&M approach that did not provide sufficient detail or clarity. Basic questions, such as the definition of “new load” and how PJM’s proposal would respect existing, executed firm service power contracts negotiated years ago, were either avoided, defined but then changed, or provided with insufficient details. In response, in late June, PJM opted to move away from its C&M proposal in favor of a voluntary, registry-based concept administered largely by the states. While the questions and concerns that ultimately supported PJM’s move had been raised since January, the late shift in position sparked, for good reason, concern among stakeholders, including the states, about implementation roles and responsibilities. It is not clear why PJM waited until so late in the process to adopt such a significant change; concerns could have been avoided or mitigated had PJM shifted stance on C&M earlier in the process, allowing entities appropriate time to respond.

These two examples raise two important points. First, we cannot be simultaneously frustrated with a “broken” stakeholder process and also frustrated with the positions developed in that process when the process is put to work. If PJM had no intent to adopt stakeholders’ positions, particularly on the RBP, it would have been easier and far less time consuming to simply move forward with a preferred approach. Second, significant changes to proposals have significant commercial ramifications. In the case of C&M, an evolving definition of “new load” created continuous uncertainty regarding the amount of capacity new large loads would be expected to 4

procure to avoid curtailment; changing that date meant constantly recalibrating procurement efforts. Stakeholders dedicate a significant amount of time preparing for, and engaging in, PJM stakeholder processes, and informing their respective leadership of the nature of the various proposals to inform commercial activity (e.g., bilateral contracting for new generation). Sudden changes in direction, even if well supported, require time to reevaluate the commercial landscape and adapt commercial activity.

PJM’s stakeholder process is unique, and as evidenced above, certainly not free from challenges. However, like any task, in order for it to be successful, it must be armed with clear directives and a level of confidence that outcomes will be respected, or, in the alternative, the rationale for any deviation by PJM should be clearly articulated and conveyed with sufficient time for the market to respond accordingly. Finally, while every stakeholder wants their position to win the day, that is an unreasonable expectation. The stakeholder process is premised on compromise, and outcomes will undoubtedly leave stakeholders with mixed emotions. However, fear of this should not stand in the way of decisive action.

II.​
Any Governance Reforms Should Be Clear About the Problems They Are Intended
to Address, and How the Proposed Reforms Will Resolve Those Problems.

PJM is understandably facing an unprecedented amount of pressure, including from the President of the United States and the governors of the PJM states. We are in the midst of a period of rapid load growth following decades of stagnation. Extreme weather events are increasing in frequency, and in early July, PJM was one emergency procedure away from deploying backup generators at data center facilities to preserve reliability while serving a record demand of over 168,000 MW.7 The problems facing PJM are real, and they are here now.

Given this, it is even more important that any changes to PJM’s governance structure clearly articulate how those changes will lead to better outcomes for the PJM region. The PJM stakeholder process would, without question, benefit from more consistent engagement from all of the PJM states, including the state commissioners, executives, and legislators. Google is proud to partner with state officials, including in the first CIFP process where the DCC joined a coalition that included the governors of Pennsylvania, Maryland, New Jersey, and Virginia. Indeed, nothing within PJM’s current rules prevents states from engaging and influencing the process today. However, if changes are contemplated that would give states more authority (e.g., Federal Power Act Sec. 205 filing rights or a more formal role in selecting PJM Board members), those changes need to be explicit regarding their ability to address and resolve the identified problems.
