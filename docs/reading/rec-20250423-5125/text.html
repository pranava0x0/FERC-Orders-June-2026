I.​
Background 
Antora’s thermal batteries will provide heat to industrial operations.  Initial deployments 
will focus on industrial process heat up to ~400C, and future deployments will include 
high-temperature processes such as mineral refining, cement, glass, aluminum, and steel.6  The 
technology uses low cost electricity to resistively super-heat carbon blocks that are capable of 
ultra-fast charging and absorption of heat.  The carbon blocks store the energy as heat, at 
temperatures over 2,000◦C, in a compact thermal battery.  The energy is discharged in the form of 
heat at the scale and temperature needed by large industrial operations.  Antora is also 
developing thermal storage products that allow the energy stored as heat to produce electricity, 
thereby serving as electric energy storage devices. 
Like electro-chemical batteries, a significant portion of Antora’s thermal battery value 
comes from flexibility.  The battery is charged during periods of high energy production and low 
demand, thereby helping reduce curtailment of renewable resources and allowing the thermal 
battery to absorb low value/negatively priced electricity.  Because thermal storage can provide 
24/7 heat over a multi-day period by charging only roughly 1/3 of the time, it can fully avoid 
periods of high demand, high-capacity utilization and high electricity pricing.  This allows 
Antora’s industrial and manufacturing clients to gain the benefits of low-cost periods of excess 
generation without requiring the factories themselves to curtail when low cost power is not 
available and during periods of coincident peak transmission system use and demand.  By 
co-locating an Antora thermal battery with a generation resource, Antora can provide factories

Conclusion 
While the Commission’s inquiry into co-location was initiated in response to a discrete 
challenge involving load locating behind-the-meter of large generating facilities, it has 
spotlighted a broader challenge in serving rapidly growing load with an aging grid and outdated 
interconnection processes for load and generation that too frequently result in unacceptable 
delays.  The Commission’s Co-Location Order takes an important step forward in finding that 
the PJM tariff may be unjust and unreasonable in several respects, and the Commission 
appropriately highlights the potential benefits of flexible facilities.   
To its credit, PJM expresses a willingness to take significant steps forward in evolving its
