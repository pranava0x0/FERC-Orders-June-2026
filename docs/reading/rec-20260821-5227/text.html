We are writing to offer comments in response to FERC’s AD-26-07-00 Notice.   

We concur with the states collaborative on the urgent need for governance reform. If anything, we do not believe the reforms are adequate. There is a fundamental conflict of interest inherent in an organization controlled by private for-profit companies creating the rules by which those companies earn their profits.  Indeed, in FERC Order 2000, which laid out the structure for the creation of RTOs, the first of four “Minimum Characteristics of an RTO” - the one FERC called the “bedrock” principle - was “Independence from market participants”.  Any participation of market participants in the governance of PJM is an inherent conflict of interest and should be halted. 

We would, however, like to turn some attention to the effects of this conflict of interest, and in particular to the unreasonableness of the billions of dollars that are being unjustly extracted from ratepayers subject to PJM’s jurisdiction. 

New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherril, on her first day in office, declared a statewide energy emergency due to the PJM price increases.  New Jersey is rushing to react to the crisis by adopting laws and programs to create incentives for the construction of new sources of capacity and enabling the mitigation of new, large loads.  It can be expected that New Jersey will spend billions of dollars creating those new resources.  Most other PJM states are doing the same.  Collectively, I am sure that the PJM states will be spending tens of billions of dollars creating the needed resources over the next few years.  They have taken on the responsibility, and the cost burden, to accomplish what the PJM capacity market exists to do. 

In effect, the states are double paying for the needed resources.  They are paying to develop the actual resources while also paying tens of billions to incumbent generators through the PJM capacity market. 

The first fundamental problem is that none of the ratepayer money being collected through the PJM BRA is paid to new resource construction.  All of the money is instead paid to old, incumbent generation resources, creating windfall profits.  The auctions for 2025/2026 through 2028/2029 comprise a total cost of approximately $62.3 billion.  If the upcoming 2029/2030 auction in December 2026 imposes a cost similar to the last two, the total cost burden will reach over $80 billion.  The loss of $80 billion in ratepayer funds is intolerable when the states must also spend the money to create the new capacity. 

There are a number of severe, fundamental problems with the PJM capacity market design and the process of the BRA, including but not limited to: 

1.  No test for “missing money”  

When supply of capacity tightens, prices in energy markets tend to increase. According to the 2025 State of the Market report by the PJM independent Market Monitor, between 2024 and 2025 there was a 51% increase in the energy segment component of the wholesale cost of power.   

This strongly suggests that, for capacity resources that have paid off their capital costs, there is no “missing money”. The IMM has repeatedly called for the capacity payments to be offset by the amount that energy markets increase. PJM has refused to adopt this recommendation. 

Of note, out of seven RTOs/ISOs, plus WPP, five out of the eight have no capacity market or only a very small residual capacity market.  Only PJM, NYISO, and ISO-NE have full capacity markets.  NYISO and ISO-NE capacity market prices hover around $80 to $110, while PJM is generating prices around $325. 

2. Exaggerated load forecasts 

The capacity subsidy mechanism depends on a forecast of load growth. The exaggeration of this forecast drives prices up. Recent load growth forecasts have been greatly exaggerated.  It ignored the process by which states are taking action to limit the demand burden that can be imposed by  new, large loads.

3. Inappropriate reductions in ELCC  

Inappropriate reductions in ELCC for natural gas, solar, etc., artificially and inappropriately lower the supply of eligible capacity. This drives capacity prices up. Again, relatively small changes in supply or demand can have dramatic effects on the auction price with a single clearing price mechanism, and these changes were not small.  The IMM clearly articulated its opposition to these ELCC reductions in its July 24, 2025 memo (eloquently) titled “IMM Response to PJM’s Memo”. PJM did not adopt the IMM’s recommendation. 

4. Payments for excess reserves 

+ In the 2025/2026 BRA, PJM continued to accept more and much higher bids after 100% of the RR had already been satisfied.  PJM continued accepting bids until 101.7% of the auction target was reached.  1.7% more doesn’t sound like a lot, but with a single clearing price mechanism the effect can be hyperbolic According the IMM’s report on the auction, that extra 1.7% raised the capacity price from $104.69 to $269.92.  There is no real justification for this action other than an intent to produce high prices. 

+ The section above illustrates that the single clearing price mechanism design of the BRA, by its nature, creates extreme volatility, and in the case of an extreme “hockey stick-shaped” bid curve, as exists here, it produces extremely high prices.  Former FERC Chair Mark Christie (while a Commissioner) wrote a paper entitled, “It’s Time to Reconsider Single Clearing Price Mechanisms in U.S. Energy Markets” that was published in the Energy Law Journal.  Commissioner Christie essentially wrote that competitive markets should work with pay-as-bid systems rather than with single clearing price mechanisms.
