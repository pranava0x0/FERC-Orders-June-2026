I. INTRODUCTION
The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC”) September 29, 2026 Orders1 in this case fail to protect consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) from the risks and costs that will result from general acceptance of PJM’s Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) proposal in these dockets. That order allows PJM to implement a one-time capacity auction, with “billion-dollar implications for consumers,”2 to procure generating capacity needed to serve forecasted load associated with large data centers and the resulting resource adequacy shortfall in the 2028/2029 Delivery Year capacity auction. But the RBP auction design subjects consumers who are not causing the need for the auction to shoulder the significant risk of paying for those costs, especially if the forecasted loads do not materialize. FERC’s acceptance of this proposal is inconsistent with the stated understandings of several of the commissioners in their concurring opinions.  For example, Commissioner David A. II. BACKGROUND
FERC generally accepted (subject to refund and paper hearing) PJM’s proposal to establish the RBP, a one-time auction to procure 6,831 MW of new generating capacity and cure the capacity shortage that occurred in the Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) capacity base residual auction for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year.7 Although the September 29 Order generally accepted the RBP proposal subject to refund and paper hearing, it postponed that auction for five months, until February 28, 2027.8 This delay appears intended to give PJM time to modify the proposal to correct certain deficiencies identified by FERC in the Order.9

The overall RBP auction design accepted in the September 29 Order (subject to refund and paper hearing) requires all loads in the transmission zones affected by the capacity shortages from the 2028/2029 Delivery Year RPM auction to participate in the RBP auction.10 FERC’s ruling accepts (a) the must-offer requirement for capacity sellers;11 (b) the $555/MW-day market seller offer cap;12 (c) the pay-as-bid auction clearing mechanism proposal;13 (d) the shortage penalty of 20% of the seller’s cleared bid times the shortage amount;14 (e) the lack of a locational

V. REQUEST FOR REHEARING
A. FERC’s approval of the $555/MW-day seller offer cap and the pay-as-bid clearing mechanism design for the RBP auction fails the test for reasoned decision-making because these decisions are not supported by the evidence and do not provide adequate protections for consumers against seller market power.

FERC failed to provide a reasoned explanation for its acceptance of the $555/MW-day proposed market seller offer cap and the pay-as-bid clearing mechanism for the RBP auction design.  This design will not protect consumers in Ohio or elsewhere in PJM from the exercise of seller market power.  In his Protest to the filing, the Independent Market Monitor for PJM (“IMM”) demonstrates the critical flaw in PJM’s proposal, i.e., that PJM explicitly decided not to use “market power screening or mitigation measures” in the design of the auction parameters.22 As the IMM argues, “[i]t should be unacceptable that PJM, at a time of severe supply shortage, would propose to not use the standard market power screening that is used in all PJM capacity auctions.”23 This is especially concerning given that the overall market design is premised on  an excessive seller offer cap and a pay-as-bid clearing mechanism, neither of which will constrain seller market power in an auction where scarcity is a known condition.

The September 29 Order’s primary reasoning for the offer cap amount is that the $555/MW-day represents the “maximum willingness to pay and process for selecting RBP resources in the RBP auction.”24 But the order points to no evidence justifying that $555/MW- day is the maximum willingness to pay in the RBP auction. C. FERC’s approval of the 20% shortage penalty for the RBP auction fails the test for reasoned decision-making because it is disproportionate to the risk consumers will incur if large load data centers do not materialize, and it is allocated to consumers who did not cause the circumstances leading to the incurrence of the penalty. FERC did not explain why the shortage penalty, set at 20% of the price bid by the seller times the cleared capacity, was appropriate.  PJM proposed that the shortfall charge would “be equal to the product of the RBP Resource’s committed . . . MW shortfall and 20% of the RBP price.”51  In approving this proposal, the September 29 Order states only that the proposed penalty is appropriate because it will work in conjunction with the high pay-as-bid RBP clearing price and the “promise” of up to 15 years of revenue certainty, which “will incentivize developers of RBP Resources to deliver” on their commitment.52  But FERC does not explain how a penalty set at only 20% of the clearing price, without additional restrictions, will encourage developers to meet their commitments.

Under existing RPM rules, the non-performance penalty is set at the clearing price plus 20% of that price.53  Those non-performing resources face additional penalties for not meeting commitments during performance assessment hours. In contrast, FERC accepted a proposal to limit the proposed shortage penalty to only 1/5th of the capacity sell offer, which seriously undervalues the reliance load places on resource adequacy.
