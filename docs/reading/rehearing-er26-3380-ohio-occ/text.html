I. 
INTRODUCTION 
The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC”) September 29, 2026 Orders1 in 
this case fail to protect consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. 
(“PJM”) from the risks and costs that will result from general acceptance of PJM’s Reliability 
Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) proposal in these dockets. That order allows PJM to implement a 
one-time capacity auction, with “billion-dollar implications for consumers,”2 to procure 
generating capacity needed to serve forecasted load associated with large data centers and the 
resulting resource adequacy shortfall in the 2028/2029 Delivery Year capacity auction.   
But the RBP auction design subjects consumers who are not causing the need for the 
auction to shoulder the significant risk of paying for those costs, especially if the forecasted loads 
do not materialize. FERC’s acceptance of this proposal is inconsistent with the stated 
understandings of several of the commissioners in their concurring opinions.  For example, 
Commissioner David A. LaCerte states in his concurrence that the “Reliability Backstop

II. 
BACKGROUND 
FERC generally accepted (subject to refund and paper hearing) PJM’s proposal to 
establish the RBP, a one-time auction to procure 6,831 MW of new generating capacity and cure 
the capacity shortage that occurred in the Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) capacity base 
residual auction for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year.7 Although the September 29 Order generally 
accepted the RBP proposal subject to refund and paper hearing, it postponed that auction for five 
months, until February 28, 2027.8 This delay appears intended to give PJM time to modify the 
proposal to correct certain deficiencies identified by FERC in the Order.9
The overall RBP auction design accepted in the September 29 Order (subject to refund 
and paper hearing) requires all loads in the transmission zones affected by the capacity shortages 
from the 2028/2029 Delivery Year RPM auction to participate in the RBP auction.10 FERC’s 
ruling accepts (a) the must-offer requirement for capacity sellers;11 (b) the $555/MW-day market 
seller offer cap;12 (c) the pay-as-bid auction clearing mechanism proposal;13 (d) the shortage 
penalty of 20% of the seller’s cleared bid times the shortage amount;14 (e) the lack of a locational

A. 
FERC’s approval of the $555/MW-day seller offer cap and the pay-as-bid clearing 
mechanism design for the RBP auction fails the test for reasoned decision-making 
because these decisions are not supported by the evidence and do not provide 
adequate protections for consumers against seller market power.     
FERC failed to provide a reasoned explanation for its acceptance of the $555/MW-day 
proposed market seller offer cap and the pay-as-bid clearing mechanism for the RBP auction 
design.  This design will not protect consumers in Ohio or elsewhere in PJM from the exercise of 
seller market power.  In his Protest to the filing, the Independent Market Monitor for PJM 
(“IMM”) demonstrates the critical flaw in PJM’s proposal, i.e., that PJM explicitly decided 
not to use “market power screening or mitigation measures” in the design of the auction 
parameters.22 As the IMM argues, “[i]t should be unacceptable that PJM, at a time of severe 
supply shortage, would propose to not use the standard market power screening that is used 
in all PJM capacity auctions.”23 This is especially concerning given that the overall market 
design is premised on  an excessive seller offer cap and a pay-as-bid clearing mechanism, 
neither of which will constrain seller market power in an auction where scarcity is a known 
condition.  
The September 29 Order’s primary reasoning for the offer cap amount is that the 
$555/MW-day represents the “maximum willingness to pay and process for selecting RBP 
resources in the RBP auction.”24 But the order points to no evidence justifying that $555/MW-
day is the maximum willingness to pay in the RBP auction. Instead, FERC states that “PJM

Order attempts to distinguish between the RPM capacity auction design and that approved for 
RBP by arguing that pay-as-bid is appropriate when the product to be procured is not uniform.42

three-year forward RPM auctions, new capacity participating in the auctions can consist of both

FERC’s approval of the 20% shortage penalty for the RBP auction fails the test for 
reasoned decision-making because it is disproportionate to the risk consumers will

who did not cause the circumstances leading to the incurrence of the penalty.   
FERC did not explain why the shortage penalty, set at 20% of the price bid by the seller 
times the cleared capacity, was appropriate.  PJM proposed that the shortfall charge would “be 
equal to the product of the RBP Resource’s committed . . . MW shortfall and 20% of the RBP 
price.”51  In approving this proposal, the September 29 Order states only that the proposed 
penalty is appropriate because it will work in conjunction with the high pay-as-bid RBP clearing 
price and the “promise” of up to 15 years of revenue certainty, which “will incentivize 
developers of RBP Resources to deliver” on their commitment.52  But FERC does not explain 
how a penalty set at only 20% of the clearing price, without additional restrictions, will 
encourage developers to meet their commitments.   
Under existing RPM rules, the non-performance penalty is set at the clearing price plus 
20% of that price.53  Those non-performing resources face additional penalties for not meeting 
commitments during performance assessment hours. In contrast, FERC accepted a proposal to 
limit the proposed shortage penalty to only 1/5th of the capacity sell offer, which seriously 
undervalues the reliance load places on resource adequacy.

FERC’s approval of the RBP auction structure violates long-held judicial precedent 
requiring that costs be allocated to those causing the costs to be incurred or to those 
benefitting from them.  It also does not protect consumers from the unjust and 
unreasonable results of RPM auctions held in the past three years that cleared short 
because of large data center load demands.  
FERC never addressed OCC’s main concern that the RBP auction model is 
inappropriate, unjust and unreasonable because it does not target procurement and allocation of 
the resulting auction costs to the large data center loads causing the shortage in the 2028/2029 
and earlier RPM Delivery Years.  PJM and FERC both recognize that the need for the RBP 
auction is the direct result of the sudden addition of more than 6,000 MW of large load data 
centers to the PJM resource adequacy forecasts.  As FERC recognizes in the September 29 
Order, the courts have long required that “Commission-jurisdictional rates and charges must 
‘reflect to some degree the costs actually caused by the customer who must pay for them,’ and 
costs must be allocated in a manner that is at least roughly commensurate with benefits.”64 FERC 
further recognized that “[c]ompliance with this principle is evaluated by ‘comparing the costs 
assessed against a party to the burdens imposed or benefits drawn by that party’.”65
