COMMENTS OF HANS ROYAL
September 3, 2026
1. Introduction and Interest of the Commenter
I am an energy professional and researcher, and spent nearly two decades advising large electricity buyers on electricity procurement before turning to this research full time. I developed the Compute Heat Rate (CHR) framework to answer a practical question: how high can the price of electricity rise before AI data center workloads are no longer profitable? CHR expresses that threshold in dollars per megawatt-hour and reports it by workload type. I published the framework on SSRN in February 2026 (Royal, 2026, doi 10.2139/ssrn.6322318), and I update the underlying index quarterly at computeheatrate.com. PJM discussed CHR at pages 49 through 50 of its May 6, 2026 white paper, Powering Reliability Through Market Design, in connection with demand-side price tolerance.

I am filing these comments on my own behalf as an independent researcher. I publish the index and provide advisory work drawing on this research; I have no client with an interest in this proceeding, and I represent no load, supplier, or other market participant, nor am I asking the Commission to adopt CHR, or any other single metric, as a tariff standard. Instead, my point is narrower: PJM is proposing a framework under which large loads are identified and, through Transmission Owners and Electric Distributors, reduced and compensated during capacity shortages. Two facilities can look the same on an interconnection diagram and have radically different economic responses to interruption, so my recommendation is that the design leave room for that fact.

2. Summary of Position
PJM needs an interim answer to a real resource adequacy problem, and this filing is a reasonable one. I support approval of the Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS), the Large Load Registry, and the Bring Your Own New Capacity (BYONC) provisions. The July 27 Board framework followed a substantial stakeholder process and places the reliability obligation where it belongs: on new load that enters without bringing new supply, not on customers already served by the system.

There is one issue the Commission should keep in view as it evaluates the filing and the work that will follow it, which is that large loads are not economically interchangeable. This shows up in three ways:

First, the cost of interrupting one large load can differ from the cost of interrupting another by well over an order of magnitude. The Q3 2026 CHR index puts the operating electricity threshold for internal, self-hosted AI inference at about $1,200 per MWh and the threshold for frontier inference at about $18,400 per MWh. The consumption-weighted figure is about $9,100 per MWh. A pro rata zonal allocation and one compensation rate wouldn’t adequately reflect the real economics. Second, the filing itself provides evidence of this economic spread. A load that arranges new capacity under the BYONC rules or installs enough backup generation to take its load off the system is paying for firmness. Another load may accept IRAS exposure and the accompanying credit. Those are different choices about the value of uninterrupted service. BYONC and the backup-generation provisions will begin sorting loads on that basis even though the tariff does not describe the process in those terms.

Third, the Registry can capture what PJM learns from those choices. I am not proposing a new mandate for the interim phase. I am asking the Commission to encourage PJM to keep the Registry and IRAS flexible enough to support voluntary reduction preferences and, where states and Electric Distributors choose to use them, economically informed reduction priorities later.

3. Background: What the Compute Heat Rate Measures
CHR starts with the revenue earned by an AI workload and the energy needed to deliver the service. From published inputs and disclosed assumptions, it estimates the electricity price the workload can absorb before continued operation ceases to make economic sense. The index reports two related values. Long-run CHR asks whether it still makes sense to build and operate new capacity for the workload. Dispatch CHR, or CHR-D, asks a different question: after the facility has been built and its capital cost is sunk, at what hourly electricity price would a rational operator stop running? CHR-D is the more relevant value here because curtailment is an operating decision.

The Q3 2026 index was published September 1. It reports a blended long-run CHR of approximately $5,630 per MWh and a blended CHR-D of approximately $9,070 per MWh. Those figures are about 113 and 181 times a conventional gas-fired reference price of $50 per MWh. The long-run CHR declined by nearly 30 percent from Q2, reflecting both market developments, including falling token prices and the commoditization of inference, and methodology updates disclosed with the release. That movement is important: the index tracks the market rather than fixing a number. It did not, however, change the basic result. For the commercial workloads that account for most deployed compute, the rational curtailment threshold remains far above any price PJM's energy market is permitted to produce.

Those numbers require two qualifications. First, neither CHR nor CHR-D says whether a facility can physically reduce load in ten minutes, or by how much. That depends on the operator, the facility, and PJM's operating rules. Second, CHR is not a price forecast. It is an economic ceiling: a measure of economic tolerance, not a prediction of what power will cost.

4. Observations on the Proposal
References below to the 'Board framework' are to PJM's CIFP Framework for Service During Periods of Insufficient Resource Adequacy (July 27, 2026), which the August 13, 2026 filing implements. Where the filed tariff language differs from that framework, the filed language controls.

4.1 Curtailment economics are heterogeneous, and the spread is large
The label "AI data center" hides a very wide range of economics. In the Q3 2026 index, CHR-D runs from approximately $1,227 per MWh for internal and self-hosted inference to approximately $18,378 per MWh for frontier inference, a spread of roughly fifteen times. At the low end, a compensated interruption may be a sensible bargain. At the high end, the lost workload value during the same hour can exceed the credit many times over.

Electric Distributors would compensate compliant IRAS reductions at a single flat hourly rate set by PJM and tied to the rate already used for pre-emergency demand response (Board framework, Credits, item 6). Measured against CHR-D, that credit is likely to cover much of what some workloads lose and a small fraction of what others lose.

4.2 What uniform treatment leaves on the table
If PJM needs a given number of megawatts during an emergency, it can obtain those megawatts from loads with very different interruption costs. The framework allocates reductions across affected zones pro rata and leaves customer-specific implementation to Transmission Owners and Electric Distributors in coordination with states and affected customers, with no economic criterion. That order could cut a workload that would pay almost anything to continue operating while leaving another workload online that would have reduced voluntarily at a much lower price. Reliability sees the same megawatts either way; customers do not bear the same cost. Over time, PJM could reduce that cost by allowing loads to volunteer for earlier reduction or by permitting states and Electric Distributors to use economic exposure when setting reduction order. The single credit rate creates the same mismatch. The credit is below the operating value of most deployed compute as measured by CHR-D. I do not view that as a flaw in the proposed rate. Fully compensating frontier inference would impose an unreasonable cost on other customers, and the Board reasonably concluded that an IRAS reduction happens earlier and under less severe conditions than a full emergency. But the credit should not be mistaken for the value of the curtailed activity. A high-CHR-D load has little economic reason to rely on it and every reason to seek firmness through BYONC or on-site generation. A lower-CHR-D load may find the credit meaningful and may be willing to reduce first.

This is why the economic spread is not merely theoretical. Once implementation begins, the Registry is expected to show some large loads arriving with BYONC or backup generation and others arriving without either. The proposal will produce economic differentiation through those customer choices. It simply does not yet use the resulting information when reductions are assigned.

4.4 The Registry as a foundation
The Board describes the Registry as providing "the critical data transparency needed to establish load reduction priorities for retail customers." It also commits PJM to publish aggregate information where confidentiality permits. I agree with both decisions. The Registry can also hold, on a voluntary basis, the information that states and Electric Distributors would need to set reduction order more intelligently when they "coordinate with the individual customers that will be subject to Interim Resource Adequacy Service" (Participation Details, item 9). PJM does not need to collect detailed economic data in this proceeding. It should, however, avoid designing a schema that forecloses the option. A voluntary field stating a customer's reduction preference, or noting planned participation in Price Responsive Demand or a state economic- priority program, would be enough to preserve a path forward. Adding that capacity now is easier than rebuilding the Registry after it is populated.

5. Recommendations
I ask the Commission to take four steps:
(a) Approve IRAS, the Large Load Registry, and BYONC as interim measures on PJM's requested schedule.

(b) Recognize in its order that pro rata allocation and a uniform credit are interim simplifications. The Commission should encourage PJM and its stakeholders to examine economically differentiated participation and reduction order in the follow-on process. That work could use Price Responsive Demand, state and Electric Distributor programs informed by Registry data, voluntary self-designation, or a combination of those approaches.

(c) Encourage PJM to include voluntary Registry fields that can support that work and to publish enough aggregate detail for independent analysis of how large loads are actually entering the system.

(d) Encourage the use of transparent, published, independently verifiable economic references when states, Electric Distributors, or PJM develop economically differentiated reduction priorities in the follow-on process. Published frameworks can be inspected,

6. Conclusion
PJM has proposed a workable interim response to an urgent problem, and I support approval. The Commission should also be clear about what the interim design leaves unresolved. Large loads that appear similar electrically can differ by an order of magnitude in the value they lose when curtailed. BYONC and backup-generation choices should make part of that difference visible almost immediately. PJM should preserve the ability to use what it learns, instead of hardening a temporary pro rata approach into the long-term answer.
