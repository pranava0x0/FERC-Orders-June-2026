On October 2, 2026, MISO filed proposed revisions to its Open Access Transmission, 
Energy, and Operating Reserve Markets Tariff (“Tariff”) to establish a LARS Process with 
supporting direct testimony prepared by Christina Drake, MISO Director, Generation 
Interconnection.  In the Transmittal Letter, MISO asserts the filing is responsive to the 
Commission’s concerns in its Show Cause Order in Docket No. EL26-70-000, including the 
finding MISO’s existing Tariff may be unjust and unreasonable because it does not provide 
generator interconnection study procedures and services that account for the operational 
dynamics of serving electrically proximate large loads.2   
 
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission found MISO’s Tariff “appears to be unjust and 
unreasonable because it lacks adequate mechanisms to mitigate the risk of cost shifting among 
transmission customers, which may result in unjust and unreasonable rates for transmission 
service.”3  In the ruling, the Commission expressed concern about the lack of transparency 
regarding the assignment of costs, including Network Upgrades, necessary to provide 
transmission service to Eligible Customers or Large Load Customers on behalf of Large Loads. 
The Commission sought to ensure Eligible Customers bear the risk and are ultimately 
responsible for costs incurred to provide transmission service, including the cost of Network 
Upgrades.4

I. 
The Commission Should Not Allow the Grandfathering of Approximately 30 
Gigawatts of Load   
 
MISO is proposing to fully exempt or “grandfather” any Large Load with a service 
commencement date on or prior to December 4, 2026, from all requirements of proposed Section 
37A, or more generally, the Large Load Interconnection Reliability Requirements.5  MISO is 
also proposing to partially exempt a Large Load or a portion of a Large Load that does not have a 
Service Commencement Date prior to December 4, 2026, but that meets the MTEP/EPR 
Condition and the ramp and ride-through requirements set forth in Section 37A.6 through 
37A.7.6  In total, MISO is proposing to fully or partially exempt approximately 30 gigawatts of 
existing load from the Large Load Interconnection Reliability Requirements.7  The Joint 
Protestors filed a Protest in Docket No. ER26-3650-000 on September 18, 2026, protesting 
MISO’s proposed grandfathering exemptions.8  The Joint Protestors maintain the proposed 
grandfathering exemptions under MISO’s Section 205 filings, including the LARS Process, are 
not just and reasonable.  If the grandfathering exemptions are carried forward in this filing and to 
the series of future Federal Power Act Section 205 filings MISO intends to make in response to 
the Show Cause Order, the exemptions could deprive electric consumers of the cost-shifting and 
other consumer protections MISO has been directed to address in the Show Cause Order.

II. 
Cost-Shifting Should be Addressed Across MISO’s Section 205 Filings and in the 
Section 206 Filing 
 
 
MISO’s LARS Process does not address the Commission’s concerns regarding cost-
shifting expressed in the Show Cause Order.  In her direct testimony, MISO Witness Drake notes 
“[t]he cost shifting issues raised in the Commission’s Show Cause Order will be addressed in 
future filings that MISO plans to submit by November 16.”9  The Commission’s concerns 
regarding cost-shifting are an integral part of the Show-Cause Order.  If the Commission 
approves MISO’s proposed Tariff changes in MISO’s Section 205 filings without considering 
cost-shifting and the collective impact of all of MISO’s Section 205 filings as a whole, electric 
customers may be deprived of the cost-shifting and other consumer protections the Commission 
directed MISO to address in the Show Cause Order.  The Commission should require MISO to 
address cost-shifting in all of its Section 205 filings and in all of its direct responses to the 
Section 206 Show Cause Order.  The Joint Protestors should be permitted to address the cost-
shifting impacts, transparency issues, and other consumer protection issues raised in MISO’s 
Section 205 filings as a whole, collectively, in the Section 206 Show Cause Docket.10

III. 
The LARS Process Lacks Transparency on Costs and Allows Cost-Shifting of 
Network Upgrades 
 
 
MISO proposes to add a new GIP Section 3.12.4 to describe the studies that will take 
place for LARS generation projects.11  With respect to mitigations or network upgrades to 
address violations observed during the studies, MISO proposes the following language: 
Mitigations developed to address violations observed in only the transmission 
studies shall be assigned Baseline Reliability Projects (BRP) or Other project 
types.  Mitigations developed to address common violations observed in both the 
transmission study and the generation study shall be assigned Baseline Reliability 
Projects (BRP) or Other project types.  Mitigations developed to address 
violations observed only in the generation study shall be assigned to the LARS 
Interconnection Customer under the Generator Interconnection Project (GIP) 
project type.12 
 
In the Transmittal Letter, MISO explains the above language: 
addresses how mitigations developed to address violations will be assigned 
depending on where they were observed.  If they were only observed in the load 
side study of LARS, then they will be assigned BRPs and Other project types.  If 
mitigations were developed to address violations observed in both the generation 
and load studies, then they will be assigned BRPs and Other project types.  And 
finally, any mitigations developed to address violations observed that were caused 
only in the generation study will be assigned to the Interconnection Customer 
under the Generator Interconnection Project (“GIP”) project type.13

V.  
MISO Fails to Explain How LARS is Not a Queue-Jumping Proposal 
 
The Joint Protestors also take issue with MISO’s classification of the LARS proposal and 
its intent to build off the success of the Expedited Resource Addition Study (“ERAS”) and the 
Zero-Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement (“ZGIA”) process, the latter of which is 
still pending at the Commission. The ERAS pathway allowed Generator Interconnection 
customers who have approval from their Retail Electric Rate Regulatory Authority (“RERRA”) 
to seek an expedited pathway to a Generator Interconnection Agreement (“GIA”), circumventing 
the traditional DPP queue. The Joint Protestors are concerned that LARS, similar to ERAS, will 
enable queue jumping. MISO Witness Drake indicates that “MISO understands the concerns that 
LARS is enabling queue jumping,” but does not believe that the LARS proposal contemplates 
queue jumping and maintains a size cap that prevents misuse of the process.26  
MISO Witness Drake discusses the likely success of the LARS process from a planning 
standpoint, and how there could be strategic efficiencies between generator interconnection and 
load interconnection processes, “If MISO can identify a common solution that 
helps integrate both the load and generation into the system, then MISO can maximize the 
benefits of both processes and reduce integration costs.”27 The LARS study process only 
evaluates generator projects with executed GIAs. Any projects currently moving through the

MISO’s testimony supposes that queue jumping only becomes a problem when the 
jumping is unrestricted and constant. Under FPA Section 205, MISO bears the burden of proof 
that LARS is just and reasonable, and not unduly preferential. MISO has not adequately 
explained how LARS generation projects are not receiving undue preference over other 
generation projects in the DPP cycles ahead of them. The Show Cause Order explicitly asks 
MISO to show, in their FPA Section 205 filing, how they will “avoid disrupting existing 
commercial arrangements.”29 MISO has not shown how the LARS filing will avoid such 
disruptions to the existing DPP queue, and will avoid being unjust and unreasonable, or unduly 
discriminatory to Generator Interconnection Customers currently in the queue.
