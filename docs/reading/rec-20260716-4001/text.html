PJM also could facilitate more effective engagement by the states by being explicit 
regarding the input that it seeks from states and the forum(s) in which that input is to be provided.  
Whether accurate or not, there is a general perception that PJM leadership does not take input from 
the states seriously.  PJM can correct that perception by identifying in a structured way the specific 
input that it desires from states, where and when that input is to be provided, and then 
communicating back to the states how that information was considered by PJM to reach decisions.  
Constellation recommends that PJM do this for the following topics at a minimum: 
• Proposed changes to market rules and planning processes; 
• Jurisdictional authority of PJM relative to the states; 
• Load forecasting, including obtaining input from state retail regulatory authorities 
on what large load requests are sufficiently real to be included in PJM’s capacity 
procurement and transmission planning; 
• Resource adequacy preferences, including whether the state intends to rely on PJM 
markets for capacity procurement or to establish its own planning and procurement 
mechanism(s) and whether the state prefers certain capacity types and, if so, how 
those resources should be procured;10 and 
• Views on the relative importance of rule or process changes so PJM can factor that 
into its workload prioritization.

This increased collaboration also should go both ways.  There are a number of market and 
resource adequacy-related matters governed by state laws and regulations that are impacted by 
PJM decisions or otherwise interact with the wholesale markets and planning functions overseen 
by PJM.  PJM should work with the states to identify the areas of support they need to fulfill their

to be specific as to the information or input to be provided and the forum(s) in which that

C. PJM’s Role: Using its Stakeholder Processes and Governance Mechanisms to 
Address System Needs in a Timely and Efficient Manner 
In Constellation’s experience, many of the complaints about PJM’s stakeholder process 
and its governance mechanisms are, in actuality, disagreements with PJM about its decisions to 
take, defer, or decline a particular action combined with a perception that competing views have 
not been adequately considered.  The Commission’s goal cannot be for PJM to always agree or 
take unwavering direction from a particular entity or group of entities.  That would be inconsistent 
with the core independence principle of Order No. 2000, which the Commission enforces in order 
to ensure that each RTO has “a decisionmaking process that is independent of control by any 
market participant or class of participants.”18  The same is true of states, which should participate 
extensively in RTO processes and, as discussed above, have the resources and organizational 
structures to do so.  While improvements are needed in PJM to ensure that the views of states (as 
well as other stakeholders) are fully considered, it does not follow that states need or should be 
given filing rights under section 205 of the FPA or decisional control over PJM leadership.   
1. PJM Leadership Matters Most 
Whether an issue is raised for consideration by a stakeholder or PJM, PJM sets the agenda 
for its standing committees and task forces, where most of the substantive work is done.  It is 
PJM’s responsibility, then, to use its stakeholder process to drive to decisions instead of delay. 
Stakeholders look to PJM to determine when an issue has been fully vetted, opportunities for 
collaboration or compromise have been exhausted, and it is time for PJM or stakeholders to move

When PJM strikes the balance right, working collaboratively with stakeholders, it can 
successfully and timely address some of the system’s most pressing needs.  For example, the

2. Who Has Section 205 Filing Rights Is a Red Herring 
Some nevertheless argue that PJM’s section 205 rights should be expanded, pointing to a 
lack of Member endorsement leading to gridlock.  As discussed above, PJM already has section 
205 filing rights over resource adequacy, generator retirements and replacements, and capacity 
market rule changes which have been the most contentious issues in PJM particularly for the states.  
Dissatisfaction over PJM’s actions and inactions on these issues has more to do with how PJM is 
addressing (or not) feedback received than who has the right to propose a rule change under 
section 205.  And while it is true that PJM needs Member endorsement to file an energy market 
rule change under section 205, the lack of stakeholder approval has not stopped PJM in the past 
from proceeding with filings under section 206 when its leadership concluded that a change was 
necessary despite stakeholders not reaching the consensus necessary for PJM to file under section 
205.20 
Others argue that states should be given section 205 filing rights, but that would be 
inconsistent with the very structure of the Federal Power Act.  Utilities providing jurisdictional 
service have filing rights, and the Commission reviews proposed changes to ensure they are just 
and reasonable.  The same is true for state regulation of utilities.  If either the Commission or a 
state commission desires to change a tariff or market rule, it can initiate its own proceeding to 
investigate if that change is needed.  It would be unreasonable to give states more authority over

If the Commission nonetheless believes that something has changed that now renders 
PJM’s existing filing rights over market rules unjust, unreasonable and unduly preferential, then it 
is generators who should receive additional authority to expedite section 205 filings proposing 
changes to those rules.  Generators are the only entities providing capacity, energy, and related 
jurisdictional services under the FPA.  To the extent generators ceded any of their rights under 
section 205 in agreeing to participate in PJM, they cannot be forced to further cede those rights 
through other changes to the filing rights structure in PJM.22   
3. Board Member Selection Must Remain Independent 
Finally, it is essential that PJM’s Board member selection process remain independent of 
undue influence from any particular interest.23  PJM’s existing selection process is consistent with 
Commission precedent and welcomes input, including potential nominees, from all interested 
parties.24  This is a healthy process that is fair to all Members and is consistent with approaches 
used in other regions.

Some states in PJM have requested a more formal role in Board member selections.  Giving 
any group, whether states or anyone else, an outsized role in Board member selection could 
undermine the independence of the Board and PJM as an organization.  Instead, states can and 
should make their views known about potential Board members by taking advantage of existing 
opportunities to engage.  This could include coordinating efforts by developing a list of suggested 
nominees, perhaps through OPSI or through the formation of nomination advisory group 
consisting of state-designated representatives.   
Relatedly, it is important that the Board is immune from political pressure and has all the 
information it needs in making decisions.  To ensure Board independence is maintained, PJM 
stakeholders and the Commission should evaluate whether Board terms should be lengthened, and 
whether substantive criteria should be adopted for removing Board members, to promote well-
informed Board decisions that are immune from political influence.  In addition, to support the 
Board’s consideration of complicated policy decisions, the Board should be given access to input 
from third-party market experts when complex technical matters are being considered.25
