II. ANSWER
The Proposal exceeds the Commission’s jurisdiction.  The Proposal also unlawfully discriminates based on load size, load date, and resource vintage, and its design is unjust and unreasonable in numerous respects.  In arguing otherwise, the Answers misunderstand the applicable law and conveniently ignore key features of the proposed tariff.  They also incorrectly suggest that the Proposal is the only way to address issues raised by new large loads and that the Ratepayer Protection Pledge somehow justifies violating limits set by the FPA—neither of which is true.

A. The Proposal Is Outside The Scope Of The Commission’s Jurisdiction
1. The Answers Incorrectly Contend That Judicial Decisions Support The
View That The Commission Has Jurisdiction Whenever An Action
Affects The Wholesale Market

The Answers are wrong about what courts have held in defining the scope of the
Commission’s jurisdiction.  The Answers’ overall import is that any effect on the wholesale market is enough to make the Proposal jurisdictionally acceptable—a proposition that the Supreme Court has soundly rejected.

First, addressing Constellation’s “targeting” argument and relying heavily on FERC v. EPSA, 577 U.S. 260 (2016), PJM’s Answer contends that the question of whether an action crosses the jurisdictional line is all about the intent behind that action, defined at a high level of generality.5  That is self-evidently incorrect.  As EPSA explains, the Commission could not “issue[] a regulation compelling every consumer to buy a certain amount of electricity on the retail market,” even though “[s]uch a rule would necessarily determine the load purchased on the wholesale market too, and thus would alter wholesale prices”6—regardless of whether (in PJM’s words) the “justification for” that

regulation was to benefit the wholesale market in some way.7  Were the law otherwise, the statutorily mandated limits on the Commission’s jurisdiction would essentially cease to exist, because—given the connection between the wholesale and retail markets—it would be easy to claim that almost any retail-market-targeted action was ultimately intended to have some wholesale-market effect.

In reality, the “targeting” question—which is just one of multiple relevant factors in assessing jurisdiction, all of which point against asserting jurisdiction here8—is a more practical one.  It asks whether “the things [the] regulation is directed at, the control of rates and facilities … , are precisely the things over which FERC has comprehensive authority.”9  In EPSA, the answer was yes—not because it was possible to hypothesize some downstream wholesale-market related justification, but because the action in question addressed only transactions on the wholesale market and the justification for the action matched its target.10  Here, the answer is plainly no.  The Proposal is directed at New Large Loads’ demand for power purchased at retail and seeks to control and regulate the terms of retail sales to those loads, including through the cudgel of curtailment.  The fact that the Proposal’s ultimate goal may be to affect the wholesale market is of no moment, as EPSA makes clear:  the Commission “cannot take an action transgressing th[e] limit” on intruding on the retail sphere “no matter how direct[] or dramatic” that action’s “impact on wholesale rates” may be.11

C. PJM’s Proposal Should Be Rejected Because Its Design Is Unjust and
Unreasonable
1. PJM Unreasonably Proposes to Exclude New Large Load Without
BYONC From the Demand Curve, Which Would Distort Market
Dynamics And Worsen Resource Constraints
PJM’s proposal would unreasonably remove New Large Loads that are subject to IRAS from the VRR Curve used to clear RPM Auctions.85  None of the purported justifications offered in the Answers overcomes the unreasonableness of that aspect of the Proposal.

First, according to PJM’s Answer, its demand curve approach is justified by the Pledge.  But the Pledge is a policy statement that does not suggest that PJM’s IRAS Proposal is the only possible approach that can be taken here, let alone that the Proposal is legally sound or is consistent with other policies supporting new load development and infrastructure to serve that new load.86 Second, the Answers contend that New Large Loads that do not bring their own new capacity should be excluded from the VRR Curve because they are “effectively electing not to receive capacity service.”87  But that justification makes no sense, because under the Proposal New Large Loads without BYONC would be excluded even in delivery years when IRAS curtailment is not triggered.88  Similarly, in any period in which no such curtailment is taking place, the excluded load will be physically present and served, yet the VRR Curve will pretend that it does not exist.  The categorical exclusion of certain New Large Loads from the VRR Curve thus is inconsistent with the realities of the market, which explains why it will undermine RPM’s structural integrity and unjustifiably mute market signals, ultimately worsening resource constraints.89

D. The Answers’ Suggestion That The Proposal Is the Only Solution To The
Challenges Presented Is Wrong, And The Commission Should Not Be Swayed
By It

Threaded through the Answers is a repeated insistence that, whatever flaws the Proposal might have, the Commission should accept it because it is the only possible solution to an overwhelming problem that needs to be solved this very instant.104  That is a false construct. Although the Answers point to the Ratepayer Protection Pledge and the Statement of Principles (issued by the White House and Governors) as support for the IRAS imperative, those documents contemplate solutions and responsive options for data-center demand.105  They do not establish that PJM’s version of IRAS is a necessary, reasonable, or lawful implementation of public policy.  Nor do they override other policy statements that emphasize the need to support “vast AI infrastructure and the energy to power it.”106

Even more fundamentally, PJM’s Proposal violates the statutory limits on the Commission’s jurisdiction—regardless of PJM’s policy motivations.  Congress is empowered to decide how to divide up responsibility for such problem-solving between the federal government and the States, and the Commission cannot step in just because industry participants—or even States themselves— believe that Commission action would be a more convenient or superior way of addressing a particular issue.
