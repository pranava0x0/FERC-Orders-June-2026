I. INTRODUCTION

The electric industry is now well into its second year of intense focus on large loads co- locating with wholesale generators. The Commission has numerous dockets, open and closed, on the subject and has held an all-day technical conference where it heard detailed testimony from industry experts on all sides of the question. Although the technical, jurisdictional, and economic issues are complex, the Commission can best proceed if it follows two hopefully non-controver- sial “North Star” principles:

(1) Protect the safety and reliability of the bulk electric system; and
(2) Prevent subsidization of new loads by network customers.
Guided by these principles, and utilizing the extensive records before it, the Commission can take action to clarify its rules around behind-the-meter loads at regulated wholesale generators.

In its Answer, PJM brings its experience—developed over several years of grappling with these issues—to the myriad questions presented by co-location. PJM recognizes that there are limits to its ability to stop behind-the-meter load from interconnecting to the wholesale gen- erators interconnected with the grid. As a federally regulated public utility, PJM has no jurisdic- tion over these retail questions. In several PJM states, state law allows such behind-the-meter re- tail load. Instead, PJM focuses on what it can control: its own Tariff. Specifically, PJM defends the justness and reasonableness of the Tariff, but it recognizes that the rules of the road can be unclear in certain cases. It outlines eight potential Options for large loads co-located with whole- sale generators. Four of these are already permitted by the Tariff (but may still benefit from

As an initial matter, the PJM Answer accurately pinpoints the potential jurisdictional gaps that led to the situation at Susquehanna.18 Under prevailing Pennsylvania law, a single gen- erator providing service to a single customer is not a public utility and is not subject to the retail jurisdiction of the Pennsylvania Public Utilities Commission.19 As this Commission is undoubt- edly aware, public utilities throughout the country have a long history of vigorously defending their state franchises to provide retail service. However, in this case, PPL Electric was not con- sulted or given the opportunity to serve what soon became (by a large margin) the single largest retail customer in its service territory.20 Instead, that behind-the-meter service was simply estab- lished by private contract. Sovereign states can allow, explicitly or implicitly, behind-the-meter load at generators. However, as PJM points out, the Commission retains jurisdiction over the transmission system and the interstate energy markets.21 While it cannot (or at least should not) infringe on the states’ jurisdiction over the behind-the-meter load itself, the Commission abso- lutely can take action to ensure its North Star principles that such facilities do not (1) impact the safety or reliability of the bulk electric system, and (2) do not create conditions for improper sub- sidization by network customers. 2. Options 4, 5

PJM’s Options 4 and 5 reflect a good faith attempt to accommodate a behind-the-meter load at an existing wholesale generator. PJM considers them “less preferred,” but recognizes that where retail load interconnects and is metered is a question of state law, beyond the ability of the local utility, regional transmission organization, or FERC to control.23 Specifically, these options reflect lessons learned through the Susquehanna ISA negotiations. Incorporating Options 4 and potentially 5 into the PJM Tariff would largely formalize the ad-hoc structure at Susquehanna without the need to reopen the most controversial portions of the Susquehanna ISA (although certain protections and physical interconnection changes may still need to be incorporated into that agreement). Making these tariff changes with no negative impact on reliability or cost subsi- dization will be difficult though, as behind-the-meter load is fundamentally less visible and less interactive with the grid than “normal” front-of-meter load.24 PPL Electric thus agrees that these options are not the preferred way forward, but may still be necessary where state law requires ac- commodation of behind-the-meter load.25

3. Options 6–8
PJM’s other Options are less controversial, but are also less directly relevant to the ques- tions raised regarding how the PJM Tariff should address co-located load behind-the-meter of a large wholesale generator. All three remaining Options instead primarily focus on the larger is- sue of resource adequacy. As PPL Electric explained in its Post-Technical Conference Com- ments, data center growth is critical for economic development and will require new generation and market adjustments.41

Option 6 would incentivize data centers to “Bring Your Own Generation” by speeding the interconnection process for those new generators if they serve as capacity resources. B. Responses to PSEG

PSE&G argues that the Commission should apply cost allocation rules developed in dis- putes between different transmission zones to the newly presented question of how transmission costs should be allocated among retail customers in a single transmission zone.43 It argues such a rule would require transmission charges “be assessed based on a party’s ‘actual use’ of the trans- mission system.44 This would trample on state jurisdiction over retail rates, which governs allo- cation of transmission costs to retail customers. It would require FERC to recognize a new class of retail customer and then direct the states how to assign transmission costs (assessed to the transmission zone via Schedule 12 of the PJM Tariff) among these retail rate classes. PSE&G’s proposal would also violate a longstanding tenet of FERC law, which “consistently reject[s] cost causation arguments made by network customers that [seek] to lower their load ratio share based on actual use of the transmission system.”45 The Commission should reject this argument.
