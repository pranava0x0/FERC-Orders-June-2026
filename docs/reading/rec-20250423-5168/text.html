I. 
INTRODUCTION 
 
The electric industry is now well into its second year of intense focus on large loads co-
locating with wholesale generators. The Commission has numerous dockets, open and closed, on 
the subject and has held an all-day technical conference where it heard detailed testimony from 
industry experts on all sides of the question. Although the technical, jurisdictional, and economic 
issues are complex, the Commission can best proceed if it follows two hopefully non-controver-
sial “North Star” principles:  
(1) 
Protect the safety and reliability of the bulk electric system; and  
(2) 
Prevent subsidization of new loads by network customers.   
Guided by these principles, and utilizing the extensive records before it, the Commission can 
take action to clarify its rules around behind-the-meter loads at regulated wholesale generators.  
 
In its Answer, PJM brings its experience—developed over several years of grappling 
with these issues—to the myriad questions presented by co-location. PJM recognizes that there 
are limits to its ability to stop behind-the-meter load from interconnecting to the wholesale gen-
erators interconnected with the grid. As a federally regulated public utility, PJM has no jurisdic-
tion over these retail questions. In several PJM states, state law allows such behind-the-meter re-
tail load. Instead, PJM focuses on what it can control: its own Tariff. Specifically, PJM defends 
the justness and reasonableness of the Tariff, but it recognizes that the rules of the road can be 
unclear in certain cases. It outlines eight potential Options for large loads co-located with whole-
sale generators. Four of these are already permitted by the Tariff (but may still benefit from

§ 205 authority. PPL Electric believes that these rules can, and should, accommodate behind-the-

As an initial matter, the PJM Answer accurately pinpoints the potential jurisdictional 
gaps that led to the situation at Susquehanna.18 Under prevailing Pennsylvania law, a single gen-
erator providing service to a single customer is not a public utility and is not subject to the retail 
jurisdiction of the Pennsylvania Public Utilities Commission.19 As this Commission is undoubt-
edly aware, public utilities throughout the country have a long history of vigorously defending 
their state franchises to provide retail service. However, in this case, PPL Electric was not con-
sulted or given the opportunity to serve what soon became (by a large margin) the single largest 
retail customer in its service territory.20 Instead, that behind-the-meter service was simply estab-
lished by private contract. Sovereign states can allow, explicitly or implicitly, behind-the-meter 
load at generators. However, as PJM points out, the Commission retains jurisdiction over the 
transmission system and the interstate energy markets.21 While it cannot (or at least should not) 
infringe on the states’ jurisdiction over the behind-the-meter load itself, the Commission abso-
lutely can take action to ensure its North Star principles that such facilities do not (1) impact the 
safety or reliability of the bulk electric system, and (2) do not create conditions for improper sub-
sidization by network customers. PJM and PPL Electric sought to use the limited tools available

PJM’s Options 4 and 5 reflect a good faith attempt to accommodate a behind-the-meter

interactive with the grid than “normal” front-of-meter load.24 PPL Electric thus agrees that these 
options are not the preferred way forward, but may still be necessary where state law requires ac-
commodation of behind-the-meter load.25

B. 
Responses to PSEG  
 
PSE&G argues that the Commission should apply cost allocation rules developed in dis-
putes between different transmission zones to the newly presented question of how transmission 
costs should be allocated among retail customers in a single transmission zone.43 It argues such a 
rule would require transmission charges “be assessed based on a party’s ‘actual use’ of the trans-
mission system.44 This would trample on state jurisdiction over retail rates, which governs allo-
cation of transmission costs to retail customers. It would require FERC to recognize a new class 
of retail customer and then direct the states how to assign transmission costs (assessed to the 
transmission zone via Schedule 12 of the PJM Tariff) among these retail rate classes. PSE&G’s 
proposal would also violate a longstanding tenet of FERC law, which “consistently reject[s] cost 
causation arguments made by network customers that [seek] to lower their load ratio share based 
on actual use of the transmission system.”45 The Commission should reject this argument.
