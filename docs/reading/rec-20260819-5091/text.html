 August 19, 2026 
 
 
Third Act Maryland Comments 
on 
PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms  
Docket No. AD26-7-000 
 
Dear Sir or Madam, 
​
I am writing on behalf of Third Act Maryland, a grassroots organization of citizens 
age 60 and over, to submit comments in the above referenced docket.  Our organization 
represents over 2,000 individuals from across multiple counties in Maryland on issues 
related to strengthening of democratic practice, climate change and clean energy 
promotion. 
​
We have been dismayed for years at PJM’s failure to bring technically sound, 
financially viable clean power (wind, solar, battery storage) projects onto its grid in a 
timely fashion.  The RTO’s delays in this regard have led to a widely recognized and 
justly criticized reliability crisis, evidenced by its failure to achieve its own reliability 
margins over two consecutive capacity auctions.  PJM has defended its opposition to 
clean power through specious technical and reliability arguments, which increasingly fail 
to stand up to scrutiny.  Its practices also have led to a scenario of high and rising 
electricity prices, which exacerbate the country’s inflationary pressures. 
The core problem is PJM’s governance, which overwhelmingly projects the 
interests of its oil, gas and coal company members, to the detriment of ratepayers and 
the general public.  PJM’s governance – who gets to vote, how they vote and the 
transparency of its votes – must change to more accurately reflect its role as a provider 
of a key public good, whose manner of provision has wide-ranging effects on public 
health and welfare.   
In that context, we are pleased to submit the following recommendations on 
changes urgently needed in PJM’s governance structures and process: 
1.​ Reflect affordability and the public interest in PJM’s mission and governing 
documents. Decisions made at PJM impact 67 million ratepayers across 13 states 
and the District of Columbia. Yet PJM has no formal commitment to serve the public 
interest or to consider the affordability of electricity on the same footing as reliability. 
The PJM Board should have its independence and power expanded – as suggested at 
the conference – but this should happen only if meaningful steps are taken to commit 
PJM to serve the public, and not just its members.  Specifically, PJM’s mission and 
governing documents must be rewritten to explicitly include a public interest mandate 
and concern about affordability. 
2.​ Grant OPSI 205 jump ball filing rights. Currently, PJM member states – much less 
consumer advocates – do not have a strong enough voice in PJM’s decision-making 
process. One way to strengthen the voice of the states and of consumer advocates is

to grant the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (OPSI) 205 “jump ball” filing rights with 
the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), so that they can advance 
proposals that will be considered on an even footing with those from PJM. This is 
already the case for other regional transmission organizations (RTOs), and PJM must 
be brought into alignment with this standard. Additionally, 205 filing rights for OPSI can 
serve as an oversight mechanism for a public interest mandate, as they will give state 
authorities and public advocates a formal route to intervene. 
3.​ Formalize Board consultation with OPSI. Under the status quo, the PJM Board 
does not sufficiently consult OPSI, its member states or consumer advocates prior to 
making major filings with FERC. Poor communication increases the likelihood that the 
states and the PJM Board will be at odds on proposals, especially those that would 
increase costs substantially for ratepayers. While granting OPSI 205 filing rights 
provides a backstop, increased consultation between the PJM Board, representatives 
of its member states and consumer advocates – such as getting feedback on draft 
filings – would foster trust, build credibility, and avoid litigation. 
4.​ Require cost impact information. To evaluate whether PJM is serving the public 
interest on the basis of affordability, it is necessary for states, advocates, stakeholders, 
and the public to have access to accurate cost impact information. PJM should 
analyze all proposals for cost impact and make the results widely and publicly 
available so that interested parties can make informed decisions. Such transparency 
will also serve to improve PJM’s accountability to the public.  
5.​ Expanding the Mandate of the Independent Market Monitor. For over 25 years, an 
Independent Market Monitor has provided indispensable oversight of PJM’s power 
markets, performing cost impact analysis and identifying structural problems. No less 
than the energy and capacity markets, transmission planning at PJM has a significant 
impact on ratepayer costs. The Market Monitor must expand his report to include an 
analysis of transmission costs at all levels of the interstate markets and to review all 
cost assignment and cost allocation rules to ensure that the rules are in the public 
interest. 
6.​ Expand the Nominating Committee to include state representatives. If the PJM 
Board is to better engage with the states, it would help to have more formalized 
involvement of the states in the nominating process for Board directors. Currently, the 
Nominating Committee does not consistently include a consumer advocate, and the 
states do not get to appoint representatives. Along with reserving a permanent seat for 
a consumer advocate, the Nominating Committee should also allow for one appointee 
from OPSI and another from a governors’/legislators’ organization – as recommended 
by the Governors’ Collaborative. 
7.​ Expand the Board expertise requirements to include state and ratepayer 
experience. Expertise requirements are an important guardrail to ensure the PJM 
Board has the collective experience and insight necessary to do its job effectively. 
However, the current requirements do not include expertise in state regulatory work, 
consumer advocacy, or clean energy. To have a Board that is equipped to understand 
how their decisions impact the affordability of electricity for ratepayers, it is critical to 
require that at least one Board member brings direct experience from each of these

areas. Moreover, expanding requirements in this way would better equip PJM to 
adhere to a public interest and affordability mandate in its governing documents. 
8.​ Open Board meetings and publish Board votes. Perhaps the most straightforward 
reforms PJM can choose to make would be to open all Board meetings to the public 
and publish the results of individual Board votes. By increasing the transparency of 
Board proceedings, PJM can concretely demonstrate accountability to its mission – 
which should include an explicit public interest and affordability mandate – rather than 
merely asking PJM members and the public to trust the process. Moreover, with 
published votes, all interested parties will be equipped to evaluate the performance of 
individual Board directors. 
9.​ Expand sector-weighted voting to all Standing Committees and Senior Task 
Forces. At higher committee levels in PJM the practice of sector-weighted voting 
helps ensure that no individual sector overwhelms the others. However, this voting 
method is not employed in lower-level standing committees and senior task forces, 
which leads to outsize influence by supply-side interests and their many affiliate 
members. Applying sector-weighted voting at all committee levels would ensure that 
the voices of end-use customers, including representatives of residential ratepayer 
interests, are not drowned out. 
10.​Require transparency of individual firm votes across all committee levels. In a 
body like PJM, whose decisions ultimately impact the affordability of electricity for 67 
million people, there is no justification for keeping the votes of PJM members under 
wraps. Just as votes by the Board should be made public, votes at all committee 
levels of PJM should be transparent and accessible to oversight. If states are to 
conduct meaningful oversight of the utilities within their jurisdiction, PJM should not 
serve as an obstacle to that function by allowing member votes to remain obscured. In 
the same spirit of transparency, all PJM meetings should be recorded and made 
publicly available. 
We very much appreciate the FERC’s initiative on reviewing PJM governance issues and we 
look forward to seeing the institution of the urgently needed changes described above. 
 
Very respectfully, 
Christine Pendzich 
Third Act Maryland  
Steering Committee member
