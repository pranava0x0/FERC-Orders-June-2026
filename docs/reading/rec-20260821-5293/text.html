I. INTRODUCTION

PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) submitted to the Commission on July 31, 2026, revisions to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff and the Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities to establish the Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) (the “RBP Proposal”).1  In submitting the RBP Proposal, PJM seeks the Commission’s authorization to (i) “procure new capacity resources through a backstop auction”; (ii) commence the backstop auction no later than September 2026; (iii) provide price certainty for new capacity resources for fifteen years; and (iv) allocate the costs associated with such new capacity resources to Load Serving Entities (“LSEs”) with new data center load that have not self-procured new capacity or agreed to be curtailable.2

The RBP Proposal purports to “address the influx of large loads while maintaining resource adequacy, reliability, and affordability in a manner that respects the divide between state and

First, the RBP Proposal as drafted would disrupt existing agreements and fails to recognize bona fide bilateral capacity agreements executed before PJM filed the RBP Proposal, including agreements supported by existing capacity. Third, the RBP Proposal does not provide RBP resources or new resources contractually committed to serve large-load customers with a reasonable path to bring new capacity online.  The Commission should therefore require PJM to amend the RBP Proposal to address these shortcomings so that the RBP (i) equitably allocates just and reasonable costs of capacity to new large-load customers that benefit from it, and (ii) facilitates the expeditious interconnection of new capacity resources needed to address PJM’s reliability shortfall.

III. COMMENTS
A. Large-load customers that procured capacity before PJM submitted the RBP
Proposal should not be required to pay for duplicative capacity through the
backstop procurement.

Under the RBP Proposal, large-load customers that do not procure their own capacity via bilateral contracts must pay capacity costs in the RBP backstop auction.  This framework ignores the fact that some large-load customers like Amazon procured their own capacity under PJM’s rules as they existed at the time of contracting.  As a result, large-load customers that entered binding bilateral capacity agreements before the date of PJM’s Statement of Principles filing in this docket (January 16, 2026) but have not finalized their load ramp under those contracts would be assigned the costs of the RBP auction in addition to the costs of the capacity they already procured, thereby requiring an unreasonable double charge for capacity.  To remedy this inequity, the Commission should protect existing arrangements to ensure that customers with ESAs and capacity contracts signed before January 16, 2026, are not charged twice for capacity under the RBP Proposal.  Customers that relied on PJM’s pre-existing framework and entered binding capacity arrangements before PJM filed the RBP Proposal with the Commission should not have the value of those agreements nullified by eligibility criteria adopted after the fact.

B. PJM’s cost-allocation proposal risks contravening longstanding cost-causation and beneficiary-pays principles, potentially penalizing existing load in LSE

zones and elevating the risks associated with forecasting accuracy.

PJM proposes to allocate RBP capacity costs to LSEs based on forecasted load growth from the 2026/2027 Delivery Year to the 2028/2029 Delivery Year (as forecasted by the LSEs) and then delegate responsibility to state regulators and Relevant Electric Retail Regulatory Authorities (“RERRAs”) to allocate capacity costs via retail rates to large-load customers that do not secure capacity through bilateral arrangements.  Once the LSE load growth and capacity allocation calculation is performed, each zone’s/area’s resulting share of the Final RBP Target “shall remain constant throughout the fixed, fifteen-year term.” 5   PJM asserts that, under its proposed methodology, zones with new load not matched with new capacity can be said to have caused the resource adequacy shortfall underlying the RBP.  This is problematic because PJM identifies those purported “cost causers” using a single forecast load snapshot and then freezes the result for the up to fifteen-year duration of RBP capacity contracts.

A static, fifteen-year load growth assumption and resulting capacity allocation cannot be reconciled with fundamental cost-causation and beneficiary-pays principles in the dynamic load- growth environment PJM itself describes.  Under the RBP Proposal, a zone assigned a large share of the RBP Target based on forecasted load growth would retain that capacity responsibility throughout the fifteen-year RBP term even if the forecasted large load never materializes, materializes at a lower level, ceases operations, or locates elsewhere.  Because large load is materializing rapidly and in locations that may differ materially from the 2026/2027 through 2028/2029 Delivery Year forecasts, a capacity allocation frozen at the outset will begin to diverge from actual cost causation.  Although PJM’s proposed RBP mechanism allows states and RERRAs

to determine how a zone’s RBP cost responsibility is allocated among LSEs and large-load customers, state regulators can allocate only the costs that PJM has first assigned to the relevant zone.  They do not have the authority to correct a regional or zonal allocation that assigns too much of the fifteen-year RBP obligation to one zone and too little to another because actual load development diverged from PJM’s initial forecast.

The Commission should therefore require PJM to periodically update the allocation of RBP costs to reflect actual incremental load growth over the RBP commitment period.  Rather than fixing each zone’s share to a single forecast snapshot, PJM should recalculate responsibility for the remaining costs based on incremental load that comes online, continuing until it identifies incremental load, net of qualifying offsets, corresponding to the Final RBP Target.  This method will enable PJM states to establish cost-allocation principles that protect customers that have or will bring additional capacity for new load.

A dynamic allocation would better match the demand side of the RBP to the duration of the supply obligation PJM proposes to undertake.  The RBP framework is intended to secure a capacity product to support new load entering the system, regardless of whether that load materializes during the 2028/2029 Delivery Year as forecasted. The new load coming online that does not otherwise enable new capacity—regardless of forecasted time or LSE zone—should be allocated RBP costs.  Aligning costs with benefits in this way would also reduce the risk of allocating stranded costs to existing customers and eliminate the perverse incentive for new large- load customers to strategically site in zones assigned comparatively small obligations under a forecast made years earlier.

C. The Commission should direct PJM to provide an accelerated interconnection process for RBP resources and resources committed to serve new large-load

customers, so that new capacity comes online when the reliability needs arise.

The Commission should require PJM to implement an accelerated generator
interconnection process for (i) RBP resources and (ii) resources supported by executed bilateral capacity arrangements with new large-load customers.

There is precedent within PJM’s recent initiatives for such an approach, including the creation of the Expedited Interconnection Track intended to interconnect advanced projects of significant size more quickly, outside the ordinary Cycle Process, in response to PJM’s urgent need for additional Capacity Resources.  Further, accelerated treatment need not guarantee interconnection or shift network-upgrade costs to other customers.  Accelerated processing would simply address timing; protracted interconnection study processes should not prevent projects that have demonstrated commercial viability and have undertaken binding obligations to mitigate the capacity shortfall within PJM from coming online in a timely manner.

To close the anticipated 6.8 GW capacity gap in PJM, the Commission should therefore direct PJM to establish or expand its existing expedited interconnection processes to provide for an accelerated interconnection pathway for RBP resources and resources with qualifying bilateral capacity contracts.

Pursuant to Rules 212 and 214 of the Commission’s Rules of Practice and Procedure, 18 C.F.R. §§ 385.212, 385.214, Amazon respectfully requests leave to intervene out-of-time in the captioned proceeding.

In determining whether to grant a motion to intervene out-of-time, the Commission may consider whether (i) the movant had “good cause” for failing to file the motion within the time

First, good cause exists for granting Amazon’s motion. Third, as a large-load customer operating within PJM, Amazon has direct and substantial interests in this proceeding.  As previously noted, this proceeding involves a proposed one-time RBP for the procurement of new capacity outside the standard PJM RPM process. Amazon may be directly and substantially affected by the outcome of this proceeding, and Amazon’s interests cannot be adequately represented by any other party.

Fourth, permitting Amazon’s motion will not prejudice or place additional burdens upon existing parties.  Amazon will accept the record as it currently stands.
