II. INTRODUCTION
The MISO Consumer Advocates represent residential and other ratepaying customers that ultimately bear substantial portions of the costs of the generation and transmission systems serving the MISO region. From that perspective, the Consumer Advocates agree with OMS that coordinated study of sufficiently mature, electrically proximate Large Loads and associated generation may provide meaningful efficiencies if appropriately designed.5 MISO has proposed safeguards intended to limit speculative projects and forecasted demand, including application deposits, generator milestones, site-control requirements, binding arrangements between associated generation and load, service deadlines, and continuing responsibility for certain assigned Network Upgrade costs following withdrawal. Those provisions should be required by FERC and adopted by MISO.6

But they do not resolve the central ratepayer risk raised by OMS: transmission investments caused by a Large Load may ultimately be recovered from customers that neither caused the investment nor receive commensurate benefits from it.

MISO’s own filing confirms that risk. MISO explains that certain constraints identified through LARS may become Baseline Reliability Projects (BRPs) or Other Projects through the MISO Transmission Expansion Plan (MTEP). The costs attributable to one Transmission

That explanation reinforces rather than resolves the concern of the MISO Consumer Advocates and OMS. The Commission instituted the related show-cause proceeding because it preliminarily found that MISO’s existing Tariff may be unjust and unreasonable insofar as it does not adequately protect transmission customers against cost shifts resulting from Large Load integration. These conditions are necessary to prevent stranded investment and to enable the States and consumer representatives to have the information necessary to adopt rates that reflect cost causation.

MISO’s proposed methodology demonstrates why that protection is necessary. Mitigation to address violations observed only in the generation study generally would be assigned to the Interconnection Customer as a Generator Interconnection Project. By contrast, mitigation to address violations found in the transmission study, including common violations found in both the load and generation studies, may become BRPs or Other Projects. That classification may materially affect who ultimately pays for the investment.10

For ratepayer-protection purposes, the inquiry should begin with cost causation, not the planning label subsequently assigned to the facility. If a LARS Associated Load causes or materially accelerates the need for a transmission investment, or the investment would not be made “but for” the Large Load, classification of that investment as a BRP, Other Project, or another MTEP project should not erase the causal relationship or allow the costs to be assigned to customers who did not cause and do not benefit from the investment.

The Commission should therefore require MISO to identify and preserve that relationship throughout the planning process. Where a LARS participant causes or materially accelerates an investment, the attributable costs should remain the responsibility of that participant except to the extent MISO demonstrates that broader allocation is independently justified by identifiable benefits to other customers.  A key inquiry is whether the investment would have been made “but for” the Large Load’s need.

This does not mean every facility identified in connection with LARS must automatically be directly assigned to a Large Load. A project may address preexisting system needs or provide

IV. LARS SHOULD NOT BECOME OPERATIONAL BEFORE THE
ASSOCIATED COST-RECOVERY PROTECTIONS ARE APPROVED
MISO acknowledges that the LARS process ultimately will require a pro forma cost- shifting agreement and additional transmission-service provisions for Large Loads. Those provisions, however, were not included in the LARS filing and remain under development for submission in a separate proceeding. MISO nevertheless maintains that LARS can be implemented without awaiting Commission consideration of those protections.12 The MISO Consumer Advocates respectfully disagree. The cost-recovery agreement is not an incidental administrative component of LARS. It is the mechanism intended to protect customers against one of the principal risks created by accelerated Large Load integration: construction of transmission facilities in reliance on projected demand that is delayed, reduced, or never materializes, and to provide supplemental payments when Network Upgrades needed for a large load create cost shift to other customers.

The Show Cause Order preliminarily found that MISO’s Tariff may be deficient because it lacks an adequate pro forma cost-recovery agreement addressing transmission investments associated with Large Loads. The Commission specifically contemplated financial commitments and credit support sufficient to reduce the risk that other transmission customers would be left paying for those investments.13

The Commission should not approve the mechanism that accelerates transmission investment decisions before it can evaluate the mechanism intended to protect customers from the resulting financial exposure. Implementation of LARS should therefore be conditioned upon

V. MISO’S READINESS AND WITHDRAWAL PROVISIONS DO NOT
RESOLVE THE COST-CAUSATION ISSUE
MISO’s proposed readiness and withdrawal provisions are meaningful. Among other measures, MISO proposes a $250,000 nonrefundable application deposit, a $30,000/MW generator milestone, 100 percent site control, binding generation-load arrangements, service deadlines, financial-security requirements, and continuing responsibility for certain Network Upgrade costs following withdrawal or termination.14

These provisions reduce the risk presented by speculative or insufficiently mature projects. They do not, however, determine which costs should be assigned to a LARS participant in the first instance.

Financial assurance and cost causation perform different functions. Financial assurance helps ensure that an entity can satisfy costs properly assigned to it. Cost-causation rules determine what costs should be assigned to that entity. If a Large Load-driven constraint is classified for broader recovery before the financial security is utilized, even complete enforcement of the financial security withdrawal provisions will not prevent the underlying cost shift.

LARS requires both meaningful financial assurance and rules that preserve responsibility for costs caused or materially accelerated by participating projects.

VI. SAME-LRZ MEMBERSHIP DOES NOT ESTABLISH THE
ELECTRICAL PROXIMITY NECESSARY FOR SPECIALIZED
TREATMENT
OMS also correctly questions MISO’s use of LRZ boundaries to establish proximity between a LARS Associated Load and associated generation. Facilities within one LRZ may remain geographically and electrically remote from one another.15

MISO explains that the LRZ standard aligns with its resource-adequacy construct, avoids capacity import and export limitations between LRZs, accommodates large or landlocked loads, and allows multiple generation points of interconnection to support one load. Those considerations may help determine whether generation contributes to resource adequacy within an LRZ. They do not necessarily establish electrical proximity.16

The Show Cause Order described an “electrically proximate large load” in functional transmission-system terms. The relevant question is whether the load is sufficiently close to the generator’s requested point of interconnection that their combined transmission-system impact, apart from the connecting facilities, is effectively the same as if they were located at the same substation. The Commission identified a Large Load located no more than two substations from the generator as one example.17

The MISO Consumer Advocates do not recommend converting that example into an inflexible geographic rule. MISO may reasonably develop an approach suited to its transmission system. But the standard should remain electrical, rather than merely geographic or administrative.

MISO should be required to demonstrate through the LARS study that associated generation materially offsets the Large Load at the relevant transmission constraints and that the pair meets explicit quantitative engineering criteria—such as a maximum electrical

VII. MATERIAL CHANGES SHOULD TRIGGER REVIEW OF
CONTINUING NEED AND COST RESPONSIBILITY
MISO proposes that there will be no LARS restudy when an Associated Load withdraws. The MISO Consumer Advocates recognize that routine comprehensive restudies could undermine the speed and predictability LARS is intended to provide.18

But categorical elimination of restudy presents the opposite risk. MISO proposes to evaluate the cumulative effects of participating projects and allow subsequent studies to rely on transmission facilities identified in earlier LARS studies. Withdrawal, material downsizing, or failure of a participating load or generator may therefore change the need for, scope of, timing of, or beneficiaries of an identified transmission investment.  Large load projects that require hundreds of megawatts of power can materially affect the need for transmission investment and changes in those projects should be readily incorporated into transmission planning. The Commission need not require automatic repetition of the entire LARS study. It should instead require a materiality review following a withdrawal, material downsizing, or other significant change. MISO should determine and document whether the change:

1. eliminates or materially reduces the need for an identified upgrade;
2. changes its appropriate scope or timing;
3. alters the projects responsible for the underlying constraint; or
4. materially affects the proper allocation of costs.
Where the change is material, MISO should perform a targeted restudy sufficient to confirm the continuing need, appropriate scope, and cost responsibility for the affected facilities.

VIII. TRANSPARENCY AND PERIODIC REVIEW ARE NECESSARY TO
PROTECT RATEPAYERS
The MISO Consumer Advocates support OMS’s recommendations concerning public reporting and a five-year sunset.19

Transparency should permit regulators and consumer advocates to trace a LARS-driven transmission investment from the initial study through MTEP approval, reclassification, construction, final cost determination, allocation, and recovery. Without that traceability, stakeholders may be unable to determine whether a facility originally driven by a LARS participant later appears under a different project designation or rate treatment.  Transparency and traceability are thus required to ensure proper cost allocation to protect existing customers. MISO should therefore assign a persistent identifier or use an equivalent mechanism to preserve the connection between a LARS project and the facilities it causes or materially accelerates.

A five-year sunset and formal review are also appropriate because MISO proposes LARS as a permanent process from its inception.20

Before continuing LARS, MISO should report at least:
• LARS applications, completed projects, and withdrawals;
• forecast and actual Large Load additions;
• transmission investments identified through LARS;
• initial and subsequent project classifications;
• estimated and final costs;
• amounts recovered from LARS participants and other customers;
• withdrawal-related materiality reviews and restudies; and
• measurable effects on MISO’s ordinary generator interconnection process. If LARS produces the efficiencies MISO anticipates while protecting existing customers, continuation should be supportable by that evidence. If it does not, customers should not be locked into a permanent process adopted before its effects are known.

IX. CONCLUSION
The MISO Consumer Advocates support OMS’s central position. Coordinated study of mature Large Loads and genuinely associated, electrically proximate generation may provide meaningful benefits. MISO has also proposed valuable project-readiness and financial- commitment requirements that should remain part of any LARS framework the Commission accepts.

But MISO’s filing confirms that LARS can produce cost shifts under existing Tariff mechanisms, proposes implementation before the associated cost-recovery agreement is before the Commission, relies on an LRZ construct that does not necessarily demonstrate electrical proximity, and categorically eliminates restudies following load withdrawal. The Commission should condition acceptance and implementation of LARS upon:

1. assignment of cost responsibility where a LARS participant causes or materially accelerates a transmission investment;

2. prior Commission acceptance of an enforceable Large Load cost-recovery and financial- security framework including technical power-quality operational rules;

3. an engineering-based demonstration of electrical proximity bounded by quantitative impedance or substation distance criteria;

4. materiality review and targeted restudy following material project changes;
5. transparent tracking of LARS-driven investments through planning and cost recovery; and
6. a five-year sunset followed by a public, data-driven review.
These conditions would preserve the potential benefits of coordinated study while adhering to a basic consumer-protection principle: customers should not bear transmission costs or development risks merely because an expedited process causes those costs to move into a category subject to broader recovery.
