II. 
Background  
 
A.  
Large Loads 
 
The CAISO’s work on large loads has unfolded against a rapidly evolving 
policy landscape. The CAISO’s initial focus was to address the most immediate 
operational challenges that could impact reliability. As the stakeholder initiative 
progressed, the priorities and expectations of federal and State regulators for 
large load interconnections continued to develop. Through it all, the CAISO has 
sought to remain responsive to that evolving direction. The CAISO adjusted the 
scope and sequencing of its stakeholder process accordingly, while continuing to 
provide stakeholders a meaningful opportunity to shape the proposals presented 
in this filing.  
 
 
In 2025, the CAISO began regular engagement with the PTOs and formed 
a Large Load Technical Requirements Working Group (LLTRWG) to explore 
possible technical standards to ensure reliable operations as new large loads 
interconnect to the CAISO system. The CAISO’s Issue Paper, posted in January 
2026, described current roles, considerations, and processes for large loads, 
including transmission and resource planning, transmission service offerings, 
cost allocation and responsibility, technical requirements and standards, 
operational requirements, and related issues.2 The CAISO has continued to 
monitor and engage in State and federal regulatory discussions regarding large 
loads and explored central issues and procedural steps that require additional 
transparency or new products. On June 15, 2026, the CAISO issued a Large 
Loads Technical Requirements Straw Proposal on behalf of the LLTRWG in 
order to stay ahead of any potential operational concerns that large loads may 
present. As NERC’s work on large loads ramped up, the LLTRWG focused on 
responding to NERC alerts and inquiries to help share eventual reliability 
standards.

load stakeholder initiative to work with stakeholders to address each issue in the

PTOs, and continuous discussions with local regulators and stakeholders.

stakeholder process, including Google, Microsoft, Tesla, and the California Large 
Energy Consumers Association. The CAISO received written comments on its

B. 
Key CAISO Differences  
 
1. 
Capacity Requirements 
 
The CAISO’s approach to large loads differs from approaches advanced 
in other ISO/RTO regions because the CAISO does not condition large load 
interconnection on a requirement to “bring your own generation.” Rather than 
requiring each new large load to develop or procure a matching increment of 
dedicated generation, California addresses supply adequacy through coordinated 
long term demand forecasting, integrated resource planning, resource 
procurement, and transmission planning. These processes are designed to 
anticipate new demand and develop the resources and delivery infrastructure 
needed to serve it. Continued generation and storage additions are expected to 
support forecast load growth, subject to timely procurement and project 
development. As such, the CAISO currently can accommodate large loads 
without making each customer’s energization and transmission service 
contingent on completion of a separate generation project. At the same time, the 
CAISO recognizes that resource sufficiency depends on the realization of 
planned additions, and large loads may develop faster than forecast or 
procurement processes anticipate. For this reason, the CAISO continually works 
with the California energy agencies on forecasting, procurement, and planning.10  
The CAISO will continue assessing whether new loads create incremental 
reliability risks; however, at this time, a bring-your-own generation requirement is 
not a condition for interconnection or transmission service.

This distinction can remove an important barrier to timely service: a large 
load customer need not independently secure new generating capacity or satisfy 
a separate CAISO forward-capacity-market requirement as a condition of 
interconnection. The CAISO has no centralized forward capacity market; instead, 
serving load remains subject to the resource adequacy obligations applicable to 
its load-serving entity (LSE) and the requirements of the relevant local regulatory 
authority. The absence of a customer-specific generation requirement therefore 
does not eliminate advance capacity procurement obligations or guarantee 
immediate service at every location. Large loads must still complete applicable 
interconnection studies, satisfy technical and operating requirements, and fund or 
await required facilities and network upgrades. The resulting framework 
facilitates access to the shared grid without requiring a one-for-one pairing of 
each new load with new generation, while preserving the planning, procurement, 
and cost-responsibility safeguards necessary to protect reliability and existing 
customers. This framework has enabled the construction of many data centers, 
including those supporting Silicon Valley, for several years. 
 
This distinction also explains why the CAISO does not propose to replicate 
generation-linked mechanisms developed or proposed in other ISO/RTO regions. 
Where a large load’s access to service depends on identifying sufficient 
incremental generation, special arrangements may be needed to pair the load 
with a generating project, coordinate their development schedules, or condition 
the load’s operation on the availability of designated supply. Those arrangements 
address a constraint that the CAISO’s framework does not impose: a large load 
customer need not sponsor a corresponding generation project to obtain 
interconnection. Adopting mechanisms whose principal purpose is to satisfy or 
provide an exception to that requirement would therefore add complexity without 
removing a comparable barrier to service in the CAISO. The relevant question is 
not whether another region has adopted a particular innovation, but whether the 
condition that makes that innovation necessary exists under the CAISO’s market 
and regulatory framework. 
 
The CAISO’s proposed reforms in the instant filing instead focus on the 
constraints that actually govern a large load’s ability to interconnect and operate 
reliably: available transmission capability, timely completion of necessary 
upgrades, adequate visibility into load characteristics, enforceable operating 
requirements, and appropriate cost responsibility. A flexible interim 
interconnection arrangement, for example, can address a transmission 
bottleneck by allowing earlier energization subject to operating limits while 
upgrades are completed; it need not also function as a bridge until the 
customer’s dedicated generation enters service. This does not eliminate the 
serving LSE’s resource adequacy obligations or the need to plan and procure 
sufficient supply. It means that those obligations remain within the existing 
planning and procurement framework, rather than becoming a separate, 
customer-specific generation-matching condition layered onto the load

2. 
Eligible Customers and Order No. 888 
 
Similarly, the CAISO’s framework for providing transmission access differs 
from the traditional Order No. 888 model. First, the CAISO does not use the 
“Eligible Customer” construct in any significant way. The very few references to 
Eligible Customer in the CAISO tariff are merely catch-alls to prescribe that all 
market participants use a scheduling coordinator to interact with the markets.11 
Second, the CAISO does not offer specific transmission services. As the 
Commission stated in the Order to Show Cause: 
 
Unlike other regions, CAISO does not offer traditional Order No. 
888 network and point-to-point transmission services, offers no 
firm, long-term transmission reservations of capacity, and does not 
provide a formal application process for transmission service. 
Instead, CAISO offers a single “daily” transmission service that is 
available to all Eligible Customers on a day-to-day basis. All energy 
that is transmitted under CAISO’s Tariff that is not associated with 
historical rights or certain wheeling transactions is treated as “new 
firm use,” and those accessing the CAISO-controlled transmission 
system cannot reserve transmission capacity beyond the day-
ahead time frame.12  
 
In other words, transmission service is assumed in the sales of energy and 
ancillary services. Stakeholders agreed with the suggestions in the Commission’s 
Order that large loads do not warrant reforming how the CAISO complies with 
Order No. 888, which has remained unchanged for 30 years. As such, the 
CAISO’s compliance focuses on achieving the Order’s goals without redesigning 
transmission service or creating a centralized capacity market for the first time in 
the CAISO. The CAISO’s reforms thus provide interconnection services to enable 
large loads to interconnect to the transmission system expeditiously, and without 
jeopardizing reliability or just and reasonable rates.

3. 
Allocation of Responsibilities 
 
The Commission’s Order does not require the CAISO and its PTOs to 
reallocate their existing responsibilities for interconnecting and serving load.13 
The Commission expressly stated it recognizes that the PTOs are responsible for 
conducting load interconnection studies, the Commission “appreciate[s] that this 
role may stem from how CAISO and the Participating Transmission Owners 
allocated rights and responsibilities in the governing documents establishing 
CAISO,” and that the Commission “do[es] not seek to alter that allocation of 
rights and responsibilities.”14 Rather than requiring a reallocation of responsibility 
between the CAISO and PTO tariffs, the Order identifies a need for greater clarity 
and consistency in the applicable tariffs, including a clear identification of the 
entity responsible for studying service to large loads. The distinction is important: 
addressing potential gaps in the governing procedures does not require 
transferring the load interconnection study function from the PTOs to the CAISO. 
Stakeholders’ preference was for the existing framework to remain in place, with 
the PTOs administering load interconnection requests and performing the 
associated studies under their tariffs, coordinated with the CAISO’s transmission 
planning, generator interconnection, and system reliability responsibilities. The 
necessary reforms can make those complementary roles explicit and improve 
coordination without centralizing all interconnection functions in the CAISO tariff. 
 
Although the PTOs will conduct load interconnection studies under their 
tariffs, the CAISO will play a pivotal role as the balancing authority and planning 
coordinator for the CAISO area in reviewing those studies. Because each PTO 
studies requests on its own system, the CAISO is uniquely positioned to evaluate 
study results in the context of the integrated grid, including the transmission 
facilities of other PTOs, pending generation, and the CAISO’s approved 
transmission plans. In its role, the CAISO will review PTO load interconnection 
studies to confirm that study assumptions, load characteristics, and results are 
consistent with the CAISO’s planning models and long-term plans. Most 
importantly, the CAISO will identify any PTO-proposed solution that could create 
reliability risk, whether because it relies on an operating measure that cannot be 
dependably implemented, it shifts or exacerbates constraints elsewhere on the 
system, or it is inconsistent with the CAISO’s planning and operational needs.

III. 
Tariff Revisions and Order to Show Cause  
 
Although the CAISO believes the tariff revisions in this filing do not 
substantially affect Order Nos. 888, 1000, 2003, 792, 845, 2023, or 1920 policies, 
the CAISO’s proposed tariff revisions meet both the just and reasonable standard 
and the independent entity standard. The tariff revisions address issues unique 
to the CAISO, and were generally designed to work without significant conflict 
with the CAISO’s tariff provisions modeled on the Commission’s pro forma Open

The CAISO’s proposed FILI framework fulfills the Commission’s central 
objective of making meaningful service options available to large loads whose 
willingness and ability to limit withdrawals permit earlier, reliable access to the 
transmission system, without importing three distinct transmission service 
products into the CAISO’s unique market structure. The Order expressly allows 
the CAISO to explain how changes within its existing framework can provide 
comparable flexibility, rather than requiring it to replicate the service 
classifications contemplated for PJM. FILI translates that flexibility into 
enforceable interconnection terms: a large load may energize before completion 
of required network upgrades, but only within operating limits supported by 
system conditions and subject to specified curtailment triggers, control 
mechanisms, response times, and verification requirements. Agreements 
executed before energization identify those obligations and the facilities 
necessary to conclude interim service, while incorporation into the CAISO’s 
models and operating procedures makes the customer’s flexibility operationally 
usable rather than merely aspirational. Public posting of those agreements also 
provides transparency into the conditions governing access. Although FILI is an 
interim arrangement rather than a substitute for every feature of the three 
contemplated services, it addresses the immediate barrier posed by transmission 
construction timelines while preserving reliability and applicable cost-
responsibility requirements. The relevant distinction is therefore not the number 
or label of service offerings, but whether the framework enables customers to 
obtain earlier service commensurate with their actual, enforceable use of the 
grid—the result FILI is designed to achieve.

permanent version of FILI in 2027. This would allow large loads to forgo some 
identified network upgrades altogether if the large loads were curtailable. The 
CAISO recognizes that permanent load flexibility can have significant value even 
in a system with adequate aggregate generating capacity. Although the CAISO 
believes its tariff is just and reasonable without a permanent option at this time, 
the CAISO is committed to exploring the permanent option. The prioritization of 
FILI is consistent with stakeholders’ comments, especially from the large load

The CAISO has committed to examining electrically proximate co-location

The Commission’s contemplated treatment of electrically proximate 
generation and large load addresses a potential benefit of studying their 
combined transmission-system effects, but the need for a separate service 
pathway must be evaluated against the CAISO’s existing framework. Unlike an 
Eastern framework that conditions a large load’s energization on the 
development of corresponding generation, the CAISO does not require a large 
load customer to bring its own generation or pair its interconnection request with 
a particular generating facility. Supply adequacy remains the responsibility of the 
LSE under applicable resource adequacy and regulatory requirements, while the 
load’s ability to interconnect depends on the applicable studies, available 
transmission capability, necessary facilities, and operating requirements. 
Accordingly, to the extent an electrically proximate pathway is intended to enable 
a customer to satisfy a generation-matching condition or obtain an exception 
from such a condition, it solves a problem the CAISO’s framework does not 
impose. The CAISO’s near-term reforms instead target the barriers its customers 
actually face, including transmission construction timelines, coordinated studies, 
and enforceable operating limits that can permit earlier energization.

can never produce transmission benefits. The Order’s premise—that sufficiently 
close generation and load may have combined effects resembling a co-located 
arrangement—presents a legitimate study question. It does not, however,

intervening facilities, the consequences of a generator outage or a change in 
load, or the obligations necessary to make any assumed offset dependable. Nor
