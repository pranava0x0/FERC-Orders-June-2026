The Commission’s consideration of PJM governance reform is timely and 
welcome. The Commission is responsible for ensuring just and reasonable rates. However, 
PJM’s structure is a major impediment to achieving those rates. PJM’s voting membership 
is overwhelmingly market participants who have an interest in higher revenues. The 67 
million customers–who pay the bills and use the electricity–are PJM’s raison d'être, but 
are woefully underrepresented in the stakeholder process. These 67 million customers in 
PJM’s footprint are represented solely by 14 consumer advocate votes – one for each 
jurisdiction. These 14 votes translate to 1.3% of potential voters at the Standing Committee 
and Senior Task Force level, where the details of rules and plans are designed. At the Senior 
Standing Committee levels, the Markets and Reliability Committee (MRC) and Members 
Committee (MC), the 14 consumer advocates make up approximately 7% of the vote.1 The 
lack of ratepayer representation in the PJM stakeholder process has led to an untenable 
status quo, where moneyed interests design and vote on the rules that benefit them, without 
regard to public cost.

Any governance reforms proposed at PJM should strengthen not only the state 
voice, but also the voice of the 67 million customers that PJM serves, who are represented 
solely by statutory consumer advocates. Our consumer advocate offices have longstanding 
technical expertise at PJM and are members of the End-Use Customer sector. Our offices 
have deeply engaged at PJM long before the RTO became front page news, and we will 
continue to do so after PJM fades back to the obscurity of the public utility world.  
Consumer advocates have perspectives that are unique and distinct from other state 
entities. We are independent bodies charged with protecting ratepayers from unreasonable 
costs on their utility bills. We represent ratepayers before our state commissions, who 
consider issues beyond cost to ratepayers. At times we agree with our state commissions 
and at times we disagree. Similarly, state policy entities, like governors or legislators, 
balance a number of competing priorities, such as economic development and 
environmental protection, of which ratepayer affordability is simply one. Unlike state 
Commissions or other policymakers, we are in the position of singularly advocating for 
positive outcomes for consumers in PJM. Our positions do not originate from a “balance” 
between the investor-owned interests and PJM ratepayers. Governance reforms must thus 
have an eye to both state and ratepayer representation. At minimum, this must include 
revising PJM’s mission and Operating Agreement to serve the public interest by balancing 
affordability alongside reliability.2

I. EXPANDED 205 RIGHTS FOR THE PJM BOARD SHOULD ONLY BE 
PURSUED ALONGSIDE REVISIONS TO THE GOVERNING DOCUMENTS AND JUMP 
BALL FILING RIGHTS FOR THE STATES AND MEMBERS COMMITTEE 
Much of the Technical Conference focused on whether the PJM Board should be 
granted expanded 205 rights. The PJM Board must not be granted expanded 205 rights 
unless and until the LLC’s mission and governing documents are reformed to account for 
affordability and the public interest, and the states and Members Committee have 205 jump 
ball filing rights on all topics. Regardless of whether 205 rights get expanded or not, these 
checks and balances on PJM would support the principles of accountability, transparency, 
and Board independence. 
 
A. The Board has not used its existing filing rights effectively 
Any proposed changes to PJM’s governance should be evaluated on their ability to 
solve the current problems that PJM is facing. This includes the question of whether or not 
PJM should have expanded 205 rights. If the Board is granted expanded 205 rights and all 
else remains equal, the regional situation will not improve.  
The Board has 205 rights for the capacity market, which has been in a state of 
disarray for years. Instead of acting decisively after the first Critical Issues Fast Path (CIFP) 
on Large Load Adjustments concluded, the PJM Board started yet another CIFP. While it 
is critical to get stakeholder feedback, the Board has not used the existing 205 rights to 
demonstrate leadership. In contrast, the PJM Board does utilize its existing 206 rights when 
it deems an issue important, so the lack of 205 rights is not necessarily an impediment. 
When the Board disagreed with the vast majority of stakeholders on proposed changes to

the Consolidated Transmission Owners Agreement, it overrode Member objections3 and 
filed a 206 at FERC. In short, there is seemingly no rhyme or reason to when the Board 
finds stakeholder opposition to be meaningful or not: the Board has 205 rights for the 
capacity market, yet has pursued a multitude of CIFPs; yet being limited to 206 rights in 
other areas has not prevented the PJM Board from taking action. 
Nevertheless, it is true that the PJM Board is fairly unique among RTOs in lacking 
205 rights over transmission planning and energy markets, and the Commission may 
choose to resolve that discrepancy. Should the Commission grant the PJM Board expanded 
205 filing rights, it must only do so once the PJM mission and governing documents are 
revised to include affordability and the public interest, and the states and Members 
Committee have jump ball filing rights. Both reforms are essential independently, but 
together, they would help to ensure that the Board’s enhanced power leads to positive 
outcomes. 
 
B. The need to reflect affordability and the public interest in PJM’s mission 
As the grid operator managing electricity for 14 jurisdictions and 67 million 
customers, PJM’s success or failure is intertwined with the public interest. Electricity is a 
public good necessary to modern life. Reliable electricity is critical and it must also be 
affordable. The PJM mission statement and governing documents should be revised to 
reflect that the Board must act in the public interest by balancing reliability and 
affordability. The Federal Power Act requires that rates be just and reasonable.4 To do so,

However, the principle of least cost is not reflected in PJM’s mission or governing

The absence of affordability from PJM’s mission and governing documents has

C. States must have 205 jump ball filing rights 
The status quo of the states having merely an informal advisory role to PJM through 
the Organization of the PJM States (OPSI) is not working. States have the least formalized 
input into PJM than any of the other multistate RTOs. This creates difficulty when state 
laws rely on PJM’s cooperation for implementation, or when PJM makes rules or 
undertakes planning processes that undercut state goals. States have responsibilities and

Improvements to PJM coordination with the states go beyond the allocation of 205 
rights. Ideally, states would feel confident in their relationship with PJM leadership so that 
those rights need never be exercised. Prior to making major filings at FERC, the PJM Board 
should consult with OPSI. This could entail the Board sharing draft filings with OPSI for 
feedback prior to formal filing at FERC. Allowing OPSI to review filings in advance would 
head off key concerns by the states and could lead to less litigation. For the purposes of 
formal OPSI consultation, major filings could be filings resulting in a certain threshold of 
cost impact, or at minimum, any policy decided through a CIFP.

II. THE “CREDIBILITY TRAP”7 IS RESOLVABLE WITH MEANINGFUL COST 
OVERSIGHT 
 
A. Require cost impact information and ongoing cost impact dialogue 
The lack of cost impact analysis at PJM should be considered unacceptable. PJM-
managed markets and planning account for more than half of the cost of residential 
ratepayer bills across the states in the region.8 There is no way to evaluate whether PJM 
has achieved just and reasonable rates without cost impact information. As consumer 
advocates, we work at both the RTO and the state level, representing ratepayers in front of 
our commissions. The difference between the quality of cost information we can access at 
the state level versus at PJM is staggering. At PJM, we rarely receive any cost impact 
analysis prior to voting on proposals. A proposal that PJM or a Member advances may 
promote reliability, but it is also possible that there are alternatives that could achieve the 
same reliability results at lower cost; there is no way to know, because currently there is 
no standardized method for evaluating proposals for cost. It should be standard practice for 
PJM to evaluate the cost impact of any proposal as part of the First Read at the MRC/MC. 
Such analysis may not provide perfect information, but just as PJM is expected to evaluate 
proposals for reliability, they should also do so for cost. This way, stakeholders and states

B. Appoint an Independent Transmission Monitor 
PJM’s Independent Market Monitor plays an invaluable role, not only mitigating 
market power, but also often providing cost information that PJM does not. However, there 
is no such equivalent accountability for transmission costs. Even when reported, 
transmission costs are often incomplete or inaccurate.9 Even worse, many transmission 
projects, namely supplemental projects, receive no cost oversight at all, as detailed by the 
Ohio Consumers Counsel.10 The result is that Transmission Owners have an incentive to 
overbuild less efficient transmission projects that escape oversight, while underbuilding 
more thoroughly scrutinized, but also likely more regionally beneficial, projects. An 
Independent Transmission Monitor would provide impartial and independent cost analysis, 
increasing the transparency and accountability of PJM’s transmission planning. By 
improving transmission oversight, planning would be improved, costs would be better

III. IMPROVEMENTS TO PJM’S BOARD ARE NECESSARY TO ACHIEVE 
POSITIVE GOVERNANCE REFORM 
The lack of a public interest mandate has allowed the PJM Board to largely ignore 
the cost impacts of PJM decisions on the 67 million customers it serves. In addition to 
reforming PJM’s mission to include affordability and the public interest, there are several 
ways the PJM Board can be more effective. 
 
A. Expand the Nominating Committee to include state representatives 
We agree with Jacob Finkel’s suggestion, on behalf of the Governors Collaborative, 
to expand the Nominating Committee to include 3 designees of the states.11 A potential 
model would be a seat for an OPSI designee, a seat for an appointee of a separate 
governors/legislators organization, and a statutory consumer advocate. Consumer 
advocates, as Members of the End-Use Customer sector, alternate with representatives of 
industrial energy users on the Nominating Committee, but do not have a permanent seat. 
As the only voice of the 67 million customers, there should always be meaningful consumer 
advocate input when Board members are selected.

B. Expand the Board expertise requirements to include state and ratepayer 
experience  
The PJM Board has a number of expertise requirements. For example, there must 
always be a Board member with transmission planning experience. Other areas of expertise 
include cybersecurity, law, and accounting. The Board expertise requirements should be 
updated to include 1) a former state regulator and 2) an individual with experience 
representing ratepayer interests. These expertise requirements are necessary in part to 
facilitate understanding of how wholesale costs are translated onto retail bills. Having a 
former state regulator on the PJM Board will also support a better understanding among 
Board members of state processes and jurisdiction.  
 
C. Open Board meetings and publish Board votes 
Board meetings are currently conducted in private. Recorded minutes are minimal. 
The unfortunate result is that Members, and the public, have no way to evaluate individual 
Board performance, which can lead to unfavorable election votes when the Board makes 
decisions contrary to Member perspectives. The lack of transparency also prevents 
Members and the public from knowing the depth of the Board’s knowledge. It is difficult 
to understand Board perspectives when their deliberations are shrouded in secrecy. Making 
Board meetings and individual votes public will greatly enhance accountability for, and 
understanding of, the Board.  
Some commentators have suggested that ex parte rules be revised to allow private 
contact between PJM Members and individual Board members. We disagree. Ex parte

IV. CONSENSUS-BUILDING IN THE STAKEHOLDER PROCESS CAN BE 
IMPROVED BY MODIFICATIONS TO VOTING AND TRANSPARENCY RULES 
 
A. Expand sector-weighted voting to all Standing Committees and Senior Task 
Forces 
Sector-weighted voting should be expanded throughout all PJM committees. Under 
current affiliate voting practices, proposals in Standing Committees and Senior Task 
Forces are biased towards PJM members representing supply interests. For example, West 
Deptford Energy (also known as LS Power) has 25 affiliate votes at the Standing 
Committee and Senior Task Force level. This is more votes than every consumer advocate 
combined, since consumer advocate votes are capped at one for each of the 14 
jurisdictions.12 The barrier to consensus emerges once proposals are advanced to the Senior 
Standing Committees of the MRC and MC. Because supply-side interests can advance a 
proposal out of the standing committees without load support, proposals at the MRC or 
MC may then face a higher risk of rejection by load interests. There is a difference between 
being able to veto a solution and shape a solution. PJM’s current structure creates a 
dynamic where load interests are often painted as naysayers over-exercising veto power, 
but any naysaying might be reduced if load interests had more meaningful input at the

Standing Committee level, where proposals are negotiated and designed in detail. Applying 
sector-weighted voting to the standing committees will allow affiliates to continue voting, 
but would prevent their ability to dominate the outcomes. Then, proposals that are 
advanced to the MRC and MC are more likely to be balanced and pass. Even supply-side 
commentators have noted that affiliate voting in the standing committees stifles consensus 
and recommended applying sector-weighted voting across all levels.13 We concur.  
 
B. Require transparency of individual firm votes across all committee levels 
Votes are only reported in full at the MC. At the MRC, vote results are reported by 
sector, but not broken down by individual firm. At the Standing Committee and Senior 
Task Force level, votes are only reported in aggregate, with the number and percentage of 
yeas, nays, and abstentions. Not only does this limit transparency — which should be a 
core value of any entity making decisions affecting rates and reliability – but it also 
scuppers consensus building.  
For example, a recent vote at the Deactivation Enhancements Senior Task Force 
(DESTF) resulted in no proposal reaching the 50% threshold to advance. However, PJM’s 
proposal was close to advancing – 44% of stakeholders preferred it over the status quo.14 
In any legislative process, knowing who voted how would then lead to conversations about 
why they took the position they did, what their concerns were, and what changes might 
result in getting a proposal across the finish line. Knowing full vote results facilitates

Requiring voting transparency will also facilitate fairness among PJM Members. In 
Delaware,15 Maryland,16 New Jersey,17 and Virginia,18 utilities are required to report the

Members. Full and comprehensive voting disclosure by PJM would eliminate this disparate 
treatment.  
PJM Members are not acting as individual voters—they are deciding on policies 
that impact the reliability and affordability of electricity for 67 million customers. Just as 
elected officials must vote in public, PJM Members should as well.  
 
C. Record and publicly share stakeholder meetings 
There are more PJM meetings than days in the year, and many meetings run as long 
as 8 hours. No human can possibly attend them all. Even for those who do attend as many 
meetings as feasible, the subject matter is highly technical and can be difficult to fully 
digest in the moment. The transparency of the PJM process and quality of the technical 
solutions will be greatly enhanced by recording meetings and posting those recordings, 
with transcripts, publicly.

V. CONCLUSION 
PJM governance is broken and leads to unjust and unreasonable outcomes. We 
recommend the following changes:  
1) Reflect affordability and the public interest in PJM’s mission and governing 
documents 
2) Grant OPSI 205 jump ball filing rights 
3) Formalize Board consultation with OPSI 
4) Require cost impact information 
5) Appoint an Independent Transmission Monitor 
6) Expand the Nominating Committee to include state representatives 
7) Expand the Board expertise requirements to include state and ratepayer 
experience  
8) Open Board meetings and publish Board votes 
9) Expand sector-weighted voting to all Standing Committees and Senior Task 
Forces 
10) Require transparency of individual firm votes across all committee levels 
11) Record and publicly share stakeholder meetings

20) 
Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a section 205 
filing related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation? 
X 
Comments: Prior to making major filings at FERC, the PJM Board should consult with OPSI. This 
could entail the Board sharing draft filings with OPSI for feedback prior to formal filing at FERC. 
Allowing OPSI to review filings in advance would head off key concerns by the states and could lead 
to less litigation. For the purposes of formal OPSI consultation, major filings could be filings resulting 
in a certain threshold of cost impact, or at minimum, any policy decided through a CIFP. States input 
should extend to transmission planning and the energy and ancillary services markets, not just 
resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation.
