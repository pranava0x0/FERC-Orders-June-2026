A. PJM’s Existing Study Processes to Identify and Allocate Costs for
Transmission Upgrades is Unjust and Unreasonable.
For co-location arrangement Options 1 and 2, PJM’s current practices assess the transmission upgrades associated with co-location agreements through a combination of three processes: the Necessary Study, Transmission Owner Load Integration Studies, and RTEP.15 Though these studies do result in the co-location arrangement paying for some portion of the identified upgrades, they fail to ensure that the co-location arrangement pays its fair share of these upgrade costs under cost allocation principles.  We note that the cost of transmission upgrades related to interconnection-like processes are distinct from broader transmission rates. Under PJM’s existing co-location options, co-located load is treated as Network Load and thus does pay for transmission service.16  As explained in greater detail in Section III (A), we believe it is appropriate for co-located load to pay for transmission service, and thus support PJM’s treatment of co-located load as Network Load.17  This section focuses solely on the cost- allocation for transmission system upgrades caused by the shift to a co-location agreement, not the costs of ongoing delivery of services via the transmission system following the initial shift.

C. PJM Must Not Limit States’ Authority Over the Co-Located Generation
Mix.
The Commission also correctly notes that under the FPA,124 states retain authority over
the “mix” (that is, the portfolio characteristics) of a state’s generating fleet, as well as the locations and permission to operate for specific generating facilities.125  While generator interconnections to the transmission system are a matter of Commission jurisdiction, and the Commission through its FPA Section 215 authority may approve reliability standards for
