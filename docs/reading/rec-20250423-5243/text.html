A. PJM’s Existing Study Processes to Identify and Allocate Costs for 
Transmission Upgrades is Unjust and Unreasonable. 
For co-location arrangement Options 1 and 2, PJM’s current practices assess the 
transmission upgrades associated with co-location agreements through a combination of three 
processes: the Necessary Study, Transmission Owner Load Integration Studies, and RTEP.15  
Though these studies do result in the co-location arrangement paying for some portion of the 
identified upgrades, they fail to ensure that the co-location arrangement pays its fair share of 
these upgrade costs under cost allocation principles.  We note that the cost of transmission 
upgrades related to interconnection-like processes are distinct from broader transmission rates.  
Under PJM’s existing co-location options, co-located load is treated as Network Load and thus 
does pay for transmission service.16  As explained in greater detail in Section III (A), we believe 
it is appropriate for co-located load to pay for transmission service, and thus support PJM’s 
treatment of co-located load as Network Load.17  This section focuses solely on the cost-
allocation for transmission system upgrades caused by the shift to a co-location agreement, not 
the costs of ongoing delivery of services via the transmission system following the initial shift.

1. The Necessary Study Process for Co-Location Arrangements is 
Opaque and Not Suited for Co-Location, Causing Unjust and 
Unreasonable Rates.  
As the Commission finds in the Show Cause Order, inadequate interconnection service 
provisions for co-location arrangements, “especially regarding studies of the impacts of those 
arrangements,” may render PJM’s Tariff unjust and unreasonable.18  We agree that the Necessary 
Study “may not be fully capturing the transmission system impacts of co-locating load,” and thus

Abraham Silverman explained in the Commission’s technical conference docket, while from an 
engineering perspective there is little difference between co-location and other load

different rate impacts.”20

entering into a co-location arrangement for all or most of its energy production.  As Glatz and 
Silverman’s comments explain, in the standard usage of the Necessary Study Process for routine 
maintenance type updates, PJM grants no new grid services or rights.22  For example, the

C. PJM Must Not Limit States’ Authority Over the Co-Located Generation 
Mix. 
The Commission also correctly notes that under the FPA,124 states retain authority over 
the “mix” (that is, the portfolio characteristics) of a state’s generating fleet, as well as the 
locations and permission to operate for specific generating facilities.125  While generator 
interconnections to the transmission system are a matter of Commission jurisdiction, and the 
Commission through its FPA Section 215 authority may approve reliability standards for 
generators, the Commission has no role to directly dictate, subsidize, discourage, or ban 
particular generating projects or types of generating projects.  With these jurisdictional contours 
in mind, any FERC-jurisdictional tariff structure that may facilitate co-location arrangements 
must be neutral to technology type and not favor or disfavor any type of co-located generator, 
lest it encroach on a state’s desire to structure co-location arrangements according to a preferred 
mix, consistent with state authority under the FPA.

V. 
Conclusion 
Given the scale and pace of data center load growth in PJM’s projections and, therefore, 
the high likelihood that generators and large loads will increasingly seek to implement co-
location arrangements, the Commission should take action to ensure that PJM revises its existing 
tariff and related agreements for co-location arrangements (Options 1 and 2), consistent with our 
comments, to ensure that rates, terms, and conditions for Commission-jurisdictional services are 
just, reasonable and not unduly discriminatory or preferential.   We agree that large loads should 
remain ineligible for the behind-the-meter option (Option 3).  And, while we see limited to no 
utility with requiring PJM to implement new co-location arrangements (Options 4-8), if the 
Commission disagrees, we request that it require PJM to implement the provisions that mitigate 
the risks that we discuss above (e.g., unlawful cost shifts).

ODEC Large Load Forecast Methodology
1.
ODEC reviews delivery point load requests monthly 
2.
To prepare for the annual PJM large load adjustment submission, ODEC meets with its 
member distribution cooperatives to review each individual delivery point request to discuss 
project specifics
▪Determine financial commitments made by the end use customer
▪Identify contracts that are in place
▪Discuss other quantitative/qualitative factors to forecast the likelihood of the project
3.
Using the information identified in step 2, ODEC selects the projects to include in the 
submission to PJM
▪30% (~950 MW) of ODEC’s total submission are projects currently in-service or under 
construction 
▪Additional projects were included that met the qualifications outlined in PJM Manual 19b
▪ODEC’s submission to PJM represents approximately 25% (in terms of MWs) of the delivery 
point requests already submitted or are anticipated to be submitted soon
4.
ODEC uses the load ramp provided by the member (by way of the end use customer) and 
assumes monthly smoothing
5.
ODEC meets with member cooperatives and TOs to verify that projects are not double counted
