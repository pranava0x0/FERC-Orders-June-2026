I. The Commission Should Fast Track Interim NITS
The fastest approach to “release the bottleneck of large load investments across the PJM footprint”25 and obtain national and economic security is to quickly implement Interim NITS for new and pending large load applicants. Taking three and half years to implement this transmission service will not win the AI race, and such delay is inconsistent with the Commission’s December 18th Order.26 Immediately after the paper hearing briefing concludes, the Commission should establish a separate track to promptly approve the rates and terms for Interim NITS as modified below in Section III and make them effective no later than October 1, 2026. Five months is a reasonable amount of time to implement this service and advance the Commission’s goal of speedy interconnection. Indeed, PJM itself has been working on such a service since at least March 2025 when it suggested its “Option 7” in its Answer to the February 2025 Show Cause Order.27 Underscoring that there is no barrier to timely implementation, the Southwest Power Pool (“SPP”) recently committed to making its version of Interim NITS, Conditional High Impact Large Loads Service (“CHILLS”), available on July 1 of this year, less than five months after filing its revised tariff proposing the service and less than three months after SPP’s requested action date by the Commission.28 PJM should be expected to act with comparable urgency.

A7.
In electric system ratemaking, regulators primarily allocate costs based on “cost 4

causation” principles. In other words, regulators allocate costs based on how 5

customers cause the utility to incur costs. As the National Association of Regulatory 6

Utility Commissioners (“NARUC”) explains, cost allocation should “attempt to 7

customers. Demand-classified costs are driven by peak demand of all customers. 11

Customer-classified costs are driven by the existence of the system and usually 12

scale with the number of customers. In other words, customer-classified costs are 13

costs that the utility incurs regardless of the energy or demand imposed by its 14

customers.

Q32. WHAT ARE THE IMPACTS OF A GRC THAT IS TOO HIGH?
10
A32. Customers that pay the GRC by definition do not impose any incremental cost on 11

the transmission system. Thus, all GRC charges that the Transmission Owners 12

collect are used to directly reduce the NITS rate for all other customers.28 In this 13

regard, it may seem that a higher GRC rate would yield higher GRC revenues and 14

thus higher reductions to the NITS rate for all other customers. However, basic 15

economics tells us that this is not necessarily the case. As Art Laffer artfully 16

demonstrated, higher tax rates can actually yield lower tax revenues to the extent 17

that they discourage work and investment.29
18
In the case of the GRC, inappropriately increasing the rate to too high of a 19

level could have the effect of reducing absolute GRC revenues for two reasons. 20

First, it could discourage the development of co-located load in the PJM region. 21
