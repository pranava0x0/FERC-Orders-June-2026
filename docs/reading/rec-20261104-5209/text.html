I. PRELIMINARY ANSWER OPPOSING FAST-TRACK PROCESSING
The Complaint requests fast-track processing of its Complaint and asks the Commission to find that the existing three-year forward procurement period employed in PJM’s Reliability Pricing Model is no longer just and reasonable and to replace it with a “prompt” auction construct.  However, the Complaint fails to satisfy the stringent standard necessary to justify fast-track processing.  Commission precedent makes clear that fast- track procedures are generally reserved for comparatively straightforward tariff disputes and are not well suited to proceedings involving complex or far-reaching policy issues.5 To qualify for fast-track processing, a complainant must present “a highly credible claim and persuasive showing that standard processes will not be capable of resolving the complaint promptly enough to provide meaningful relief.”6  Because expedited schedules impose a substantial burden on the parties and can strain the Commission’s resources, fast- track processing is appropriately reserved for only the most limited circumstances.

A. Legal Consequences Do Not Attach Merely When Planning Parameters Are Posted.

Exelon’s request for fast-track processing rests entirely on its assertion that the Commission should issue an order before PJM posts the planning parameters for the

PDF page 6

6
2030/2031 Base Residual Auction on January 31, 2027.  Implicit in that argument is the suggestion that Commission action after that date could run afoul of the prohibition against retroactive ratemaking.  That suggestion is unfounded.

Both the Commission and the courts evaluate retroactivity under the rubric
established by the Supreme Court in Landgraf v. USI Film Products.12  The salient question under Landgraf is at what point in the auction process would a change in the capacity auction rules attach new legal consequences.13  As the Supreme Court explained, a rule is impermissibly retroactive where it “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed.”14  Applying those principles in the capacity market context, the critical question is not when planning parameters are posted, but when Market Participants take action in reliance on those parameters that carries legal consequences. Those consequences do not arise merely because PJM posts planning parameters.  Rather, they arise when Market Participants submit binding offers into the auction.  Indeed, PJM’s Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) already provides express authority to update the planning parameters in certain circumstances.15

The Third Circuit’s decision in PJM Power Providers Group v. FERC confirms this distinction.16  There, the court held that a tariff revision approved by the Commission during the conduct of a capacity auction was impermissibly retroactive because PJM had already opened the offer window and Market Participants had submitted binding offers based on the previously posted planning parameters.17  Under those circumstances, the Commission’s action altered the legal consequences of conduct that had already occurred, thereby “nullif[ying] a legal consequence attached to a past action taken in the Auction.”18 Significantly, the court did not hold that legal consequences attach upon the mere posting of planning parameters.  To the contrary, the court’s reasoning focused on the fact that Market Participants had already taken binding action in reliance on those parameters.  The prohibition against retroactive ratemaking therefore protects reliance interests that arise once Capacity Market Sellers submit binding offers, not merely expectations that may exist before any such action occurs.19

This distinction reflects the underlying purpose of the filed-rate doctrine and the rule against retroactive ratemaking, which is to promote stability and predictability in regulated markets.20  While the posting of planning parameters may inform market expectations, expectations alone do not create vested rights or legal consequences. B. Market Participants Receive Adequate Prior Notice Upon Establishment of the Refund Effective Date So Any Posted Planning Parameters May Be

Subject to Revision Under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act

Apart from the fact that no legal consequences attach merely upon the posting of planning parameters, Exelon brought this proceeding under section 206 of the FPA.  Under the statutory framework established by Congress, the FPA expressly contemplates that rates, rules, and practices subject to a section 206 complaint remain subject to Commission review and potential modification following the establishment of a refund effective date. Upon the filing of a complaint and the establishment of a refund effective date, Market Participants are placed on notice that the challenged rate, term, or condition may ultimately be found unjust and unreasonable and that the Commission may order prospective relief, as well as refunds dating back to the refund effective date.22

The D.C. Circuit recently reaffirmed this principle, explaining that section 206(b) operates as a statutory “exception to the filed-rate doctrine’s general prohibition of retroactive rate modifications” and authorizes the Commission to provide relief reaching back to the refund effective date established upon the filing of a complaint.23  There, the court emphasized that the refund effective date serves a critical notice function because it informs Market Participants that the challenged tariff provision is subject to potential Commission revision and refund liability from that point forward.24

Thus, even assuming, arguedo, that the legal consequences attach when planning parameters are posted, section 206 independently defeats any claim that Commission action after that date would constitute impermissible retroactive ratemaking.  The D.C. Circuit has expressly rejected the notion that the filed-rate doctrine bars Commission action under section 206 of the FPA.25  That is, Congress specifically authorized the Commission, through section 206, to modify existing rates and provide refund relief after establishing a refund effective date, thereby placing all affected parties on notice that the challenged rate structure remains provisional pending resolution of the complaint.  To be clear, the Commission has historically been reluctant to order refunds with respect to final market results, and PJM has opposed such requests for refund relief.  PJM references the Court’s decision solely to demonstrate that the Commission need not issue an order on the underlying complaint prior to PJM posting the planning parameters.

C. PJM is Scheduled to Post Planning Parameters By March 1, 2027 for the 2030/2031 Base Residual Auction.

Further undermining Exelon’s claim of urgency, the Commission recently accepted Tariff revisions that require PJM to post the PJM Region Reliability Requirement and the Locational Deliverability Area Reliability Requirement by March 1 preceding each Base Residual Auction.26  Consistent with that order and the updated Tariff,27 PJM will revise its pre-auction schedule and now anticipates posting the planning parameters for the 2030/2031 Base Residual Auction on or before March 1, 2027.  Accordingly, even if the Commission were to conclude that action should be taken before the planning parameters for the 2030/2031 Base Residual Auction are posted, the Commission would have until February 28, 2027, rather than January 31, 2027, to act (i.e., four extra weeks).  Exelon’s asserted deadline therefore no longer reflects PJM’s anticipated pre-auction schedule for posting the planning parameters.
