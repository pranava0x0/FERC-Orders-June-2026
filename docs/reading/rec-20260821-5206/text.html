Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission (“Commission”),1 Google LLC (“Google”) hereby submits these
comments regarding PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) Proposal.  Google commends PJM for taking steps to ensure reliability, which must remain its first priority.  Nevertheless, while RBP may help maintain near-term resource adequacy, Google urges PJM to focus future efforts on reforms that incentivize new large loads to procure and bring their own new generation rather than relying on an interim, one-time solicitation, like RBP.  As explained below, Google believes that this bilateral contracting will do more to procure the necessary supply—after all, there is no guarantee that RBP will procure enough GW to cover the shortfall2—and do so in a way that meets future demand on a durable, forward-looking basis, rather than relying on measures that address only previously identified reliability needs.  A bilateral approach also does more to ensure the new supply meets the needs and preferences of new large load customers, unlocking the economic growth benefits that they can provide. I. WHO WE ARE
Google’s mission is to organize the world’s information and make it universally accessible and useful.  AI is critical to that mission.  Data centers necessary to serve Google’s customers consume a large amount of electricity and require reliable electric service to operate.  As Google’s business grows, so too does its demand for reliable, affordable, and clean electricity.

II. COMMENTS
Google recognizes that the unprecedented load growth that PJM is currently experiencing is both a tremendous opportunity and a complex challenge.  The load growth reflects the dynamism of the nation’s technology sector, which can drive economic growth, create jobs, and significantly expand the tax base of the areas in which that growth occurs.  Those are real, verifiable benefits that accrue across the local, state, and national levels, and should be available to the communities that want to host the necessary digital and energy infrastructure. But those economic benefits must not come at the cost of sacrificing the reliability and affordability to which customers in those communities, and PJM more broadly, are rightly accustomed.  RBP may help protect against that result in the short term by procuring additional

1. A Viable Long-Term Solution to PJM’s Capacity Shortfall is a Bring Your Own New Capacity Framework that Protects Existing Ratepayers from Cost Shifts and

Stranded Asset Risk
Google has long advocated for the principle that new large loads should pay for the costs associated with the increase in demand that they are driving and the strain that puts on existing

At the federal level, Google has consistently argued that new large loads must bring their own new generation to support the demands they place on existing electrical infrastructure, where existing structures, like the Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”), have not secured adequate new supply.8  Although not a silver bullet, requiring new large loads that interconnect to the grid to secure their own capacity is the single most important mechanism for shielding existing customers from adverse rate impacts of those new large loads.  Indeed, from the outset of PJM’s first Critical Issue Fast Path (“CIFP”) focused on large load issues, Google has supported this concept, including through a BYONC framework with an accelerated pathway for new generation and new load to interconnect to the transmission system.9

2. The Costs of the RBP Must Be Allocated in a Manner that Complies with Cost
Causation, the CCF, the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, and the Statement of
Principles from the NEDC and the PJM Governors
As noted above, Google recognizes that RBP may be a reasonable response to the current resource adequacy shortfall, but it should not become a permanent substitute for market-driven, long-term mechanisms that deliver resource adequacy at just and reasonable rates.  Indeed, Google is concerned that, as things stand, RBP could lead to the sort of cost shifting that Google has long advocated against and that would be inconsistent with both the Ratepayer Protection Pledge and the National Energy Dominance Council and PJM Governors’ Statement of Principles.17  Because not all states in PJM have separate mechanisms for allocating costs directly to large loads, there is no way to ensure that the costs of RBP can be allocated exclusively to those that have not already covered their load.  It should be incumbent on PJM to proactively coordinate with states to ensure that they have the retail mechanisms in place to properly allocate to new large loads the costs that RBP would impose.

To that end, Google applauds and supports the collaborative effort announced by the Virginia State Corporation Commission (“VA SCC”) and the Data Center Coalition (“DCC”) to develop model state tariff provisions addressing the retail allocation of RBP costs, among other necessary criteria (e.g., geographic matching of load and supply).  Such provisions are designed to assign RBP costs to the large load customers whose demand causes the need for those

3. PJM Must Make Progress to Address the Additional Factors Contributing to the Current Resource Adequacy Shortfall

Reforms that require new large loads to secure their own new capacity are a critical element of ensuring reliability and affordability.  Yet they are only part of the answer.  As Google has consistently maintained, one of the chief barriers to the integration of new large loads in a manner that does not undermine affordability and reliability are long interconnection timelines and the “[t]erawatts of new generation capacity [that] are stuck in interconnection queues because inadequate transmission infrastructure” renders interconnection too slow, too expensive, or both.19 New large loads cannot bring forth new capacity resources if those resources’ interconnection to the transmission system is stymied or delayed due to unpredictable—and often high—network upgrade costs or delays in identifying or developing that infrastructure.

Further, PJM’s existing rules also unnecessarily limit the ability of new storage resources to use Surplus Interconnection Service (“SIS”) to provide additional capacity, even where Capacity Interconnection Rights (“CIRs”) at the same point of interconnection are underutilized.  Where storage is added to an existing inverter-based resource, PJM requires the resources to operate as a single hybrid resource for the storage to access those CIRs, which can significantly limit the commercial viability of the arrangement.20  And where storage is added to a non-inverter-based resource, such as a gas generator, PJM’s mixed-technology facility rules do not permit the storage resource to use those CIRs to increase the facility’s unforced capacity (“UCAP”) contribution at all.

These barriers could be addressed through a relatively straightforward rule change allowing an existing resource with underutilized CIRs to allocate a portion of those rights to a new resource interconnecting through SIS.  Because the surplus resource must be studied before receiving SIS, PJM can identify and impose any necessary restrictions through that process to address reliability, operational, or market-power concerns.  Such a reform could unlock significant amounts of additional capacity from storage using existing interconnection capability and allow that capacity to come online more quickly.

Google intends to work with PJM and its stakeholders to address these barriers promptly. Although Google recognizes the significant progress that PJM has made and continues to pursue on interconnection reform, these issues remain a major reason why, at the moment, supply is not keeping up with demand.  Resolving that imbalance also requires coordinated, holistic transmission planning processes that reflect the growing demands placed on the grid by both new demand and new supply.  Given the scale, pace, and volume of large load interconnection requests as well as the corresponding scale, pace, and volume of new generator interconnection requests— both of which contribute significantly to the need for new transmission infrastructure— transmission planners must have a realistic picture of the generators and large loads planning to interconnect to the transmission grid so that they can develop the transmission system that accommodates both sides of the supply and demand equation.  Only then will PJM have the infrastructure foundation needed to maintain resource adequacy at just and reasonable rates and capitalize on the economic opportunity that load growth presents without succumbing to its challenges.

III. CONCLUSION
PJM is right to focus on ensuring reliability and affordability.  However, a more enduring structural approach is preferable to perpetual reliance on RBP or similar stopgap mechanisms. BYONC frameworks can unlock more capacity, do so in advance of the relevant reliability needs, and automatically ensure that the costs of new demand are borne by the entities that cause those costs.  That is a much better result for PJM and all its stakeholders.  Accordingly, BYONC should form the foundation for future PJM and Commission efforts to address the demand-driven effects of new large loads.
