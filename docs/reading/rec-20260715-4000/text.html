I. Introduction and Summary
The United States has before it today one of the greatest requirements for energy development in generations. This required energy development is largely driven by the national interest in being the worldwide leader in artificial intelligence infrastructure and technology. Addressing this national interest is essential to national security and presents a tremendous opportunity to drive economic growth. At its core, AEP’s concern with PJM is that it is dysfunctional, ineffective, and not capable of doing its job of supporting the deployment of massive amounts of needed generation and transmission by its members. In short, PJM is broken and must be immediately fixed.

To avoid the catastrophic effects of jeopardizing our national security and economic growth, PJM must immediately change. PJM must change its operations and governance to allow customers to interconnect load and generation faster. PJM must have a planning process that takes transmission and generation into account together. That planning process must identify and provide sufficient resources to meet the needs of existing and future customers. Finally, PJM governance must change so stakeholders can be heard, innovation can occur, decisions can be made, and PJM can move forward and get out of the deadlock it faces today.

AEP proposes three overarching areas of change for PJM. First, generator interconnection must be reformed in PJM. Under current interconnection rules, a generator cannot reach commercial operation without significant delays and inefficiencies. This discourages generation development. Second, PJM’s approach to ensuring resource adequacy in the region must be updated. PJM’s Reliability Pricing Model has failed to send efficient investment and retirement signals which has distorted price formation. The result is diminished long-term revenue certainty for generation developers which discourages investment in dependable, dispatchable generation and undermines resource adequacy over time. Third, PJM governance and stakeholder processes

must be reformed. PJM is seeking to solve today’s challenges with an outdated Board of Managers (“Board”) governance and stakeholder process where necessary reforms cannot be implemented efficiently and effectively.  Too often, reforms are stymied by a stakeholder process that consistently deadlocks on important issues and a Board governance model lacking clear accountability and the will to drive change.

The need for change in these three areas is urgent. PJM is confronting the most significant reliability, affordability, and infrastructure challenge in its history. Demand from national security needs, advanced manufacturing, data centers, and electrification is accelerating faster than the region is able to plan and build the infrastructure needed to support it through PJM’s current mechanisms. The question before the Commission is whether PJM’s Board governance structure and stakeholder process enable timely, technically sound decisions that maintain reliability, support affordability, and enable economic growth. In AEP’s view, they do not. PJM suffers from a governance mismatch: responsibility for outcomes rests with the Board, but authority over many of the tools needed to achieve those outcomes remains fragmented. PJM’s existing governance framework has not consistently enabled timely action to meet customer needs or address evolving reliability, planning, and market challenges. That is not a sustainable near-term or long-term operating condition for the region. PJM’s governance structure must align responsibility, authority, and accountability so that those charged with ensuring reliability have the ability—and the obligation—to deliver it. PJM states, accountable to their citizens, lack any formal role. While every stakeholder deserves a voice, governance must better reflect who is responsible for planning, financing, operating, and maintaining the infrastructure upon which reliability depends, and solving the urgent generator interconnection and resource adequacy issues.

Effective governance is not measured by the number of committees or the degree of stakeholder participation. It is measured by whether responsibility, authority, and accountability are aligned to produce timely decisions that maintain reliability, support affordability, and enable fast generation interconnection to support national security and economic growth. It is ultimately measured by customer outcomes: Do the lights stay on, is growth supported on the customer’s development timeline, and do planning processes stay ahead of demand so growth can occur and important national interests like security and AI development can move forward unconstrained? Reliable, affordable, and efficient customer service form the foundation of AEP’s recommendations.

II. How Process Thwarts Substance in PJM—An Illustration
Today’s stakeholder process makes it nearly impossible to evaluate integrated solutions that span the two areas most critically in need of change—generation interconnection and resource adequacy—as well as related areas of load interconnection and market design. A reformed process must enable stakeholders to bring creative proposals designed to solve PJM’s biggest problems to the table for serious review by other stakeholders, PJM management, and the Board. The current PJM stakeholder process does not encourage new innovative ideas and the structure of the committee system complicates needed market reforms that hope to solve some of today’s greatest challenges. As far back as December of 2024, the PJM Board publicly acknowledged that they foresaw immediate capacity shortage conditions in the 2026/27 Delivery Year forecast. With an unprecedented challenge that should have prompted bold action, PJM proposed only minor changes.  This was, in part, due to the byzantine nature of the stakeholder process and an inability to bring about consensus from its membership. When faced with today’s unprecedented challenges, AEP believes that instead of minor tweaks, PJM should be empowered

to move forward boldly with solutions which may not gain the support of entrenched market participants who benefit from the status quo.

One such proposal that helps to demonstrate this archaic stakeholder misalignment within the PJM governance process is AEP’s Accelerated Load, Infrastructure, Generation and Network Planning (“ALIGN”) concept. AEP’s ALIGN concept is for PJM to consolidate planning so that committed load, generation resources, and transmission facilities would be planned and evaluated as a coordinated portfolio rather than through separate and sequential processes. Eligibility would be limited to projects supported by executed agreements, financial security, and other enforceable commitments, thereby helping distinguish executable projects from speculative queue activity. ALIGN would also augment PJM’s existing planning and interconnection processes, not replace them. By concentrating planning studies on committed demand, the framework is intended to improve queue integrity, increase planning certainty, and better align infrastructure investment with actual customer need.

AEP’s ALIGN proposal illustrates why governance reform matters: today’s stakeholder process could require the proposal to be fragmented across multiple committees and multiple filing authorities before its merits could even be evaluated. Despite working as an integrated framework, the proposal could be broken up among stakeholder groups focusing on generator interconnection, load interconnection, transmission planning, and others where aspects would be debated in isolation. Then, if the proposal advanced, because it would require both Tariff and Operating Agreement revisions, a two-sector Members Committee veto could block the whole concept. A well-formed stakeholder process must not stifle big, innovative ideas; it must facilitate their consideration on the merits.

AEP’s ALIGN concept is not without precedent. Other RTOs have been able to
successfully incorporate and demonstrate the value of integrated planning processes that evaluate multiple infrastructure needs together rather than through disconnected stakeholder processes. SPP was able to incorporate its Integrated Transmission Planning process which evaluates reliability, economic, and public policy needs within a single coordinated planning framework. ALIGN applies that same planning philosophy to today’s emerging challenges and takes it one step further. Rather than evaluating committed load, generation, and transmission through separate interconnection queues, planning processes, and stakeholder committees, ALIGN would enable PJM to evaluate these interdependent facilities as a coordinated infrastructure portfolio. The lesson is not that PJM should replicate another RTO’s planning process. Rather, it is that governance must enable planners and stakeholders to evaluate comprehensive infrastructure solutions that reflect how the electric system actually functions. As load growth accelerates and infrastructure development becomes increasingly complex, PJM’s governance structure must evolve to support the innovation that will be required to meet the needs of today and the next century. The purpose of governance reform is to enable technically superior solutions by aligning responsibility, authority, and accountability.

III. PJM And Its Stakeholders: Reform Is Needed To Facilitate Action
PJM’s immediate market and planning issues must be addressed through the most effective near-term mechanisms available, but the underlying governance deficiencies also must be addressed to place PJM, its members, and its states on a more durable path. A stakeholder framework that clearly allocates responsibility, facilitates the Board’s work, and enables timely Commission review will better support the Board’s independence, responsiveness, and ability to act—producing better results for reliability, infrastructure development, and market reforms over the long term.

Specifically, AEP recommends PJM create an advisory stakeholder structure aligning the advisory function with an appropriate process to enable stakeholders to provide better, actionable advice to the Board. This will restore balance to PJM’s governance structure and unlock the Board’s ability to make decisions and address the most important issues proactively, nimbly, and decisively.

While an advisory stakeholder process and an empowered board are ideas borrowed from some other RTOs’ approaches, PJM’s size, diversity of states, and range of regulatory paradigms distinguish it. The Commission’s mantra—that one-size-fits-all approaches do not work—rings true here. AEP’s goal is not for PJM to adopt any particular structure for its own sake, but to achieve a balance that is fair and sensible for all stakeholders, including by appropriately reflecting the interests of parties with responsibility for, and exposure to, reliability and cost impacts in the region. And critically, PJM needs a structure that enables the Board to make timely decisions in support of national security, reliability, affordability, and economic development.

VI. Conclusion
PJM stakeholders—from transmission owners and generators to states and consumer advocates—are committed to serving customers across the region reliably, at least cost. But too often, PJM’s governance structure has inhibited, rather than facilitated, this essential work. Action takes too long, even as problems proliferate, and the solutions adopted do not meet the moment. PJM’s structure handcuffs the Board from leading from the front and mires stakeholders with process over substance. “[T]he status quo,” as Chairman Swett has put it, “is unacceptable and has been for some time.”4  To secure the greatest benefits for the 67 million customers who rely on PJM, the Board must be able to act proactively, nimbly, and decisively with useful input from stakeholders to solve the region’s challenges.

PJM’s governance structure was developed for a different era—one characterized by slower load growth, excess generation, and incremental infrastructure needs. Today’s environment is fundamentally different. Meeting the nation’s growing demand for electricity will require an RTO capable of making timely, informed, and accountable decisions. Governance reform must not seek to diminish stakeholder participation, but rather ensure that stakeholder engagement produces actionable advice, that the Board possesses the authority necessary to fulfill its statutory responsibilities, and that states have a meaningful voice in the process. The next generation of grid challenges will not be solved by incremental procedural reforms. They will require governance capable of evaluating integrated technical solutions such as ALIGN, empowering the Board to act, incorporating meaningful stakeholder and state input, and aligning responsibility, authority and accountability.

1. What is the PJM Board’s role in ensuring that PJM is sufficiently tackling the region’s operational and market challenges? How does the Board evaluate its effectiveness in that role? How often, and under what circumstances, does the Board act unilaterally, and should this change?

The resource adequacy challenges facing PJM are existential. Its fundamental charge is to serve customers reliably. Despite a half-decade of lead time, the issue is still not under control. This struggle has revealed that the Board lacks a consistent ability to take timely action to meet customer needs or address evolving national security matters, reliability, planning, and market challenges. The Board has been forced to be reactive and defensive rather than nimble and decisive, preventing timely action. AEP believes the cause is an imbalance among the core goals that RTOs under Order No. 2000 must pursue: independence, responsiveness, and action.

Those decisions, however, need not and must not be made in isolation; they must be made based on experience, a robust understanding of the substance, and full consideration of diverse stakeholders’ needs. 2. How should states, through the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (OPSI) or other structures, interface with the Board to enable the region to meet operational and market challenges while respecting PJM’s independence?

States must have an expanded and more formal role in PJM’s governance process—

These issues have become critically important challenges within the PJM region and are directly impacted by PJM decisions and policies. Yet states do not have any formal role in PJM governance, either individually or via the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”). States should be able to act as a key voice in promoting innovative, integrated solutions that they see as valuable for addressing today’s issues. 6. Recognizing regional differences, are there lessons from other regional transmission organization/independent system operator (RTO/ISO) governance structures that PJM could reasonably adopt to improve its responsiveness to the region’s operational and market challenges?

AEP recommends five targeted modifications to PJM’s governance rules that would strengthen the PJM Board’s effectiveness while preserving its independence and responsiveness. These recommendations—which draw on approaches adopted by other RTOs and ISOs to improve governance—are discussed in greater detail below. Collectively, these reforms would modernize PJM governance by strengthening the connection between Board oversight and operational execution, improving the Board’s understanding of the unique and varied conditions within the PJM region, expanding the pool of qualified Board candidates, protecting against entrenchment and status quo bias in the Board nomination process, and allowing the Board to engage productively with stakeholders to effectively guide PJM and confront the complex challenges facing the region’s electric industry today. The common lesson across other RTOs is not identical governance structures, but rather that governance must align responsibility, authority, and accountability while enabling innovation.

Indeed, the CEOs of MISO, SPP, and NYISO are voting members of their respective RTO/ISOs’ boards (subject to certain recusal limits).26 Giving PJM’s President and CEO a vote on the Board would make PJM more dynamic by improving information flow, enhancing Board debate, and making the President/CEO a decision-making partner.

In-region preference for Board candidates. PJM must adopt a preference for Board candidates who reside within the PJM footprint or have significant professional experience with the region’s electric system. Although PJM’s Operating Agreement imposes certain minimum expertise requirements that the Board collectively must meet, it does not require or prioritize familiarity with the PJM region.27 Accordingly, few current Board members had ties to the PJM region before joining the Board. Because Board decisions directly affect national security, electric reliability, affordability, and economic development across the PJM footprint, regional knowledge must be recognized as an important qualification for Board members. Notably, both ISO-NE and NYISO currently have preferences for in-region directors.28 Regional knowledge is particularly important in PJM, which has a uniquely large and diverse footprint that includes both vertically integrated and restructured states with significant variation in local supply and demand dynamics. Shorter cooling-off period for prospective Board members. Limiting PJM’s “cooling-off”
period for prospective Board members to one year also would improve PJM’s ability to attract qualified Board candidates with relevant experience and local knowledge. PJM’s rules require at least a two-year gap between service as a director, officer, or employee of a PJM member (or an affiliate or related party) and election to the PJM Board.30  This restriction unnecessarily limits the pool of qualified candidates with recent industry experience. A one-year period would maintain Board independence31 while improving PJM’s ability to recruit members with current expertise. This adjustment would more closely align PJM’s governance rules with those of other RTOs. MISO in 2017 reduced its cooling-off period from two years to one, explaining that the longer restriction had “affected MISO’s ability to recruit Board candidates” as it could “often times create conflicts that … disqualify an individual from consideration.”32 ISO-NE and SPP have no cooling- off periods for board members.33

Reduce Board control over nomination process. PJM must limit the role incumbent Board
members play in the Board nomination process. Under current rules, PJM’s Nominating
Committee—which nominates Board candidates to be elected by the Members Committee—
includes three incumbent Board members (one of whom must not vote on the Nominating
Committee) and five sectoral representatives. 34  This structure gives substantial influence to incumbent Board members and creates—even if unintentionally—a thumb on the scale favoring

the status quo. Corporate governance scholars have long recognized that self-perpetuating board nomination processes can lead to detrimental entrenchment and insulation.35 Allowing only one incumbent Board member to serve on the Nominating Committee in a non-voting role would

preserve information flows and representation of the current Board’s perspective in the nomination process while promoting true independence, responsiveness to PJM members, and bolder, more

effective leadership. Notably, two ISOs—CAISO and ERCOT—have board nomination processes
that provide no formal role for incumbent directors.36

Nor are these counterproductive restrictions needed to protect the Board’s independence: No other RTO or ISO imposes such severe restrictions,38 and they do not fit the Board’s role: PJM under the Federal Power Act is a public utility, not a regulator, and we must not foster the misimpression that the Board is akin to another layer of regulation. Reforming PJM’s ex parte rules would improve governance by enabling Board members to receive timely, balanced, and informed perspectives on complex reliability, market, and transmission planning issues.
