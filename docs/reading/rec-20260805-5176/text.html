Larry Fisher, Romney, WV.
PJM REFORM MUST CLOSE THE GAP BETWEEN LOAD, GENERATION, TRANSMISSION AND LAND
I am President of the Quivey’s Grove Property Owners Association, a small private residential community in Hampshire County, West Virginia. A proposed study segment for the Valley North 765-kV transmission project would cross through the center of our community.
I am not an electrical engineer or utility regulator. I am a retired information-technology architect. My concern arises from a familiar systems problem: every participant can perform its assigned function correctly while the complete process still produces a poor result because no one owns the end-to-end decision.
The Commission has asked how PJM can become more accountable, transparent and responsive while giving states and stakeholders a meaningful role. Valley North provides a practical example of why those reforms must address more than the speed of PJM’s decision-making.
The underlying problem is that no participant appears responsible for proving that the combined load, generation, transmission, timing, cost and land-use result is the least costly and least destructive reasonable solution.
I do not oppose data centers, artificial intelligence, necessary generation or necessary transmission. Nor do I claim to have proved that another engineering solution can replace Valley North. I ask FERC to require an accountable process that examines the complete problem before a major greenfield transmission decision becomes effectively irreversible.
HOW THE REGIONAL NEED DEVELOPED
PJM’s January 14, 2026 forecast summary quantifies the “hockey-stick” change: PJM’s 2021 forecast projected average annual summer-peak growth of 0.3% over the following ten years; its 2026 forecast projects 3.6% annually over the next decade—more than ten times as fast—even after PJM reduced its forecast for several near-term years through 2032, in part because of improved vetting of large-load requests.
In its 2022 RTEP Window 3 analysis, PJM considered up to 7,500 MW of new data-center load associated with facilities to be sited in Virginia and Maryland, together with approximately 11,100 MW of announced generator deactivations west and south of the Conastone area.
PJM received 72 transmission proposals. The resulting package included MARL and related reinforcements intended to move more electricity toward Northern Virginia and surrounding load centers.
For its 2024 RTEP Window 1 analysis, PJM modeled approximately 10,000 MW more load for 2029 and approximately 15,000 MW more for 2032 than in earlier cases. PJM attributed most of that increase to data centers, with additional growth from electrification and electric vehicles.
PJM received 94 transmission proposals from 16 entities. Forty of those proposals were primarily greenfield projects.
The selected regional package included Proposal 708, the approximately $1.945 billion John Amos–Welton Spring–Rocky Point 765-kV project now being developed as Valley North. It was part of a larger group of 63 baseline reliability projects totaling approximately $5.9 billion approved by the PJM Board on February 26, 2025.
The developers then began translating PJM’s electrical endpoints into possible geographic routes. That is when the regional planning decision appeared on the ground as study segments crossing West Virginia farms, homes and communities, including Quivey’s Grove.
Then, on July 28, 2026—after PJM had approved Valley North—Dominion announced plans for a new 2.6-GW natural-gas combined-cycle generating station at Mt. Storm, West Virginia. Dominion also stated that the existing 1.6-GW coal-fired Mt. Storm station would continue operating. The proposed new station is expected to begin operating around 2034, subject to permits and regulatory approval.
The later Mt. Storm proposal does not prove that Valley North is unnecessary, nor does it prove that Valley North caused Dominion to select Mt. Storm.
It does, however, expose the planning question:
Would PJM have selected the same transmission solution, of the same size and across the same general geography, if the new Mt. Storm generation and other possible load-generation combinations had been evaluated as part of the same decision?
The publicly available record I have reviewed does not answer that question.
THE MISSING INTEGRATED DECISION
The present process divides responsibility:
•	Local governments approve data centers.
•	Developers select locations and request electric service.
•	Utilities incorporate those requests into load forecasts.
•	Generator owners separately decide whether plants remain open and where new plants may be built.
•	PJM receives those decisions, identifies projected reliability violations and evaluates transmission proposals.
•	State commissions or other state siting authorities later consider whether to approve the proposed facilities and routes under applicable state law.
•	Communities and landowners enter near the end, after the regional need and broad transmission solution have largely taken shape.
PJM is effectively handed one set of assumptions saying, “Add enormous new load here,” and another saying, “Remove generation there.” PJM must then keep the grid reliable.
Transmission becomes the default extension cord because PJM directly controls neither the location of the new load nor many of the generation decisions.
PJM may perform a thorough comparison among transmission proposals without performing—or having authority to perform—a comparable system-wide analysis of whether the transmission requirement could be avoided or reduced through:
•	Dependable generation nearer the load;
•	Locating extraordinary new loads nearer dependable generation;
•	Retaining strategically important existing generation;
•	Upgrading or rebuilding within existing rights-of-way;
•	Advanced conductors;
•	Storage and demand response;
•	Staged, flexible or interruptible service;
•	Reasonable undergrounding; or
•	Delaying unconditional firm service until the required infrastructure is ready.
That is the governance gap FERC should address.
RATEPAYER PROTECTION DOES NOT PROTECT LANDOWNERS
President Trump’s Ratepayer Protection Pledge establishes an important principle: companies creating extraordinary new electric demand should build, bring or buy the power they require and pay for the delivery infrastructure needed to serve them.
Ordinary electric customers should not subsidize private data-center development.
But customer payment does not prove that a project is necessary, properly sized or based upon the least harmful reasonable solution. Payment answers who writes the construction check.
If data centers pay the utility’s construction bill, ratepayers may be declared protected while rural families absorb losses involving land, property value, privacy, security, views and community character to transmission lines required to provide the power.
That is not full cost. It is cost shifting to people whose losses do not appear on a utility invoice.
Avoidance must therefore come before compensation. Unnecessary impacts should be avoided, unavoidable impacts minimized, and affected owners fully compensated only after the need and alternatives have been fairly examined.
WHAT FERC SHOULD REQUIRE
I am not asking FERC to adopt my engineering solution or select a transmission route. I am asking FERC to use its authority over PJM’s governance, tariffs and regional planning requirements to ensure that someone is accountable for evaluating the complete problem.
That process should require:
1.	AN INTEGRATED ALTERNATIVES RECORD
Before selecting major greenfield transmission, the record should show that load location, load readiness, generation, retirements, existing transmission capacity, existing rights-of-way, storage, flexibility, staging and new transmission were evaluated as parts of one system.
2.	AN ACCOUNTABLE DECISION-MAKER
The process should identify who is responsible for determining that the combined result is reliable, reasonably economical and the least destructive reasonable alternative.
That responsibility should not disappear into the boundaries among utilities, generator owners, PJM, FERC and state regulators.
3.	EARLY STATE AND COMMUNITY PARTICIPATION
States, consumer advocates, local governments and representatives of affected communities should enter while meaningful alternatives remain available—not only after the broad electrical solution has been selected and routing has begun.
4.	PERIODIC REVALIDATION
Load commitments, generator retirements, new generation, technological developments and project timing should be retested before major property acquisition and construction.
A project justified by one forecast should not proceed automatically if the underlying facts materially change.
5.	CLEAR RESPONSIBILITY FOR FORECAST RISK
The customer creating the extraordinary load should bear the financial risk if that load is delayed, reduced or never constructed.
Ratepayers should not inherit stranded costs, and landowners should not permanently surrender property for infrastructure that updated analysis no longer supports.
CONCLUSION
FERC’s objective should not be simply to make PJM’s existing process faster or give PJM more unilateral authority.
A faster fragmented process is still fragmented—and may merely reach an incomplete decision sooner.
FERC should enforce coordination, not take control of the land.
I cannot establish that another solution will eliminate Valley North. But neither should affected landowners be required to design an alternative regional grid before questioning whether the responsible institutions ever evaluated the complete load-generation-transmission-land problem as one system.
First determine what is reasonably necessary. Then determine who should pay for it. Before either decision becomes irreversible, require someone to examine and accept responsibility for the complete result.

Respectfully,
 Larry Fisher
