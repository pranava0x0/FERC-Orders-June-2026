In accordance with the July 30, 2026 notice issued by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or the “Commission”),1 the Electric Power Supply Association (“EPSA”)2 submits these post-conference comments following up on the Chairman- and Commissioner-led technical conference held on July 23, 2026 (the “Technical Conference”), to discuss the governance and stakeholder processes of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).  EPSA appreciates the Commission’s efforts to address these matters and this opportunity for input on the important issues discussed at the Technical Conference.  EPSA is submitting these comments to address two questions posed by the Commission about the allocation of filing rights under Section 205 of the Federal Power Act (the “FPA”),3 and to underscore the critical importance of ensuring that any attempted reallocation of such rights is fully in accordance with the statutory framework of the FPA. EPSA is particularly concerned by suggestions that Section 205 filing rights could be transferred or delegated to states or a state entity like the Organization of PJM States Inc (“OPSI”), which are not public utilities.  EPSA is also concerned about suggestions that the Commission ought to upset the bargain struck when PJM was formed, under which consent from its members, including power suppliers, was required under PJM’s Amended and Restated Operating Agreement (the “Operating Agreement”) for Section 205 filings with respect to certain matters.  Specifically, while PJM may be dissatisfied with the need for member approval for Section 205 filings with respect to rules for the energy and ancillary services markets,4 there is no basis for the Commission to disrupt the agreement that was the basis for PJM’s formation or to otherwise give PJM the ability to usurp the filing rights of the suppliers that have real “skin in the game” and who will be relied on to make needed investments in the coming years.

DPU a state commission ordered the Western Massachusetts Electric Company, a public utility under the FPA, to make a Section 205 filing to change a rate-setting practice objectionable to the state commission.  The public utility dutifully made the filing, but FERC rejected it, finding that Section 205 “governs changes that the utility itself proposes, not those that a state regulator requires it to propose.”22  As future Supreme Court Justice Breyer summarized the FERC rejection: “If Massachusetts objects to the rule, says FERC, it should file a complaint in accordance with [Section 206].”23  The court agreed with FERC.  Under Breyer’s reasoning, Section 205 filings are limited to when a public utility seeks to change its tariff for reasons of its own, not when a public utility desires no changes, but a state commission does.  As Breyer put it, Section 205 “governs changes that the utility itself proposes, not those that a state regulator requires it to propose.”24
