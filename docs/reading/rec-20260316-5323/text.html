COMMENTS OF THE DATA CENTER COALITION
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“Commission”)1, the Data Center Coalition (“DCC”), respectfully submits these formal comments in response to PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) February 23, 2026 tariff filing in compliance (“PJM’s Compliance Filings”) to the Commission's December 18, 2025 order.2

I.​
Introduction
DCC respectfully submits these comments on PJM’s proposed revisions to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff to comply with the Commission’s directives in its Order to Show Cause to (1) revise its behind-the-meter generation Tariff provisions, (2) establish three new transmission services for Eligible Customers seeking to take service on behalf of Co- Located Load, and (3) revise its necessary studies processes, among other changes. This proceeding arises at a pivotal moment for the nation’s energy grid and digital infrastructure, as regional grid operators face rising demand, constrained transmission capacity, increased local opposition to infrastructure development, and growing uncertainty around supply adequacy.

DCC is the membership organization for the U.S. data center industry, representing leading data center owners and operators with infrastructure and employees across the country.3 DCC member companies are making significant, multi-billion dollar investments in America’s infrastructure. These investments support hundreds of thousands of quality jobs across the nation and contribute billions of dollars in local, state, and federal tax revenue, providing consistent funding for important community priorities like public safety, education, and transportation. Between 2017 and 2023, the data center industry’s total contribution to U.S. GDP was $3.5 trillion. In 2023, the U.S. data center industry directly employed more than 600,000 workers and supported 4.7 million jobs in total. The sector generated $404 billion in total labor income and contributed $162.7 billion in federal, state, and local taxes in 2023.4

Data centers are foundational to the modern economy and the digital services on which it depends. They support essential services across nearly every sector, including finance, healthcare, education, communications, manufacturing, defense, and government. As the backbone of the nation’s digital infrastructure, data centers host the computing platforms that power artificial intelligence and machine learning workloads, cybersecurity systems that protect critical institutions, and data processing capabilities relied upon by federal agencies and national security entities. These facilities support real-time digital operations that are now inseparable from the functioning of core public and private systems.

In recent years, energy demand has accelerated—driven by Artificial Intelligence (“AI”) development, cloud computing, electrification of buildings, industry and transportation, hydrogen fuel production, and the reshoring of U.S. advanced manufacturing. Data center operators are responding with urgency—but face growing constraints from generator interconnection delays, opaque infrastructure planning, and limited transparency into available capacity.

In this context, co-location of load with generation—whether front-of-the-meter or behind-the-meter—is a pragmatic, common, and commercially legitimate strategy to secure timely access to power as markets grapple with years-long interconnection queue backlogs. These arrangements provide critical flexibility and efficiency, enabling data center developers to meet emerging needs on faster timelines and without undue reliance on already-congested grid infrastructure should they pursue this option.

The Commission has the opportunity in this proceeding to ensure PJM is complying with its Order to Show Cause and ensure co-location arrangements are a viable pathway in the future for large load customers. To assist in that effort, these comments: (1) provide an overview of PJM’s Compliance Filing; and (2) point to the unworkable nature of PJM’s compliance filing and recommend solutions.

II.​
PJM’s Compliance Filing
In response to the Order to Show Cause, PJM filed revisions to its Tariff addressing Retail Behind-the-Meter Generation (BTMG) and transmission service for large loads. The proposal establishes a 50 MW materiality threshold for Retail BTMG, under which facilities with a cumulative nameplate rating of 50 MW or less may qualify for retail netting treatment. PJM indicated this threshold aligns with its definition of large load additions and reflects stakeholder feedback. The filing also includes an emergency generation exemption, which excludes on-site backup generators from the BTMG calculation so that facilities such as data centers can maintain 3

diesel or natural gas backup generation for reliability purposes, provided those units are not used for routine retail netting.

PJM also proposes grandfathering provisions intended to protect existing arrangements. Facilities with BTMG capacity exceeding 50 MW may be permanently grandfathered if they operate under a contractual arrangement that was in effect before December 18, 2025. In addition, the proposal exempts certain Qualifying Facilities that achieved operational status before that date under the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978. PJM stated these provisions are designed to avoid disrupting existing investments while establishing clearer limits for future projects.

Finally, PJM proposed three new transmission service options for Eligible Customers. Firm Contract Demand Transmission Service would allow customers to receive service up to a defined maximum megawatt level. Non-Firm Contract Demand Transmission Service would allow co-located loads to withdraw power from the grid on an as-needed and as-available basis. PJM also proposed Interim Network Integration Transmission Service (“Interim NITS”), which would allow customers that have requested full Network Integration Transmission Service (“NITS”) to begin operations on a non-firm, interruptible basis while transmission upgrades necessary for long-term firm service are constructed. These proposed service options form the basis of DCC’s concerns discussed below.

III.​
DCC Comments
Co-location is a commercial arrangement that enables load and generation to be sited together in ways that facilitate timely power delivery, reduce dependence on constrained transmission systems, and enhance investment certainty. These arrangements involve sophisticated engineering, contractual, and regulatory compliance components. When properly 4

structured, they can preserve and improve overall grid reliability while expanding the set of viable options available to new large loads.

DCC has previously urged the Commission to direct PJM to develop clear, enforceable tariff language that affirms the permissibility of multiple co-location configurations, including those where load is served directly from a generation resource located on or near the same site. The Commission did just that with its Order to Show Cause. Tariff clarity is essential to reduce unnecessary disputes, avoid case-by-case confusion, and enable transmission providers to process interconnection and load addition requests efficiently and predictably. The Commission provided clear guidance to PJM on how PJM should implement the Commission’s co-location order and facilitate the interconnection of new large loads such as data centers. In its filing, PJM states it “understands the importance of, and remains committed to, advancing our nation’s leadership in connecting large loads, including supporting the development of Artificial Intelligence (“AI”) infrastructure. PJM continues to work with its members to support efforts to accelerate the speed and increase the scale with which the United States can build the next generation of AI infrastructure, especially the necessary capacity to support this load while also ensuring reliability and fair treatment for all users of the grid.” 5 However, PJM’s proposal falls short of the Commission’s clear directive and the goals PJM itself identifies. As a result, the data center industry is left without viable pathways to bring solutions that accelerate the interconnection of critical load and generation and address the ongoing capacity shortfalls in PJM. FERC’s direction to provide a materiality threshold, which PJM set at 50 MW for BTMG, was to coincide with a suite of changes that also included a viable and efficient co-location path that is not BTMG. But PJM’s compliance filing leaves several core operational questions unanswered. For example, PJM’s proposed Interim NITS will be difficult 5 PJM’s Compliance Filing at 2.

As proposed, the framework leaves significant uncertainty regarding how the service would operate in practice, including the interaction between Interim NITS and co-located generation arrangements. This uncertainty creates substantial risk for customers considering major infrastructure investments and may render the service largely unusable in its current form.

a.​ Interim NITS Is Not Workable If Customers Cannot Use Co-Located
Generation During Curtailment
The proposal leaves unresolved a critical question regarding whether customers receiving Interim NITS may take energy from a co-located generator when their network service is curtailed. Interim NITS is intended to provide an incentive for load to bring generation behind the meter in exchange for getting energized more quickly while awaiting for transmission upgrades. However, PJM has proposed to prohibit customers from relying on co-located generation during curtailment events. In its January 23, 2026 Preliminary Proposal, PJM outlined its curtailment procedures for Interim NITS. As part of those procedures, PJM stated “[i]f curtailed, EC cannot take energy from co-located generator.”6 This is unworkable because curtailment should limit use of the grid, not prevent the customer from using the co-located resource it supplied, which supports Interim NITS in the first place. If the customer cannot use that generation during curtailment, Interim NITS provides no practical value. Maintaining such a limitation would significantly undermine the operational value of co-located generation arrangements. If co-located customers are expected to bring new resources to market, they should have access to those resources in time of curtailment. Further, PJM must define the trigger, scope, and sequence of Interim NITS curtailment with respect to the operational limitations identified in the study and should not be able to curtail 6PJM Staff Preliminary/Working Draft of Concepts as of January 23, 2026.

load for unrelated broader resource adequacy concerns, particularly where the colocated load has brought sufficient new supply to meet its demand. Curtailment direction unrelated to the result of the NITS studies should be outside the scope of this proceeding.

b.​ PJM Must Establish a Defined Process and Timeline for Necessary Studies
and CIR Review
In its filing PJM also provides guidance on interconnection rules for existing generators. PJM proposes any existing power plant seeking to serve co-located load must now go through a formal Necessary Studies process, reduce its Capacity Interconnection Rights (CIRs) accordingly, and execute a new Generation Interconnection Agreement before a single megawatt of co-located load can be served. However, clearer interconnection rules for existing generators also require more concrete timelines to review and approve these Necessary Studies and CIR revisions. PJM also needs to account for CIRs going to the specific co-located customer in a manner that doesn't force a generator to reduce more CIRs than necessary, which may happen when this is done on a 3-year look ahead. Clarity on the timeline for these reviews is needed so large load customers can plan their investment accordingly.

c.​ PJM’s Effective Date Should Be Earlier
PJM has proposed an effective date of June 1, 2029 for these new transmission services. Given the immediate need to connect new generation and load in PJM, this presumably means that PJM cannot start studying these innovative solutions for over three years. PJM should work with stakeholders to implement workable solutions in a timely manner that ensures these services can meet the immediate need within the PJM region. As discussed, PJM still has much work to-do to refine these services, but the goal should be to implement needed reforms earlier than 2029.

d.​ PJM Should Provide Greater Transparency Regarding how Customers Will
Transition from Interim NITS to NITS
While Interim NITS is intended to function as a temporary solution, the proposal does not provide clear timelines to co-location customers to expect to be transitioned to NITS or how long customers may be required to rely on the interim framework. Without clear timelines, Interim NITS could persist indefinitely, creating regulatory and operational uncertainty for customers making long-term investments in load and generation resources.

IV.​
Conclusion
DCC appreciates FERC’s leadership and innovation in pushing forward new transmission services in PJM. At this pivotal moment in the global AI race and America’s economic growth, FERC has a critical role to play in ensuring these services are not simply theoretical, but can actually be implemented in practice in a timely manner. In light of the significant policy and operational questions, several issues require further clarification and development. Accordingly, the Commission should require PJM to further clarify and revise its proposal to: (1) confirm that an Interim NITS customer may continue to use co-located generation when network service is curtailed; (2) establish a defined process and timeline for Necessary Studies and associated CIR revisions, and to ensure that any CIR reductions are tailored to the co-located service actually being provided; (4) PJM should endeavor to implement needed reforms in a timely manner; and

(5) provide greater transparency regarding how customers will transition from Interim NITS to full NITS. These matters, as well as additional concerns that may arise through stakeholder engagement, should be more fully addressed by PJM to ensure that the resulting framework is workable and provides sufficient certainty for customers considering major infrastructure investments.
