COMMENTS OF RMI  
 
I. 
Introduction 
RMI thanks the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (the “Commission”) for its examination of issues 
related to the co-location of large loads at generating facilities in PJM Interconnection L.L.C (“PJM”), as 
consolidated in Docket No. EL25-49-000, and hereby submits these comments pursuant to the invitation 
to address “(1) whether the existing Tariff remains just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory or 
preferential; and (2) if not, what changes to the Tariff should be implemented as replacement rates”, 
including process improvements and forecasting transparency. 
RMI analysis finds that a co-location strategy — which we call “Power Couples” — can help rapidly 
supply clean electricity to data centers and other new, large loads without risking grid reliability. A Power 
Couple is the pairing of a large electricity consumer with new-build solar, wind, and battery storage 
resources sized to meet the on-site load, all located near an existing gas generator with an approved 
interconnection. The load draws no energy from the grid and is equipped with physical protections to 
prevent the potential emergence of a load sink should co-located generation trip, while the gas generator 
continues to offer the network priority access to capacity, energy, and ancillary services whenever called 
upon.1 Rounding out this winning combination, surplus clean electricity from the renewables will often be 
available to serve the grid while reducing emissions, in all likelihood achieving a net reduction of the 
historical emissions of the co-located gas generator. 
Founded in 1982 as Rocky Mountain Institute, RMI is an independent, nonpartisan, nonprofit that 
transforms global energy systems through market-driven solutions to secure a clean, prosperous, zero-
carbon future for all. We work in the world’s most critical geographies and engage businesses, 
policymakers, communities, and nongovernmental organizations to identify and scale energy system 
interventions that will cut climate pollution at least 50 percent by 2030. 
 
II. 
Comments 
 
1) Fully or wholly isolated co-located load is very well consistent with engineering and 
economic principles, and PJM contention to the contrary is unduly discriminatory 
PJM expresses a clear preference for co-location solutions featuring Network Load. Adumbrating two 
proposed co-location options (4 and 5) in which the Network Load designation (and responsibilities) 
would not apply, PJM stipulates the installation of “system protection facilities…to prevent delivery of

energy from the grid” and then proceeds to question the efficacy and economic fairness of those same 
facilities.2 As PJM tells it,  
This configuration has been described as “fully” or “wholly” isolated by others, but this 
characterization is inconsistent with engineering and economic principles. This 
configuration relies on and takes grid services (for example, ancillary services) and 
should pay its fair share of those charges. Additionally, this configuration carries 
operational risk in that there is a dependency on the protection system to support

The allegation that fully or wholly isolated co-load is inconsistent with engineering and economic 
principles is not substantiated by PJM.  
In the Power Couples approach, power electronics offer protection on two fronts: 1) preventing 
the co-located load from drawing energy from the grid if the gas generator is insufficient or 
unavailable to meet any of the load’s otherwise unmet needs and 2) by curtailing dispatch from 
the renewables and discharge of storage in the event the co-located load cannot absorb such 
output and the grid has no call for it.

The Power Couples architecture does not prevent the System Operator from managing the gas 
generator’s output as it did prior to co-location, and the same communications controls should be 
capable of managing the export of surplus renewables under export conditions.  
To reiterate, with this architecture the co-located load never takes energy from the grid. At times 
when the gas generator is not called upon by system operator, it may deliver energy to the co-
located load, but RMI is hard-pressed to identify the use of grid services: the onsite inverter and 
storage resources are fully capable of maintaining voltage and following load fluctuations locally. 
In a black start eventuality, it stands to reason that the grid would be in distress and availing itself 
of its claim on the legacy gas generator; it follows that the benefits and the costs of black start 
service should accrue to Network Load, not the co-located load that would not be relying on any 
grid resources during the event. Thus, the only change from the grid’s perspective would from the 
variability of surplus exports from the existing interconnection; logically, the costs of managing 
this variability are the networked loads that benefit in real-time from the clean and affordable 
export electricity, not the co-located load managing power quality onsite with self-funded power 
electronics. The Power Couples option is in effect a rebuttal of PJM’s insistence that “At a 
minimum, co-located arrangements implicate the Commission’s transmission jurisdiction due to 
PJM’s provision of ancillary services and in light of the wholesale sales taking place over certain 
facilities.”3 
PJM adds that its Option 4 “configuration carries operational risk” because it creates a 
“dependency” on technology “to support system reliability.” RMI is curious whether PJM can 
identify any load-serving configuration, networked or otherwise, that is devoid of technology-
dependent risk. Failing such demonstration, if PJM could show that the protection systems 
envisioned for the RMI Power Couples solutions (and conceivably other solutions suitable for 
service under proposed Option 4) create more risk than current configurations, then there would 
be a case for a developing a tariff element to compensate the network service accordingly. PJM, 
however, has offered no facts in this regard. 
 
2) PJM should rectify the current lack of a “tariffed study process for load (that is not PJM 
Network locating behind a generator’s Point of Interconnection,”4 implementing study 
processes tailored specifically for assessing such co-locations solutions 
In these comments, RMI has described a co-location architecture where load sits behind an existing 
generator’s interconnection but cannot draw energy from the grid (thanks to physical protections), 
otherwise benefit from grid services, or occasion adverse impacts on the grid. 
In the absence of a suitable tariffed study process, PJM currently employs the Necessary Study “for a load 
co-locating with an existing generation resource and not seeking to be a Network Load,”5 evaluating it for 
the full amount of load and reflecting any Capacity Interconnection Rights reductions, and involve 
generation deliverability, N-1 and N-1-1 thermal and voltage studies, special protection systems review, 
baseline stability, and, if applicable, a nuclear plant interface requirements voltage analysis.”6 
Assessing co-located load for the full amount of load without first assessing the efficacy and 
reliability of the protection systems intended to prevent the addition of any load imposes, in 
RMI’s opinion, unnecessary costs and burdens on a Power Couple or similar architecture. 
Furthermore, since Power Couples do not alter the use of the existing interconnection other than

3) PJM should require earlier standardized information sharing between itself, large load 
customers, and transmission owners (TOs) to improve forecasting accuracy and reduce 
planning uncertainty. 
PJM acknowledges in its response to Section D.v that uncertainty around large load additions can lead to 
overbuilding or underbuilding of system resources, and that improvements to forecasting may be 
warranted. RMI agrees, and underscores the importance of earlier, standardized, and more detailed load 
information sharing between itself, large load customers, and TOs. 
Receiving critical information such as ramp rates, load profiles, project timelines, and capital 
commitments could help PJM more accurately determine and communicate resource adequacy needs and 
risks. Standardizing this information—and ensuring it is shared directly with RTOs—would improve 
forecasting, guide more efficient transmission planning, and help avoid infrastructure misalignment.  
RMI also supports aligning these planning improvements with meaningful large load corporate 
commitments, so that these customers contributing to new infrastructure needs are positioned to share in 
their costs.
