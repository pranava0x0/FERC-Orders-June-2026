PROTEST OF THE INDICATED PJM TRANSMISSION OWNERS 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy 
Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or the “Commission”),1 the Indicated PJM Transmission 
Owners2 respectfully submit this Protest to PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) proposed 
tariff amendment (“Proposed Tariff”) concerning its Federal Power Act (“FPA”) section 205 
filing seeking to amend its tariff to adopt a Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”).  
Specifically, this Protest addresses section 18.7 of PJM’s Proposed Tariff (the “Exit 
Amendment”), which would establish new prerequisites to a Transmission Owner (“TO”) 
exiting PJM and new exit fees.  This Protest is not brought on by any desire to exit PJM; rather, 
the Protest aims to confront PJM’s deviation from the Consolidated Transmission Owners

As set forth herein, the Exit Amendment is procedurally and substantively defective 
and should be rejected by the Commission.   
First, PJM did not follow the required procedure under the CTOA to file the 
amendment.  Contrary to PJM’s position, the Exit Amendment is not a mere change to the 
“terms and conditions of the PJM Tariff” that PJM may unilaterally file under Section 9.2(a) 
of the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT”).  Rather, the Exit Amendment 
effectively amends the CTOA, and therefore could be filed only after a vote of the TOs with 
sufficient support—a requirement that was indisputably not met here.  Under CTOA section 
8.5.1, amending the CTOA requires a two-thirds majority of both representatives holding two-
thirds of the Individual Votes cast and representatives holding two-thirds of the Weighted 
Votes cast.  There is a narrow fallback—a simple majority of both Individual Votes and 
Weighted Votes suffices only if opposition stays below five percent of Weighted Votes (for 
the Individual-Vote count) and below three Parties (for the Weighted-Vote count).  PJM’s Exit 
Amendment met neither of these requirements. 
Second, the Exit Amendment is inconsistent with Commission precedent and violates 
cost-causation principles by assigning generation- and capacity-related costs to TOs rather than 
to Load Serving Entities (“LSEs”).  The Commission has never allocated generation- and 
capacity-related costs to TOs and, more specifically, the Exit Amendment conflicts with highly 
relevant precedent in the Duquesne Light Co. proceeding where the Commission notably did 
not apply generation-related costs to an exiting TO in its capacity as a TO.3

I. 
BACKGROUND 
On December 15, 2005, the TOs and PJM entered into the CTOA, whose purpose is to 
establish the “rights and commitments” of PJM and the TOs.4  
PJM’s RBP proposal had its origins in a January 2026 statement of principles signed 
by the governors of the states within PJM’s territory.5  PJM advanced the proposal through its 
Critical Issue Fast Path (“CIFP”) process, presenting an initial proposal at an April 16–17, 
2026 set of meetings and revising it at further CIFP meetings on May 4–5, May 27, and June 
10–11, 2026.6  In these latter meetings, PJM began to highlight the purported risk posed by a 
TO exiting PJM during the RBP’s 15-year term.   
PJM addressed this “Transmission Owner Separation Risk” at its May 27, 2026 CIFP 
meeting and again in its June 11, 2026 proposal paper.7  As of the June 11 paper, PJM had still 
not settled on the fees a departing TO would owe, presenting instead alternative “Options”, 
including one that would add a twenty-percent “deficiency penalty” on top of an accelerated 
payment of the remaining notional contract value.8

On July 31, 2026, PJM filed its RBP proposal with the Commission with a proposed 
effective date of September 29, 2026.9  The RBP would commit PJM’s members to significant 
expenditures via the procurement of new capacity resources for a fixed fifteen-year term, from

ARGUMENT 
A.

Administrative Committee (“TOAAC”) before filing the RBP Proposal.  Second, even if the 
Exit Amendment does not amend the CTOA, PJM can only justify its proposal under cost-

1. 
The Exit Amendment Effectively Amends The CTOA Without A 
Majority Vote In Support By The Transmission Owners 
 
Under CTOA section 8.5.1, any amendment to the CTOA may be filed only after an 
approval vote of the TOs with either a two-thirds majority voting in favor, or a simple majority 
voting in favor but with votes in opposition remaining below a certain threshold.14  PJM’s Exit 
Amendment effectively proposes such an amendment, and yet was filed without the required 
approval by the TOs in the TOAAC.   
Under the current CTOA, an exiting TO’s obligations are governed by sections 3.2 and 
3.4.  Pursuant to section 3.2, “[a]ny Party may withdraw from” the CTOA if it meets three 
preconditions: (1) securing alternative arrangements for comparable transmission service; (2) 
obtaining FERC approval to withdraw; and (3) “if its Transmission Facilities do not comprise 
an entire Control Area,” satisfying all applicable NERC and Applicable Regional Reliability 
Council standards for operating a Control Area or being included within an existing one.15  
Pursuant to section 3.4, a withdrawing TO remains liable for obligations it incurred, that were 
incurred on its behalf, or that arose under the CTOA before the withdrawal’s effective date.16

The Exit Amendment changes both sections.  It modifies section 3.2 by adding new 
RBP-related preconditions to withdrawal—making it no longer true that a TO “may withdraw 
from” the CTOA if it meets section 3.2’s preconditions alone.  The Exit Amendment likewise 
amends section 3.4 by making a withdrawing TO liable for new RBP-related obligations found 
nowhere in the current CTOA.  Because the Exit Amendment amends these terms of the 
CTOA, under section 8.5.1, the Exit Amendment could be properly filed only after a vote of 
TOs in the TOAAC in support, with either a two-thirds majority voting in favor, or a simple 
majority voting in favor but with votes in opposition remaining below a certain threshold—
which PJM did not obtain. 
PJM cannot escape section 8.5.1’s limitations by characterizing the Exit Amendment 
as a change to the “terms and conditions of the PJM Tariff” under CTOA section 7.5.1(i).  If 
PJM could alter the CTOA’s substance simply by casting the change as an OATT amendment, 
CTOA section 8.5.1 would be a nullity, as it would effectively provide PJM unilateral 
discretion to amend the CTOA in clear contravention of that section. The only way to give 
effect to both provisions is to treat CTOA section 8.5.1 as controlling wherever an amendment 
would change the CTOA’s substantive terms, as this one does.17 
2. 
If The Exit Amendment Concerns Transmission-Related Rather 
Than Generation- and Capacity-Related Costs, It Is Within The 
Transmission Owners’, Not PJM’s, Exclusive Authority To File 
As discussed infra, the Exit Amendment violates cost-causation principles because it 
imposes generation- and capacity-related costs on TOs.  But if the proposal could somehow be

If the Exit Amendment does not concern capacity costs, and if contrary to the argument

Pursuant to the Exit Amendment, an exiting TO with “Committed RBP Resources” 
(i.e., resources that were cleared to provide capacity in the RBP) “located within the physical 
boundaries of the Transmission Owner’s Zone” must, before exiting, either preserve the RBP 
commitments with the owners of RBP Resources within its Zone, pay all costs for those 
Resources to pseudo-tie into PJM, or “pay costs associated with the remaining term of the RBP 
Resource” in an amount to “be determined through a filing submitted under the Federal Power 
Act.”25  For a TO “seeking to exit PJM[] with net load that has been allocated RBP Charges 
for RBP Resources not located within the physical boundaries of the [TO’s] Zone,” the Exit 
Amendment requires that TO either to enter into agreements that preserve those RBP 
commitments or “pay all forward obligations on an accelerated basis” before exiting.26 
These charges make an exiting TO responsible for the RBP commitments of generation 
resources and/or load within its zone.  Those cannot be fairly allocated to a TO as transmission-
related costs.  Commission precedent is directly on point: “cost-causation and benefits/burdens 
principles” require that costs be allocated to those who cause and/or benefit from them.27  In 
other words, “costs are allocated to the parties who cause the incurrence of such costs.”28  In 
particular, where “real-time load is the primary beneficiary, and the primary cost-driver” of a 
program, and where that program “address[es] generation-related reliability concerns, not 
transmission-related concerns,” the “costs of the Program should be allocated to Real-Time

Load Obligation,” not TOs.29  Notably, Duquesne Light Co., the leading Commission 
precedent, involved Duquesne Light Company’s (“Duquesne”) planned exit from PJM in both 
its capacity as a TO and an LSE.  It was only in the context of its role as an LSE, pursuant to

Cost-causation principles condemn PJM’s Exit Amendment.  TOs neither cause the 
costs of procuring generation capacity over the RBP’s 15-year term (nor the costs of procuring 
generation capacity under PJM’s one-year-at-a-time Base Residual Auction), nor do they 
directly benefit from those costs.  LSEs and their customers do.  Allocating the costs to TOs, 
therefore, violates cost-causation principles.

CONCLUSION

request that the Commission reject PJM’s proposed Exit Amendment.
