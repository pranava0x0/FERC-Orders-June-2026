I. 
Background 
A. 
ATC 
ATC is a Wisconsin limited liability company created as a single-purpose, for-profit 
transmission company.7  ATC began operations under the terms of its own open access 
transmission tariff on January 1, 2001, and currently owns and operates over 10,000 miles of 
jurisdictional transmission facilities in the states of Wisconsin, Illinois, Minnesota, and 
Michigan.  Many of ATC's facilities were acquired, with Commission approval, from a number 
of previously vertically integrated companies, municipalities, and electric cooperatives.  On 
February 1, 2002, ATC transferred operational control of its facilities to MISO.8  Since that time, 
MISO has provided transmission service over ATC’s transmission facilities pursuant to the terms 
of the MISO Tariff, with ATC operating its transmission facilities at MISO’s direction.  ATC has 
its own Commission-approved local planning process set forth in the MISO Tariff at Attachment 
FF-ATCLLC. 
B. 
ATC’s Planning Functions and Attachment FF-ATCLLC 
Since beginning operations in 2001, ATC has engaged in local transmission planning as 
one of its core functions.  ATC’s local transmission planning process is open, collaborative, 
driven by stakeholder needs, and responsive to industry changes and technology developments.  
Attachment FF-ATCLLC sets forth ATC’s planning functions and the manner in which ATC 
meets the transmission needs of its customers, which is a key element of ATC’s operations.

ATC’s Distribution – Transmission Interconnection Process   
Attachment FF-ATCLLC sets forth ATC’s process for Distribution – Transmission 
Interconnections.  The process is initiated by one or more of ATC’s distribution customers 
through submission of a Load Interconnection Request Form (“LIRF”).  Upon receipt of a 
complete LIRF, ATC will begin its studies and analyses of the interconnection.  More 
specifically, ATC will engage with its Best Value Planning process, as defined in Attachment 
FF-ATCLLC, and collaborate with the customer to determine the best value solution to 
interconnect the new load request.   
 
Upon ATC’s development of the project containing the transmission facilities necessary

coordinates with MISO and engages with MISO’s planning process.  ATC will submit the 
proposed project to MISO either through the annual MTEP process or MISO’s Expedited Project 
Review (“EPR”) process.  Upon MISO’s approval, the project would be included in Appendix A 
of MTEP.

D. 
ATC’s Development of Large Load Studies and Requirements  
In 2022, ATC began receiving serious inquiries about potential new large loads, and in 
particular data centers.  While ATC had already developed a robust local planning process, 
internal planning experts quickly realized that more than traditional steady state analysis would 
be needed to appropriately study and determine the system impacts of the unique characteristics 
presented by large load data centers.  ATC began intensive internal studies and engaged with 
vendors to develop concepts for mitigation of key concerns related to large data center loads.  
Subsequently, in 2024 through 2025, ATC elevated its engagement with external 
resources, including specialized power system consultants.  Specifically, ATC and its consultants 
engaged with new and unique types of analyses to study large loads, including flicker analysis, 
rotor swing modes, and generator shaft studies.  ATC determined that to safely and reliably 
interconnect large loads, more robust planning criteria requirements that apply specifically to 
these new, unique large loads must be developed and implemented.  Development of these 
requirements posed unique challenges as there was no industry documentation of these issues, 
concerns, impact, mitigation measures, or performance requirements.  Authorities such as NERC 
had not yet engaged with these issues.  Recognizing the need for transformation in response to 
the characteristics of new large loads, ATC stepped into the role of industry leader in developing 
reliability criteria.  
Each new large load presents its own unique characteristics and challenges, however 
ATC realized early in the development phase that it would not be practical to perform custom 
studies for each load interconnection request and to derive bespoke project-specific limits to 
address reliability concerns.  Therefore, through intensive studies and analyses, internally and 
with consultants, ATC developed new local planning criteria, including new criteria developed 
exclusively to apply to large load interconnections across ATC’s footprint.  Uniquely situated 
among the Great Lakes and Mississippi River, ATC’s geography stands out from other areas in 
the MISO footprint.  Additionally, as a transmission-only company, ATC has developed close 
relationships with its customers.  These factors have contributed to ATC’s ability to develop 
processes and requirements that are specifically applicable to ATC’s system without 
compromising reliability.  
Through studies and analysis, ATC initially determined that the appropriate threshold for 
categorizing large loads in the ATC footprint would be 200 MW.  As discussed below, ATC 
continues to analyze, on no less than an annual basis, the appropriate threshold for categorizing 
large loads and associated reliability and operational criteria.  Through the proposed Attachment 
FF-ATCLLC updates described below, ATC is now updating its large load threshold to 100 MW 
to align with directives from the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin (“PSCW”) regarding 
appropriate measures to mitigate cost shifting concerns at the retail level.11  ATC has determined

II. 
Discussion  
ATC’s proposed revisions to Attachment FF-ATCLLC are just and reasonable.  The 
Commission acknowledged in the Show Cause Order that ATC has its own distribution 
interconnection request process and load interconnection procedures described in Attachment 
FF-ATCLLC, but that Attachment FF-ATCLLC did not explicitly address interconnections of 
large loads.12  Over the last few years, ATC has developed company-specific business practices 
containing policies, practices, and requirements directed specifically at large loads that also 
address several concerns identified by the Commission in the Show Cause Order.13  Therefore, in 
this filing, ATC is seeking to amend Attachment FF-ATCLLC to add these requirements and 
processes associated with large loads in ATC’s footprint.  
 
As discussed above, many of the revisions to Attachment FF-ATCLLC reflect existing 
processes, practices, and requirements in ATC’s footprint.  Therefore, ATC’s proposed revisions 
should not disrupt existing commercial arrangements and in-flight projects.  By allowing ATC to 
maintain existing process and requirements, while seamlessly building in additional requirements 
and provisions necessary to address several of the Commission’s concerns in the Show Cause 
Order, the proposed revisions are just and reasonable and will provide clarity and transparency 
for the reliable interconnection of large loads in the ATC footprint.14

3. 
Study Procedures  
i. FERC Directive  
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission expressed concern that the MISO Tariff 
lacked specific study procedures for Eligible Customers seeking transmission service on behalf 
of large loads for new and incremental additions.23  Additionally, the Commission indicated that 
studies to produce a non-binding good-faith estimate of the cost and time to construct facilities 
necessary to interconnect a large load customer should take no more than 90 days to complete.24  
The Commission also indicated that studies should consider operational characteristics and that 
accurate models using Electromagnetic Transient studies may be helpful in analysis of large 
loads.25 
ii. ATC Compliance  
As described above, Attachment FF-ATCLLC has guided ATC’s interconnection process 
since its adoption in MISO’s Tariff.  In response to the Commission's Show Cause Order and 
changing industry needs, ATC proposes to add additional detail and requirements to its 
distribution interconnection study process.  Upon completion of all applicable readiness 
requirements as discussed above, ATC will initiate a System Impact Study.  System Impact 
Studies are split into two distinct phases:  (1) steady state analysis, and (2) applicable special 
studies.   
All load interconnections require ATC to conduct the steady state analysis.  The steady 
state analysis phase of the System Impact Study shall result in a non-binding good-faith estimate 
of time, cost, and potential facilities necessary to interconnect a load, responsive to the 
Commission's directives outlined in the Show Cause Order.  Upon completion of the steady state 
analysis, ATC can move into the special studies phase, as needed.  Not all load interconnection 
requests require special studies, and the extent to which depends upon the complexity of the 
project.  However, potential projects that exceed ATC’s large load threshold or display unique 
characteristics will require additional studies during the special studies phase.  The special 
studies phase includes analysis of, but is not limited to, transfer analysis, reactive power analysis, 
angular and voltage stability of nearby generators, load voltage stability, voltage ride through of 
both generators and loads, power quality analysis, short-circuit analysis, and other transient study 
analyses.  The special studies phase analyses may also involve several positive sequence and 
Electromagnetic Transient study tools and result in technical reports or recommendations for 
potential mitigation of any limitations observed.

Large Load Definition and Operational Requirements  
1. 
Large Load Definition  
i. FERC Directive  
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission expressed concern due to the lack of a 
definition of large load in MISO’s Tariff that sufficiently captures loads that present unique 
challenges described in the Show Cause Order.30 
ii. ATC Compliance  
As stated above, ATC previously established a threshold of 200 MW to define large loads 
in its system.  To address the Commission’s concerns in the Show Cause Order, ATC is

operational, and cost shifting concerns.  This threshold shall serve as a baseline threshold and 
may be adjusted in appliable ATC Business Practices and local planning criteria as necessary.  
As described above, ATC has determined that 100 MW is an appropriate threshold from 
a planning, reliability, and operational perspective within ATC’s footprint.  ATC consistently 
coordinates with MISO, other regional and national entities, its customers, and other 
interconnected entities to ensure that any process or requirement in ATC’s footprint shall not 
compromise reliability within ATC’s territory, MISO’s service area, or broader.  Additionally, 
the PSCW has recently established that 100 MW is an appropriate threshold to address retail cost

ii. ATC Compliance  
For the past two years, ATC has engaged with its customers and other stakeholders to 
address the concerns described by the Commission.  Ultimately, this led ATC to develop a new 
Business Practice on Large Load Cost Allocation and develop two new agreements to ensure 
cost shifting concerns are addressed in ATC’s footprint at the wholesale level, and in a manner 
that allows ATC’s customers who provide retail service, are able to comply with directives from 
state authorities on cost shifting prevention at the retail level.  Specifically, ATC has developed a 
Large Load Project Commitment Agreement (“LLPCA”) and a Minimum Transmission Charge 
Agreement (“MTCA”) that it will enter into with its distribution customer.  Pursuant to ATC’s 
Large Load Cost Allocation Business Practice, ATC requires these agreements if the project 
exceeds the definition of large load (i.e., 100 MW), or exceeds $100M in costs.  The PSCW has 
determined that 100 MW would be an appropriate threshold in the State of Wisconsin to 
adequately protect against cost shifting concerns.43  Further, ATC recognized the need to address 
the potential impact from new loads that may be smaller than 100 MW, but because they are in 
areas of the system that have less transmission buildout than others, drive a disproportionate 
amount of network upgrades.  Therefore,  ATC intends to ensure that no cost shifting occurs for 
distribution interconnection projects that drive projects that exceed $100M in cost.  
ATC recently filed several LLPCAs and an MTCA associated with a large load in its 
footprint44 and intends to develop additional LLPCAs and MTCAs with in-flight projects in its 
footprint.  The protections provided by the LLPCA and MTCA work in tandem, each described 
in detail below.  
The LLPCA is designed to protect the Parties and other wholesale customers from costs 
associated with construction of facilities tied to a new large load and in the event of project 
cancellation, termination, or substantial modification prior to the project being placed in service.    
Likewise, ATC can invoke the terms of the LLPCA in the event of cancellation, termination, or 
substantial modification to recoup costs incurred during project development and construction.

such that the distribution customer, and by extension, its customer, will not subsidize 
transmission service for other customers.

Commission’s Show Cause Order related to large load interconnections.45  In the Show Cause

transmission customers, which may result in unjust and unreasonable rates for transmission

customers are protected during the period of construction and also in the event the large load 
end-use customer fails to materialize or fails to take service to the full extent of its request to 
ATC’s distribution customer.  The protection mechanisms provided by the LLPCA and MTCA 
directly respond to the Commission’s concerns on the risk of unjust cost shifts.47  The Amended 
LLPCAs and MTCA offer the protections that the Commission is seeking in a fair, just, and 
reasonable manner and similar to other accepted cost recovery agreements, such as Transmission
