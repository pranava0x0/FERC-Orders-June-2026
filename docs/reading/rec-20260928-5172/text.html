II. ANSWER
The PJM Answer does not resolve the disparate treatment identified in CEBA’s Protest. Instead, PJM responds to an argument CEBA did not make. PJM characterizes CEBA and other intervenors as challenging the “omission of Peak Shaving Adjustment MW from [Bring Your Own New Capacity (BYONC)] arrangements” and ultimately concludes that Peak Shaving

Adjustments (PSAs) “cannot and should not qualify as BYONC MW capable of reducing
[Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS)] exposure.”5  But CEBA did not ask PJM to treat PSA MW as BYONC, permit them to participate as Capacity Resources in capacity auctions (Reliability Pricing Model [RPM] Auctions), or make them dispatchable during an IRAS Action. CEBA asked for a tariff-based mechanism that recognizes incremental, customer-sponsored PSAs that reduce PJM’s capacity requirement, including qualifying investments elsewhere within the relevant utility service territory or zone.6

Fundamentally, CEBA seeks recognition of incremental, customer-sponsored PSAs for what they are: reductions in forecast peak demand that reduce the underlying capacity need associated with new load growth. The IRAS proposal is patently unjust, unreasonable, and unduly preferential insofar as it fails to recognize that core resource adequacy benefit of customer-sponsored PSAs.

A. PJM conflates reducing the capacity need with providing a Capacity
Resource while failing to reconcile its position with its treatment of PSAs under the RBP proposal

PJM conflates two distinct ways of addressing a resource adequacy need: adding capacity to meet incremental demand and reducing the incremental demand for which capacity must be procured. PJM emphasizes that PSA MW are “adjustments to PJM’s load forecasts, not resources that may participate in RPM Auctions.”7 That distinction does not rebut CEBA’s argument; it is the premise of it. As CEBA has previously argued, PJM’s own rules recognize qualifying PSAs as reductions to forecast peak demand and, therefore, to the capacity requirement that PJM must satisfy when procuring capacity, whether through the RBP or RPM Auctions.8 The fact that the reduction occurs on the demand side rather than through the addition

PJM’s observation that PSA MW are not “real-time dispatchable” is irrelevant.10 As PJM itself explains, “[t]he impetus for IRAS is to incent New Large Loads to bring new capacity to meet their resource adequacy needs.”11  If an incremental, qualifying PSA reduces PJM’s forecast peak in the first instance and therefore reduces the amount of capacity that must be procured to meet a New Large Load’s resource adequacy needs, that capacity need has been reduced before any real-time IRAS Action ever occurs. C. PJM’s disparate treatment of customer-sponsored PSAs is unjustified and
counterproductive to state-led efforts to support resource adequacy
Under the IRAS proposal, the disparate treatment is straightforward. Consider two otherwise identical New Large Loads. One customer supports new Capacity Resources that PJM determines provide 25 MW of capacity toward addressing the incremental resource adequacy need associated with its load. The other finances qualifying incremental peak-shaving capability that reduces PJM’s forecast peak demand and, after application of the Forecast Pool Requirement, reduces PJM’s capacity requirement by the same 25 MW. PJM’s proposal recognizes the first customer’s contribution in determining its IRAS exposure but provides no comparable mechanism to recognize the second. PJM identifies differences between Capacity Resources and PSAs, but it does not explain why those differences justify treating differently two investments that reduce PJM’s otherwise-unmet capacity need by the same amount. In addressing other parties’ tangential arguments about BYONC gating criteria for Demand Resources and DER Capacity Aggregation Resources—both supply-side Capacity Resources—PJM implies that its skepticism toward customer-sponsored PSAs may similarly stem from concern that such investments will not produce “new and lasting” solutions to the capacity shortfall.13 Customer-sponsored PSAs can rely on the same types of distributed energy

D. Customer-sponsored PSAs and new Capacity Resources are complementary
pieces to the resource adequacy puzzle
To reiterate, CEBA is not asking the Commission to transform PSAs into Capacity Resources or to require PJM to count PSA MW as BYONC. Rather, PJM should revise proposed Tariff, Attachment DD-4, section V(b), to recognize the reduction in PJM’s capacity requirement attributable to a verified, incremental, customer-sponsored PSA when determining the sponsoring New Large Load’s Eligible New Large Load Amount.16 PJM should apply the Forecast Pool Requirement as needed to express that benefit in the units used by section V(b), and establish appropriate rules for verification and safeguard against double counting. This would recognize the PSA’s effect on resource adequacy without treating it as BYONC or as a resource PJM can dispatch during an IRAS Action.

This approach preserves the distinction on which PJM relies. BYONC supplies incremental capacity; PSAs reduce forecast demand. But both can support resource adequacy by reducing the incremental capacity need associated with a New Large Load. PJM has not explained why IRAS should recognize only one side of that equation.

III. CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, CEBA respectfully asks that the Commission accept this answer in the record and find that PJM’s IRAS proposal is unjust, unreasonable, and unduly preferential insofar as it provides no mechanism to recognize the reduction in PJM’s capacity
