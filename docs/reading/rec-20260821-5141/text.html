COMMENTS OF VITOL INC.

Vitol appreciates the opportunity to offer perspectives on PJM governance arising from the Commissioner-led Technical Conference on July 23, 2026. A world leader in power commodities, Vitol brings deep expertise in valuing power and its inputs. Within PJM, Vitol participates as a member of the “Other Supplier” sector, a classification that reflects its diverse business interests including power trading, serving default-service requirements to utilities in several states, providing structured bilateral energy solutions, and developing solar and storage assets through its affiliate, VC Renewables LLC, which presently operates 120 MWAC of solar projects in Pennsylvania and has developed dozens of projects across PJM.  Vitol’s primary representative to PJM stakeholder committees also serves as the current Chair of the PJM Members Committee.

Vitol agrees with the Commission that there are many opportunities to improve the PJM governance processes, expedite collective decision-making, and foster progress.  PJM faces significant challenges that stem from mostly exogenous factors: an influx of large loads, global supply chain constraints, state policies that inhibit generation development, glacial transmission development speeds, and price shocks. PJM governance improvements may enable PJM, its Members, states, and stakeholders to act prospectively and respond to such challenges more expeditiously.

Generally, we seek:
 A PJM Board that is independent, includes diverse expertise, and is empowered to make decisive, timely business judgements;

  PJM Board and management accountability to all stakeholders for timely issue resolution1;

 Active state participation in the PJM governance process through defined roles that enable earlier engagement;

I. PJM Board Independence
Vitol agrees with the Technical Conference consensus that the PJM Board should be informed by stakeholder input but fully empowered to make courageous and timely decisions in the best interest of maintaining PJM’s ability to meet current and future power needs while adhering to the FPA and Commission orders establishing RTOs.

1) Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years?
Extending Board member terms to five or more years might help insulate Board members from any undue Member influence arising from the need to secure re-nomination after the current three-year terms. Renomination after a longer initial term could still be appropriate, although consideration should be given to whether PJM Members or Board members are responsible for renomination decisions. Extending Board member terms should be conditioned with other reforms, including open Board meetings and a more detailed written record of Board decision making. These transparency improvements provide Board member accountability consistent with the term extension. Moreover, transparency measures permit stakeholders to assess individual Board member performance and provide feedback to the Board that can inform their independent retention decisions. See further details in response to Question 6, below.

2) Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may serve (e.g., 10 Years)?

Board term limits should be maintained. Historically, PJM Board members have been limited to total service of nine years (three, three-year terms), which has worked well. Balancing the need for Board members to gain experience with a need for new perspectives is critical. However, the appropriate limit should be reconsidered if terms are extended beyond the present three-year term.

3) Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a preference for some Board members that reside in the PJM region?

A residence requirement is not necessary. In our experience, there has been little, if any, difference in Board members’ grasp of regional issues and culture because of where they live. Understanding regional issues and culture is certainly one element in a new Board member’s learning curve, but it is far less important than understanding unique PJM policy, markets, and

operations features. If a residency requirement were to be instituted, then it should only apply to a small fraction of the Board.

4) Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off period for Board members from two years to one year?

Retention of the existing conflict-of-interest rules is appropriate to diminish potential Board candidate bias from recent PJM Member or state affiliations. There is no indication that the current rule limits the pool of qualified candidates.

5) Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable more communication between the Board and stakeholders?

The status quo works reasonably well to balance access to the Board while guarding against undue influence that might be levied without notice to all stakeholders. Board members are readily accessible at various public PJM meetings and periodic state gatherings (e.g., OPSI and MACRUC meetings). Members and OPSI have both public and limited private group opportunities to meet with the Board. Any stakeholder can send public correspondence to the Board at any time on any subject. However, if ex parte communication rules are loosened then it should be conditioned with obligations for notice regarding private meetings.

6) In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the Board be required to explain its reasoning?

Board votes should be made public. Board decisions authorizing any FPA Sec. 205 or Sec. 206 filing should be explained to demonstrate how the Board considered and weighed alternatives in authorizing a PJM submittal. Specifically, if OPSI or a state governors’ organization provides a resource adequacy proposal backed by a supermajority of its members, PJM could be obligated within its FERC transmittal to explain its rationale for rejection of such proposal.

7) In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or available to the public?

Consistent with practice in other RTOs, PJM Board meetings should be open to Members and authorized state representatives, perhaps with limitations for sensitive corporate or operational matters (e.g., personnel evaluations and Critical Energy Infrastructure Information-protected matters).

8) Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest?

Revising Section 7.7 of the PJM Operating Agreement to include a public interest standard may be beneficial if the application of such a standard is clearly defined in a manner consistent with PJM’s obligations under the FPA and the Commission’s orders.

9) Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the Nominating Committee?

Vitol supports adding one state representative to the diverse mix of Members and Board Member representatives on the Nominating Committee.  The addition of a state representative may duplicate certain consumer interests, but the added consideration of broader state concerns should enhance the committee’s perspectives. However, adding multiple state representatives would result in over-representation of states and skew the balance of interests within the committee.

10) Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management compensation to their achievement?

Additional details about the proposed metrics are required to fairly address this question. Generally, stakeholders would benefit from understanding management motivations, but the Board’s independent decisions regarding management oversight should not be unnecessarily encumbered by rigid metrics.

II. State Participation
The technical conference revealed clear, shared interests among the Commission, state representatives, and many stakeholders for more formalized state participation and governance roles. Vitol supports some reforms that give states a greater opportunity for both engaging in stakeholder deliberations and conveying states’ interest to the PJM Board and management. However, new privileges extended to states should be coupled with accountability for timely, concerted expression of the states’ interests.

Currently, there are many opportunities for state engagement that have not been fully utilized by state representatives.  States representatives are not unduly limited in their ability to participate in PJM committee deliberations, offer proposals, inject ideas, and influence PJM management decision-making. There are many recent examples of such state influence.  Historically, some state public service commission representatives have regularly participated in some stakeholder committees, but input is infrequent. State consumer advocates participate more regularly through

their CAPS (Consumer Advocates of PJM States) representative.  Only recently – roughly coincident with capacity market price escalation – have state representatives outside of PSC staff and CAPS become regularly involved in PJM committee deliberations. Through OPSI, states have opportunities for closed door meetings with the PJM Board on multiple occasions each year, similar to the opportunities afforded PJM Members. Notwithstanding existing opportunities for state engagement, more can be done to help states advance their aims and to understand matters under consideration by PJM management and Members.  Any such reforms must be constructed to ensure state concerns are presented in a timely and actionable structure.

18) In addition to OPSI, should there be a second state representative group consisting of governors’ representatives and/or state legislators’ representatives?

Creation of additional state representative groups, in addition to OPSI, should be the prerogative of the states. Regardless, PJM governance would be more robust if OPSI obtained additional funding to support full-time professional staff that can actively engage in stakeholder deliberations alongside Members. Active OPSI representation could give state commissions and perhaps, by proxy, state policymakers greater in-depth understanding of the complexities and trade-offs under consideration by PJM and stakeholders on any given issue. As seen in other RTOs, state governors may benefit from their own organization to develop strategic policy perspectives and engage with the PJM Board and management to relay those perspectives (similar to the New England States Committee on Electricity structure in ISO-New England). It is the states’ collective choice whether such governor organization would be administered separately or in concert with OPSI. Regardless of the organization(s) states choose to enable, all such organization(s) must unambiguously express who is speaking on behalf of the states on any given matter.  Groups comprised of state legislators would not be able to speak clearly on behalf of the states since they represent only a fraction of a state with potentially diverse views from the state executive branch.

19)  Should state representatives have a formal voting role in the stakeholder process (limited to certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional transmission cost allocation) in addition to the consumer advocate votes?

PJM and its Members value state input on matters under consideration by stakeholder committees.  To facilitate such input, states could be provided a non-voting ex officio role in PJM committee proceedings to express their interests throughout deliberations.

20) Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a section 205 filing related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation?

PJM consultation with OPSI prior to making Section 205 filings related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation could yield more informed PJM filings that align with state interests. However, any consultation structure should neither impede PJM’s independent decision making nor delay submissions to FERC.

III. Section 205 Filing Rights
A legally-permissible allocation of Section 205 rights between PJM and its Members over the energy and ancillary services markets and regional transmission planning function is possible. Appropriately structured, such an allocation would empower the Board to act independently, while retaining Members’ contractual rights over the energy and ancillary services and planning functions.

11) Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes only inform the Board?

The stakeholder process should be “advisory,” subject to Member retention of jump-ball Section 205 rights. The PJM Board should be empowered to act independently, free from any stakeholder veto or delay of meritorious PJM proposals. Order No. 2000 makes independence the “bedrock” of RTO governance, while Order No. 719 requires inclusive, balanced, and responsive stakeholder processes, but not stakeholder control.2  The PJM Board should be fully empowered to direct PJM actions on its own accord. Empowering the PJM Board with the ability to make Section 205 filings regarding the energy and ancillary service markets and regional transmission planning will enable the Board to make timely and courageous action, notwithstanding Member consensus.  Extending Section 205 rights to the Operating Agreement would also make PJM accountable for presenting reforms to the Commission if Members are deadlocked or delayed. However, Board independence need not impede Members from submitting competing Section 205 proposals, as discussed in response to Question 15. Member retention of jump-ball Section 205 rights enables a supermajority of Members to develop consensus proposals that can enrich the record before the Commission and check PJM intransigence.

12)   Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over the energy market and ancillary services unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee

Subject to Member retention of jump-ball Section 205 rights, PJM should have Section 205 filing rights over the energy market and ancillary services unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee.

13)  Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over Regional Transmission Expansion Planning (RTEP) unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee?

Subject to Member retention of jump-ball Section 205 rights, PJM should have Section 205 filing rights over Regional Transmission Expansion Planning (RTEP) unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee.

14) Should the Organization of PJM States Inc (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights over resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a supermajority of OPSI agrees on an alternative proposal?

States should have a formal and meaningful consultative role in PJM governance, but OPSI should not possess Section 205 jump-ball rights. Section 205 permits a public utility to propose changes to its jurisdictional rates and practices, but OPSI and its member commissions are not public utilities. In Massachusetts DPU v. FERC, the court sustained FERC’s rejection of a utility filing compelled by a state regulator because Section 205 governs changes the utility itself proposes, not changes a state requires it to propose.3  OPSI may submit advisory alternatives, intervene, protest, or seek relief under Section 206, but should not compel PJM to advance a state proposal under Section 205.

Although the FPA prohibits states from holding Section 205 rights, there is no prohibition against the RTO engaging in timely and detailed consultation with the states in advance of such filing. PJM should gather state feedback, including any state-sponsored written proposal that would modify or replace PJM’s proposed filing. To demonstrate that PJM has thoughtfully considered such feedback, it is reasonable for PJM to commit to addressing such feedback in a public response to OPSI, within a reasonable time after any Section 205 filing addressing resource adequacy or regional transmission cost allocation.

15) Should the Members Committee have “jump ball” filing rights over the portions of the tariff where PJM has 205 filing rights when a supermajority of stakeholders approves an alternative proposal?

Members should retain their present Section 205 rights which can be exercised upon a super- majority vote of the Members Committee, but PJM should have the opportunity to make independent Section 205 filings. PJM should be the Section 205 filer and should not be delayed or prevented from filing its preferred proposal.  However, if a supermajority of the Members Committee approves one complete, germane alternative, PJM should include that alternative, its supporting record, the voting results, and PJM’s reasons for rejecting it. This would resemble the Commission-approved ISO New England (ISO-NE)/ New England Power Pool (NEPOOL) process, under which ISO-NE retains primary filing authority but transmits a qualifying stakeholder alternative. Such a jump-ball mechanism should not authorize Members to compel a filing where PJM proposes none or impair transmission owners’ separately held Section 205 rights.

IV. Stakeholder Process
There are many potential improvements that could be made to speed the stakeholder process, but these are secondary in importance to the questions posed above. PJM’s stakeholder process is designed to identify stakeholder interests, generate ideas, evaluate ideas consistently, and develop consensus outcomes. While some modifications to voting protocols may be beneficial, they are not the most significant impediment to the speed or quality of stakeholder process outcomes. Additionally, the institution of jump-ball 205 rights (as discussed above) would diminish the import of the voting thresholds to authorize Section 205 filings over the energy and ancillary services market and regional transmission planning.

16) Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered from two-thirds sector-weighted voting?

While lowering the Members Committee approval threshold from its current two-thirds supermajority could accelerate the passage of matters before it, any reduction should stop short of 60% — the minimum at which approval demands support from at least three sectors. A threshold below that level would fail to secure adequately diverse Member representation. On the other hand, the present two-thirds requirement necessitates the support of at least four sectors; as a consequence, any two sectors may pool their votes to exercise an effective veto.

17) Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing committees, also apply to lower-level committees?

Affiliate voting should be eliminated entirely. For a $5000 membership fee, any special purpose LLC under the ownership of Member can itself acquire membership and gain an affiliate vote that conveys little information about the preference of the diverse Membership interests. However, even if affiliate voting is eliminated, it does not naturally follow that sector-weighted voting is appropriate throughout the stakeholder committees.

Application of sector-weighted voting at lower-level committees is likely to impede idea generation and development.  Active participants at lower-level committees are often subject matter experts, not ultimate decision-makers for a Member. Depending on the subject matter, there may be only a small number of Members and a fraction of the sectors present (or their presence may be through a professional agent rather than an engineer, account, lawyer or other professional specifically versed in the matters the lower-level committee is undertaking). Consequently, application of sector-weighted voting may suffer from an insufficient quorum of the five sectors to convey meaningful preferences to senior committees.

Instead of sector-weighted voting at the lower-level committees, a potentially better approach would be allowing a simple majority vote of those present for a proposal to move forward to the more rigorous sector-weighted voting applied to Standing Committees and Senior Committees.

V. Conclusion
Vitol respectfully offers the responses above to the Commission’s Post Technical Conference queries.  Addressing Board independence and state participation will enable PJM to move forward more swiftly and confidently.  Swift decisions from an empowered and informed PJM Board will provide the Commission increased confidence in the proposals that the Board presents for consideration.
