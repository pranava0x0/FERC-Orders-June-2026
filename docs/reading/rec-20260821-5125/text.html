The Institute for Policy Integrity at New York University School of Law (Policy Integrity)1 respectfully submits the following comments to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) regarding PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM)’s Federal Power Act (FPA) Section 205 proposed tariff implementing Reliability Backstop Procurement (RBP). Policy Integrity is a non-partisan think tank dedicated to improving the quality of government decisionmaking through advocacy and scholarship in the fields of administrative law, economics, and public policy.

On July 31, 2026, pursuant to Section 205 of the FPA, PJM filed for revisions to its Open Access Transmission Tariff to implement RBP, which will include a Reliability Backstop Auction (Backstop Auction). These revisions are designed to address the capacity shortfall in the 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction (BRA, or Legacy Capacity Market) and future BRA shortfalls that PJM anticipates will occur.2 Part I of these comments provides context for PJM’s proposed tariff revisions. Part II explains that FERC should reject PJM’s proposed tariff because PJM has

II. FERC should reject the proposed tariff revisions because PJM has failed to demonstrate that the Backstop Auction is just and reasonable.

PJM has not met its burden to demonstrate that the Backstop Auction is just and reasonable for the following reasons:

1) PJM has not adequately explained why it is appropriate to run the Backstop Auction before three consecutive BRA shortfalls indicate long-term, systemic market problems;

2) PJM has not adequately explained why the Backstop Auction is appropriate given that it will not isolate the capacity market from the impacts of large load growth; and

3) PJM has not adequately explained why the Backstop Auction is warranted given that other alternatives will address the same problems at lower cost and with less risk of cost-shifting.

Because PJM cannot demonstrate that its proposed tariff is within the zone of reasonableness and has not provided substantial evidence showing that the revisions will result in just and reasonable rates, FERC should reject the proposed tariff revisions.20

B. FERC should reject the proposed tariff because PJM has not adequately
explained why it is appropriate to run the Backstop Auction before three
consecutive BRA shortfalls indicate long-term, systemic market problems.

PJM’s current tariff authorizes PJM to hold a reliability backstop auction when three consecutive BRAs clear short of the procurement target.28 It is important that PJM can run a backstop auction only when there are long-term systemic issues in the BRA, not based on short- term market dynamics, because consistent and stable market rules are critical for investment. FERC should reject the revised tariff because PJM has not provided substantial evidence to justify its decision to depart from the backstop provisions in its existing tariff, particularly given rapid and substantial reform occurring at the state and federal levels.29

Although the BRA cleared short of its target two years in a row, these shortfalls do not provide sufficient evidence that revising PJM’s current tariff is warranted. First, it is unclear whether the two BRA shortfalls would count towards triggering a backstop auction. The current tariff triggers a backstop auction only after three consecutive BRA shortfalls.30 But the tariff contemplates that BRAs will acquire capacity three years in advance of the targeted delivery year. PJM has strayed from the auction schedule stipulated in the tariff and held the last two auctions to acquire capacity for delivery years closely proximate to the auction year.31 Because of these delays, PJM has demonstrated evidence for only two near-term years of capacity shortfalls.32 PJM has not adequately explained why two years of near-term predicted capacity shortfalls are sufficient to identify systemic problems in the BRA, when the current tariff

D. FERC should reject the tariff revisions because PJM can address its resource adequacy mechanisms through other means at lower cost and with less risk

of cost-shifting.

There is no single just and reasonable rate, and PJM may choose from a range of options within the “zone of reasonableness.”53 Nevertheless, PJM must adequately explain and support its decisions with substantial evidence in order to meet its burden to demonstrate that the proposed tariff is just and reasonable.54 Here, PJM has failed to carry its burden because PJM has not adequately explained its decision to amend the tariff. While PJM’s tariff filing cites demand growth and generator retirements as reasons to run the Backstop Auction earlier than the current tariff authorizes, PJM does not explain why it is opting to hold the Backstop Auction given that existing or forthcoming resource adequacy mechanisms would solve the same problems at lower cost and with less risk of resulting in unjust and unreasonable rates.55

PJM is more likely to achieve just and reasonable rates by implementing IRAS, encouraging the bilateral trading process among sophisticated large loads, and focusing on its capacity market redesign efforts than by implementing a Backstop Auction with fixed, 15-year terms. Considering this alternative, PJM has not adequately explained its decision to resolve its resource adequacy problems through the Backstop Auction.

Under any version of the Backstop Auction, PJM must select proxies for demand. Demand includes the quantity of capacity customers wish to purchase (the procurement target) and customers’ “willingness-to- pay” for that capacity (the price cap, usually derived from the net cost of new entry). PJM proposes to approximate willingness-to-pay by creating a “soft cap” on the levelized cost per MW of capacity at $555/MW-day.58 As opposed to a “hard cap,” like a price collar or a price cap, the soft cap would allow bids over the limit as long as the total average paid per MW does not surpass $555/MW-day.59 While centralized auctions are generally considered to result in more efficient price discovery than private contracts, it is less likely that the Backstop Auction will accurately and efficiently balance the dynamics of supply and demand since PJM has to select artificial proxies for demand and willingness-to-pay.

III. If FERC permits PJM to implement the Backstop Auction, FERC should require PJM to adjust its proposed market design to mitigate the risk that the Backstop Auction will result in unjust and unreasonable rates.

FERC should reject the proposed tariff revisions because the Backstop Auction is not just and reasonable. But if FERC permits PJM to implement the Backstop Auction, it should require PJM to implement adjustments to the proposed market design which reduce the risk that the Backstop Auction will result in unjust and unreasonable rates.82 FERC should require PJM to adjust its proposed market design to mitigate the risks of over-procurement, unfair cost allocation, and overpaying for capacity.
