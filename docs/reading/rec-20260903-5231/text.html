COMMENTS OF
THE PJM INDUSTRIAL CUSTOMER COALITION

The PJM Industrial Customer Coalition (“PJMICC” or “Coalition”) respectfully submits these Comments in response to the Proposal of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. to Establish Interim Resource Adequacy Service and A Large Load Registry to Address System Reliability (“IRAS Filing”), which was filed at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or “Commission”) in the above-referenced docket.1 PJMICC is a coalition of energy-intensive industrial and institutional customers in the PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) region and is active in PJM’s governance and stakeholder processes.

As energy-intensive retail customers, PJMICC’s members are directly and substantially affected by the rules governing the PJM grid and its markets, including PJM’s rules to accommodate new large load growth. In PJMICC’s view, the rules to accommodate new large load growth must be fair to existing customers but also crafted to support the economic growth of the region. To that end, PJMICC offers the following Comments for Commission consideration.

I. BACKGROUND
On August 13, 2026, PJM filed the IRAS Filing with the Commission. Also on August 13, 2026, FERC issued a Combined Notice of Filings, setting a comment deadline of September 3, 2026. PJMICC filed a doc-less Motion to Intervene on August 14, 2026.

PJM’s IRAS Filing proposes revisions to the Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities (“RAA”) and the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) to establish the Interim Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”). PJM’s IRAS proposal broadly consists of five parts. First, PJM proposes to clarify a Load Serving Entity’s (“LSE”) obligation to bring in new capacity that is equal to or greater than a New Large Load’s2 peak load, or else be subject to load reduction requests during emergencies.3 Second, PJM seeks to create and maintain a Large Load Registry that will list existing Large Loads on the system and New Large Loads that plan to interconnect in the future.4 Third, PJM proposes an IRAS product, which it describes as “a new supplemental capacity product through which New Large Loads provide a load reduction service” – put simply, IRAS is a curtailment tool for deployment by PJM to manage resource adequacy shortfalls “that would otherwise drive shedding of native loads.”5 Fourth, PJM proposes the Bring Your Own New Capacity (“BYONC”) concept as a “category of new capacity eligible to address New Large Load capacity needs.”6 Specifically, BYONC is an incentive intended to address new capacity needs by reducing a New Large Load’s potential IRAS requirement in lockstep with the new capacity the New Large Load secures itself. Finally, PJM proposes to exclude New Large Loads from the Variable Resource Requirement (“VRR”) Curve in future Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) Auctions, to the extent they lack sufficient BYONC provisions. II. INTRODUCTION & EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
PJMICC is a coalition representing industrial consumers, institutional customers, and other large electricity users in the PJM Region. Coalition member organizations require significant volumes of electricity and natural gas to support their operations—they include manufacturers of paper, cement, chemicals, metals, pharmaceutical products, and consumer goods; institutional customers such as hospitals and universities; and other large energy users. Coalition members represent the backbone of American manufacturing and core services that support the United States’ competitive strength in a global economy. They are anchor employers across the PJM Region, supporting the regional economy with good-paying jobs and domestic production of goods and services.

For decades, PJMICC members and other similarly-situated retail customers have contributed to system reliability and invested in resource adequacy by participation in PJM’s capacity market, development of behind-the-meter generation, bilateral power purchase agreements, or supporting cost-of-service regulated generation. Many Coalition members have also made long-term investments in their on-site electrical infrastructure, strengthening the grid. However, PJMICC is concerned that large retail customers who have contributed to resource adequacy for many years may be required to bear costs triggered by the rapid growth of new large- load customers. For American industry, institutions, and other traditional energy users to thrive, their costs must be just, reasonable, and consistent with cost-causation principles.

To that end, the Coalition responds to the IRAS Filing with these Comments. Its recommendations can be summarized as follows:

 First, existing large loads should be excluded from the Large Load Registry. The registry’s purpose is to track new large loads driving new system costs, not existing customers already integrated into the grid and reflected in historical forecasts. If existing loads are included for informational purposes, they must be clearly exempted from all IRAS operational obligations — including curtailment, BYONC requirements, and RPM auction adjustments — and any collected data must be subject to robust confidentiality protections.

 Second, ordinary load fluctuations should not subject existing customers to IRAS. PJM’s definition of “new” vs. “existing” Large Load must distinguish between genuine new load growth and normal operational variation at existing facilities. The baseline measurement should not confuse normal business operational fluctuations as being new load growth associated with business expansions, triggering IRAS for customers that have effectively been operating as existing Large Loads.

 Third, incremental expansions should trigger IRAS obligations only for the net new MW above 50 MW. As a threshold matter, it must be noted that very little evidence has been presented that 50 MW is an appropriate bright-line trigger for the obligations set forth by PJM’s instant filing.  Many of the new computational loads are significantly in excess of 50 MW, whereas 50 MW is not unusual for an industrial or institutional facility.  When an existing Large Load facility expands, only load growth over 50 MW from the baseline should be subject to IRAS. (That is, a 50 MW facility should not be subjected to IRAS until it expands beyond 100 MW.) PJM should adopt bright-line, easy-to-implement grandfathering rules for pre-expansion load, clear methodologies for measuring incremental growth, and anti-gaming provisions, while avoiding a framework that chills legitimate phased and planned development.

 Fourth, IRAS participants should not receive compensation that shifts costs to other ratepayers. Compensating new large loads for IRAS curtailment — and recovering those costs broadly through retail rates — directly contradicts the Ratepayer Protection Pledge’s commitment that new computational loads will not increase electricity costs for other consumers. If any compensation is provided, it should be funded by the new large-load customer base itself; States must retain full discretion to set the rate at zero without risking a violation of the filed rate doctrine.

 Finally, PJM’s “peak load” measurement in the New Large Load definition should align with FERC-approved retail BTMG rules. Load netted against on-site generation under BTMG should be measured in a similar manner for IRAS purposes, providing certainty to retail customers who have invested in resource adequacy through retail BTMG.

PJMICC respectfully requests that the Commission consider these Comments in its review of the IRAS Filing. Each of these points is set forth in greater detail below.

III. COMMENTS
A. Existing Large Loads Should Be Excluded from the Large Load Registry.
PJM explains in the IRAS Filing that the Large Load Registry (“Registry”) will contain information about Large Loads in the PJM Region that will “facilitate the transparent implementation of IRAS and assist the Commission and states in monitoring whether loads are, in fact adhering to the Ratepayer Protection Pledge (“Pledge”) commitment to ‘build, bring, or buy the new generation resources and electricity needed to satisfy their new energy demands.’”8 Specifically, PJM will use the Large Load Registry to identify and quantify New Large Loads by zone or area and “allocate IRAS responsibility to each zone/area accordingly,” beginning with the Delivery Year starting June 1, 2027.9

PJMICC does not object to the need to track New Large Loads with IRAS responsibility. However, PJMICC disagrees with PJM’s decision to place all Large Loads—including Existing Large Loads—into the Registry, for the following reasons.

i. First, the Primary Purpose of the Registry Is To Facilitate IRAS, Which
Does Not Apply to Existing Large Loads.
Existing users, including large industrial and commercial users, have historically paid for the generation, transmission, and distribution infrastructure they use. Existing large users are not the source of the new costs driving the need for IRAS and, by extension, a Large Load Registry. This cost causation principle, which is a foundational element of utility rate design, requires that costs be allocated to the customers who cause them. Existing large retail customers are not driving new infrastructure costs and should not be subjected to new tracking and potential curtailment

ii. The Large Load Registry Imposes New Privacy Concerns Upon Existing
Large Load Customers, Without Adequate Justification.
The Large Load Registry proposes collecting substantial operational data, raising new privacy concerns for existing customers who should not be subject to the IRAS. Data such as customer identity, location, in-service date, delivery point, load quantity, and backup generation details represent confidential information that existing customers have never been required to report in this manner. For energy-intensive industries, such as steel, air separation, paper, and consumer goods manufacturers, knowledge of load size, location, Demand Response (“DR”) participation, and LSE relationships enables competitors (including international competitors) to infer critical operational intelligence. For example, publicly disclosing which DR programs a steel mill participates in would provide competitors with insights into its operations and cost structure. Further, in restructured states, a customer’s supplier relationship is typically confidential, and public disclosure of retail customers’ LSEs could affect the functioning of retail markets. State-regulated utilities—with whom large retail customers have a direct relationship— already have much or all of this information, but it is often subject to state confidentiality rules. These customers’ local utilities understand the critical sensitivity of this information. It is carefully guarded by American industry.

While PJM may argue that including existing loads helps improve overall forecast accuracy, the forecasting problem driving this initiative is new load growth, not existing load that is already captured in historical data. To the extent that existing loads are included for informational purposes, the Commission should require PJM to limit the information collected to

iii. Additional Substantive and Due Process Protections Are Needed If Existing Large Loads Are Included in the Registry.

PJMICC firmly argues that under no circumstances should existing Large Loads be subject to IRAS curtailment obligations, which, by PJM’s own design, apply only to New Large Loads that do not bring their own capacity. However, the Registry, as proposed, does not contain provisions that ensure IRAS load is not conflated with existing load. If Existing Large Loads are included in the Registry, the Commission should require PJM to incorporate additional classification or disclosures within the Registry to ensure that IRAS is not assigned to loads that are merely experiencing year-over-year normal operation fluctuations. Additionally, Registry determinations, as currently proposed, lack clear customer notice, correction, and appeal procedures. Per the filing, PJM may validate information with Transmission Owners and Electric Distributors, request additional information, and “consult” with the Large Load, but the Tariff does not establish a customer right to notice, review, correction, hearing, or appeal before classification and eligible New Large Load amounts become operative. Unremedied errors concerning peak load, in-service date, affiliation, site boundaries, BYONC, or Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) allocation can directly change curtailment exposure and capacity treatment. The IRAS Filing also invokes significant enforcement consequences for false or incomplete information without providing a corresponding safe harbor for good faith estimates or corrections. This is concerning for existing customers, some of whom operate complex configurations with multiple facilities, generation integrated with industrial processes, and other nuances that may make reporting obligations less than straightforward. Any such process should also minimize the

Further, any Registry data collected from existing customers must be subject to robust confidentiality protection. The Registry may include site descriptions, locations, in-service schedules, peak loads, telemetry, backup generation and permitting limits, contracts or service agreements, and contact information. As explained above, this is highly sensitive competitive intelligence for many retail customers. Additionally, the disclosure of this information creates cybersecurity and commercial information risks. While the Registry is intended to administer IRAS transparently, it must not pose operational or competitive risks due to inadequate confidentiality safeguards. PJMICC respectfully requests that the Commission require PJM to implement appropriate safeguards, privacy restrictions, due process, and, for non-IRAS load, limit the amount of data required for the Registry.

B. Ordinary Load Fluctuations Should Not Subject Existing Retail Customers to IRAS.

As PJM states in the IRAS Filing, IRAS is designed as a “supplemental capacity product through which New Large Loads provide a load reduction service.”10 PJM drafted the IRAS Filing, including the proposed definition of New Large Load, to address resource adequacy challenges on the grid primarily driven by large load growth.

PJMICC agrees with the focus on new and expanding load (to the extent not otherwise considered to be “organic” load growth). However, the Coalition is concerned that, under the proposed definition of New Large Load, existing Large Load customers may be mistakenly deemed New Large Load solely due to ordinary load fluctuations.

Ordinary load fluctuations can occur for a manufacturer, for example, during a slower production year or when weather patterns are milder than usual. To use a simple illustration, consider a customer facility whose peak load this Delivery Year is 48 MW but whose peak load was 54 MW in the prior two Delivery Years. If, in the next Delivery Year, this customer’s peak load returns to 54 MW, PJM may deem it to have 6 MW of load growth. Under the New Large Load definition, it is unclear if PJM would deem the entire facility as “New Large Load” or only a part of it. Either way, the customer facility is merely returning to its typical historical levels after a slow business cycle year (such as after COVID). It would not make sense to impose 6 MW (or 54 MW, if PJM fails to recognize only the incremental increase) of IRAS and Registry obligations on a customer facility that has been paying to support resource adequacy for years, if not decades. This is an important issue for several reasons. First, the point of IRAS is not to burden existing customers – even existing Large Load customers – with issues that are caused by rapid new load growth. Second, it is important to be clear as to the rules of the road, so energy-intensive businesses can continue to do the work it does every day without worrying about triggering additional obligations (that are designed to accommodate the new, super-sized large load growth). Third, incremental modernization or electrification at facilities should not create new obligations for existing loads.

To address this issue, PJMICC offers the following recommendation: the Commission should require PJM to establish a “baseline” load value for existing Large Load customers based on historic usage.  Any value established for a customer should accommodate, if not facilitate, existing Large Load customers’ ability to respond to changing grid and resource adequacy conditions, such as, for example, moving away from “peak management” strategies toward new

C. Incremental Expansions Should Trigger IRAS Obligations Only for the Net
New MW Above 50 MW.
Another concern for existing industrial and institutional customers is what the process looks like for incremental load growth—for example, when a university adds a new building or a factory adds a production line.

Currently, the proposed definition of New Large Load includes (a) “a Large Load that goes into service after June 1, 2027” or (b) “incremental load in-service after June 1, 2027, that increases the existing end-use customer load at a single electrical site, that, prior to such incremental load, did not previously meet the definition of Large Load.” Presumably, this would mean that a 45 MW facility that undergoes a 10 MW expansion would be deemed New Large Load.

This approach fundamentally puts existing large loads at a competitive disadvantage. If a new facility can be built at 20 MW without triggering IRAS obligations, surely a 50 MW facility that has been paying into the system for decades should be able to expand to 70 MW (an equivalent 20 MW update) on a similar basis. To obligate the latter to IRAS, but not the former, would be unduly discriminatory against existing customers.

Unfortunately, PJM’s proposal does not clearly address how such incremental expansions should be treated. First, as a threshold matter, PJM must be clear that only incremental expansion should be classified as New Large Load. The proposed definition of New Large Load includes “incremental load in-service after June 1, 2027, that increases the existing end-use customer load at a single electrical site that, prior to such incremental load, did not previously meet the definition of Large Load.” While this appears to address incremental growth of existing retail customers whose “baseline” is under 50 MW and that then expand, the definition does not expressly address

PDF page 11

11

situations where an existing Large Load (already >50 MW) expands. PJM should calculate a “baseline” peak load (as discussed above) for each Large Load so that it is clear when there has been real growth at a customer location and should then explain that only the incremental MW becomes “New Large Load.”

Second, the Commission should order PJM to require that, for existing Large Load customers, only the incremental growth above and beyond 50 MW of net new load should be classified as new load. This approach is the clearest and least discriminatory, as it avoids putting existing retail customers who have paid for capacity at a competitive disadvantage. Without clarification that an existing facility may add up to 50 MW of expansion without triggering IRAS obligations, there is a distinct risk of chilling legitimate expansions. Treating the entire facility as a New Large Load upon expansion could discourage rational, phased development at existing facilities.

In summary, PJM should establish clear rules for phased expansions, including the following:

 A methodology for measuring both “baseline” peak load and incremental load growth at existing facilities.

 A bright-line rule that the pre-expansion load quantity is grandfathered and not subject to IRAS. The load would be treated as a New Large Load only after 50 MW is added to the customer’s baseline load.

 Provisions addressing the timing of registration obligations for expansions (i.e., registration should be required only when the incremental expansion is contracted or committed, not when the original facility was interconnected). Additionally, PJM should address the potential for gaming (e.g., a customer structuring multiple sub-50 MW facilities within a one-mile radius to avoid the threshold) while still protecting legitimate existing multiple sub-50 MW operations within one-mile from being swept into the Large Load framework.

These updates to PJM’s proposal would provide significant protections for existing retail customers and avoid stifling economic investment by American manufacturing, education and medical institutions, and other existing large retail customers.

D. IRAS Participants Should Not Receive Compensation That Shifts Costs to
Other Ratepayers
PJM proposes that New Large Loads subject to IRAS curtailment during grid emergencies would receive wholesale compensation at a maximum rate equal to 50% of the Non-Performance Charge Rate.11 PJM also proposes that Large Loads may waive such compensation consistent with any Large Loads’ signing of the Pledge.12

PJM does not propose a specific cost recovery mechanism for IRAS compensation, stating that cost recovery “will be left to the Electric Distributors that directed the specific load reductions to implement IRAS, consistent with their state-approved program.”13 PJM states that “[w]hether a specific New Large Load may be entitled to compensation for provision of IRAS will be determined by state authorities under state law….”14

Unfortunately, PJM’s approach is flawed. By establishing a FERC-jurisdictional payment obligation to IRAS load, PJM creates tensions with the Pledge and the filed rate doctrine and will make it particularly difficult for States to protect existing customers from having to pay IRAS customers for their reductions.

i. PJM’s Proposed Compensation Is Inconsistent with the Ratepayer
Protection Pledge.
The Ratepayer Protection Pledge (“Pledge”) Proclamation, issued by the President on March 4, 2026, provides that “data centers’ energy needs will not increase household electricity costs for American citizens” and that data center companies “will build, bring, or buy the new generation resources and electricity needed to satisfy their energy demands, and pay for all new power delivery infrastructure upgrades.”15 The Pledge itself states that “Companies will build,

At heart, the Pledge’s commitment is to consumer protection. The Pledge contemplates that data centers bear their own costs, not receive payments from the system after failing to bring their own capacity. The Pledge’s framework is premised on data centers paying their own way. If new data center loads that do not bring their own capacity receive IRAS compensation recovered through retail rates, that cost is shifted to other ratepayers—directly contradicting the Pledge’s commitment that data centers will not increase electricity costs for other consumers. In fact, the risk that these costs are shifted to large retail, industrial, and institutional customers is heightened, because many States are focused primarily on preventing cost shifts to residential customers. However, curtailment of IRAS load should not be compensated by other ratepayers (whether residential, small commercial, or industrial/institutional); rather, it is the condition upon which a New Large Load can begin taking service sooner without bringing matching capacity to the system.

ii. PJM’s Approach to IRAS Compensation Creates Filed Rate Doctrine and
Consumer Protection Risks for States.
Once FERC approves a wholesale rate—such as the maximum IRAS compensation rate— it is difficult for States to set rates inconsistent with FERC-jurisdictional rates. As a legal matter, States may not undermine FERC-jurisdictional rates.17 However, States are also limited, as a practical matter of rate setting, in allocating rates in a manner inconsistent with the underlying

Consequently, if FERC approves a maximum wholesale compensation rate for IRAS, states may be constrained in their ability to decline to pass through those costs or to set retail rates that nullify the compensation. Although PJM characterizes IRAS compensation as subject to state discretion, a FERC-approved wholesale rate may create a federal floor that states cannot easily undercut. E. PJM’s Definition of New Large Load Should Track with PJM’s BTMG Rules.
In the IRAS Filing, PJM mentions co-located load, but it does not explain how Behind- the-Meter Generation (“BTMG”) rules interact with IRAS obligations.18 In PJM’s Co-Located Load proceeding before the FERC, the Commission has ordered PJM to maintain Behind-the- Meter Generation (“BTMG”) rules for retail BTMG up to 50 MW.19 The Commission should require PJM to clarify that its measurement of “peak load” in the proposed definition of New Large Load should track with retail BTMG rules approved by the Commission. For example, if an 80 MW load with 50 MW of generation is tagged at 30 MW pursuant to retail BTMG rules, it should be considered 30 MW for purposes of IRAS, as well. This approach will provide certainty and consistency to retail customers who have invested in resource adequacy through retail BTMG.
