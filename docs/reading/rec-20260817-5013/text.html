Mark Christie, Williamsburg, VA.
     I file this comment on behalf of myself, not any other person or party.    
I comment as a former Virginia utility regulator of 17 years in the PJM region, as a former president of the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”), a board member of OPSI for more than a decade, and a former commissioner and chairman of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission”).

     I applaud Chair Swett and the Commission for conducting this timely and compelling proceeding.  During my long history as a state and federal regulator dealing with PJM issues, I came to the conclusion that PJM’s governance system was fatally flawed from its very beginning a quarter century ago.  PJM as constituted and governed to meet the requirements of Order No. 2000  (as opposed to PJM the power pool dating back to the 1920s) was set up to be a de facto regional government for the power grid, but subject only to some degree of Commission authority and wholly exempt from state authority, and with no state role in governance, even though tens of millions of consumers across the states within PJM are deeply affected by PJM’s actions.   

Major reforms in governance must be made if PJM is to survive and, much more importantly, if it is to serve the public interest in the 13 states and DC which make up PJM’s territory.  

	To borrow Commissioner See’s terminology at the conference asking the first panel to identify needed reforms by “buckets,” here are my recommended four buckets:


1.	Board 205 Rights:  The PJM Board of Managers should have Federal Power Act (FPA) Section 205 filing rights across all major business functions, including transmission planning, cost allocation, the RTEP, as well as the energy and ancillary services markets.  “All” means all.  PJM stakeholders should have the right to comment on potential 205 filings, but not to block or influence the Board's decision to file a 205.  Adopt for PJM essentially the CAISO model. 

2.	Board Independence:    Stakeholders should not be able to fire Board members, as happened recently.  As Comm. LeCerte so aptly put it, "The players should not be able to fire the referees if they don't like their calls."  Board independence is absolutely essential.  Anything less than full Board independence will be a failed reform ab initio.  

3.	Board Nominations, Terms and Requirements:  

a.	The states, acting through the PJM Governors, should nominate either all or at least a majority of the voting Board members.   The stakeholders who stand to profit financially from PJM’s actions should not be choosing the Board, nor should any other interest group.  To expand on Comm. LeCerte’s football imagery, the players should neither be able to fire nor choose the referees.  

b.	Board members should get a single term of seven (7) years with one new member chosen annually, to maintain continuity and institutional knowledge.  The advantage of a single term removes any motivation for a Board member to maneuver to seek re-appointment.  The Board itself should have the authority to remove any member for misfeasance, malfeasance or nonfeasance in office.   And a minimum of five Board members should have his or her primary residence, and be a retail customer of a load-serving utility, in a PJM state.  

4.	State 205 Filing Rights:  States should have FPA Section 205 filing rights as to transmission planning criteria and cost allocation, for the obvious reason that PJM policies on these matters flow right into retail consumers’ monthly power bills.   This authority should rest solely with the state utility regulators of OPSI – who have the technical expertise – to be exercised when the states have a supermajority in favor of a proposal, as already set forth in the OPSI by-laws.  I do not agree with those who claim that Section 205 makes it illegal for state utility regulators to have the authority to make a 205 filing at the Commission.  If that were true, then states in ISO-NE, MISO and SPP have been exercising illegal authority for many years.   I would recommend authorizing OPSI to make a 205 filing through PJM.   PJM could file an alternative proposal if it wished, as could the transmission owners.   That would be an acceptable “jump ball” arrangement, but I would strongly oppose any “jump ball” in which the states can only file when PJM or the transmission owners also file a proposal.  That would give PJM and the transmission owners an effective veto by doing nothing.

State 205 filing rights on transmission planning criteria and cost allocation are essential.   Two of the biggest flaws in the PJM Order 2000 structure was to deny the states any serious role in PJM governance and to deny the states any substantive authority to develop and file alternative transmission planning and cost allocation proposals, even though those issues directly affect states’ consumers.   And spare me the argument that “the states can always file a 206.”  Anyone in the phone book can file an FPA Section 206 complaint, and FERC can and often does ignore 206 filings.

	It appeared from the technical conference there is at least a majority of Commission members who are ready and determined to make the fundamental changes to PJM governance that have been critically needed for the past 25 years.    With the strong support from the presidential administration, expressed in the speeches prior to the first panel from the Deputy Secretary of Energy and the representative of the National Energy Dominance Council, the Commission can order fundamental reforms to PJM governance now, even if it ultimately takes the Commission issuing its own Rule to Show Cause under Section 206.   

	So I urge the Commission:  Do not back off to avoid push-back from influential stakeholder interests who do not want to give up their power within PJM and never will do so voluntarily.   Do not settle for cosmetic half-measures to get some “kumbaya” moment that falls short of what is needed.  

After a quarter century, this is the moment for fundamental governance reforms to PJM.  I urge the Commission:  meet the moment.


/s/  Mark C. Christie
