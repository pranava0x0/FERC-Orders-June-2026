Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (Commission) 
Rules of Practice and Procedure,1 the Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)2 and 
Advanced Energy United (United)3 submit this protest of the Midcontinent Independent System 
Operator, Inc.’s (MISO) LARS filing.4  
In the LARS filing, MISO proposes a process that would study load and generation on a 
coordinated basis. But to enter that process, a load and generation owner must be granted access 
by the Transmission Customer, which in MISO, is also often a vertically integrated Load Serving 
Entity (LSE) and Transmission Owner (TO).5 In this way, a Transmission Customer is also a 
direct competitor to independent power producers in developing generation in MISO. MISO 
proposes to effectively grant the Transmission Customer, which has an incentive to discriminate 
against competitive generation, the ability to discriminate against competitive generation. By

decide if their competitors may participate in the LARS process.

Cause Order.7 As relevant here, the Show Cause Order found that MISO’s tariff may be unjust 
and unreasonable because it does not contain provisions allowing an interconnection customer to 
seek interconnection service that reflects the operational dynamics of serving electrically

Under the LARS Proposal, MISO would study load and generation pairs in parallel, with 
both study processes brought under a common application.9 To be eligible for LARS, an 
Interconnection Customer and the LARS Associated Load must provide an agreement 
demonstrating that the output of the LARS Generating Facility will serve the LARS Associated

“often a Load Serving Entity (“LSE”).”12 The LARS Associated Transmission Customer is the

As MISO states, the LARS Associated Transmission Customer is often a Load Serving 
Entity,14 and in the MISO region, LSE are often vertically integrated utilities that have a 
generation affiliate and/or build their own generation.15 These entities have a competitive motive 
to discriminate in favor of their own facilities.16 Utility-owned generators are competing with 
independent power producers for the ability to be the power procurement solution for new large 
loads in MISO. MISO’s proposed LARS creates a process wherein that competition to serve a 
new LARS Associated Load is organized and refereed by one of the competitors – the 
Transmission Customer utility. The Transmission Customer is the utility party with an interest in

is a market participant.”20 Despite this being a process under the MISO Tariff, MISO designed 
LARS to ensure it is the Transmission Customer—who has the incentive to discriminate against 
competitors—that controls access to the LARS process.

B. The LARS proposal is unjust and unreasonable because it conditions 
generators’ interconnection rights on specific large loads while risking double-
counted transmission headroom, harm to earlier-queued projects, and 
improper cost shifts. 
i. LARS makes a generator’s rights depend on a specific large load, 
exposing viable projects to operational and financial uncertainty if 
that load departs. 
MISO’s proposed LARS framework provides expedited Interconnection Service based on 
a generator’s relationship with a particular large load,26 but fails to address the very real 
possibility that the relationship may be altered over time due to business decisions of the large 
load, creating significant uncertainty regarding the long-term durability of a generator’s 
interconnection rights.27 Without any kind of transition mechanism, this uncertainty may force 
otherwise viable generating resources into an uncertain and potentially disruptive transition 
process. By transferring the risk of the load customer’s future business decisions to the generator, 
the proposal could penalize generators for circumstances they cannot control and thereby deter 
otherwise viable projects from using the LARS pathway. 
Under the LARS Proposal, a generator’s eligibility for LARS Interconnection Service is 
tied to the continued existence of a specific associated large load for a substantial portion of the 
generator’s operating life.28 LARS provides off-ramps when an associated load departs.29 If the 
LARS Associated Load withdraws, ceases taking transmission service, or the required 
commercial agreement terminates during the first fifteen years, the generator’s LLGIA ultimately 
will be terminated and the generator must obtain new Interconnection Service through the 
Definitive Planning Process (DPP) to continue operating.30 This proposed structure creates a

material risk that a generator could lose the benefit of its expedited interconnection path through 
no fault of its own. Large-load facilities may cease operations, relocate, reduce demand, change 
suppliers, or otherwise alter their commercial arrangements for reasons unrelated to the 
generator’s performance. When this occurs, the generator faces uncertainty regarding its future

MISO acknowledges that the proposed process could “strand a Generating Facility” with 
remaining useful life and create “significant long-term commercial risk” affecting project 
financing,32 yet its proposed structure does not resolve those concerns. The risk of stranding a 
generator is particularly acute given the long development timelines and financing horizons of 
generation projects. Developers, lenders, and investors require predictable, durable 
interconnection rights. Conditioning those rights on the continuation of a particular customer 
relationship introduces uncertainty that may undermine financing and increase project costs. In 
effect, the proposal requires developers to evaluate not only the viability of the generation 
project, but also the associated load’s long-term business prospects. 
The proposed transition mechanism is woefully inadequate to address this stranded asset 
risk. If an associated load departs during the period in which the generator remains subject to the 
fifteen-year pairing requirement, the generator’s LLGIA ultimately may be terminated and the 
generator must obtain new Interconnection Service through the DPP to continue operations.33 
MISO proposes only a temporary transition period of up to one year, during which the generating 
facility would remain subject to Quarterly Operating Limits (QOL) and Annual ERIS (AERIS) 
restrictions while seeking new Interconnection Service through the DPP.34 Although MISO states

operating becomes subject to substantial uncertainty because of events occurring outside the 
generator's control. Such outcomes would undermine investment certainty and could discourage 
participation in the very framework MISO seeks to establish.

ii. MISO has not demonstrated how transmission capability will be 
transparently managed across parallel LARS and DPP study 
processes to protect earlier-queued projects from double-counted

MISO has not adequately demonstrated that the proposed LARS framework will preserve 
transparency and certainty for customers currently progressing through the Generator 
Interconnection Queue. While MISO states that protecting the DPP process was an express 
consideration during development of the proposal,39 there is limited detail regarding how 
transmission capability assumptions will be coordinated between LARS and DPP studies or how 
potential conflicts will be identified and resolved before stakeholders make significant 
commercial and development decisions.40  
Under the proposal, LARS and DPP requests will be studied separately and using 
different assumptions.41 MISO specifically states that LARS studies will not include 
interconnection requests currently in the generator interconnection queue that have not executed 
GIAs.42 As a result, projects advancing through the two processes may be evaluated based on 
different assumptions regarding future system conditions and available transmission capability.43

III. 
ALTERNATIVE RECOMMENDATION 
A. Alternatively, the Commission should clarify that the LARS application 
process must be administered on a nondiscriminatory basis.   
 
If the Commission disagrees with SEIA and United that MISO’s LARS proposal is unjust 
and unreasonable, or unduly discriminatory, and accepts the proposal, SEIA and United request, 
in the alternative, that the Commission expressly clarify that the LARS Associated Transmission 
Customer’s exclusive initial application role does not authorize discriminatory treatment of an 
otherwise qualified applicant. As discussed above, the Federal Power Act and Order No. 2003 
require just and reasonable, nondiscriminatory access to jurisdictional transmission and generator 
interconnection service.49 To preserve those principles, the Commission should expressly require 
nondiscriminatory administration of LARS from the outset. 
Specifically, the Commission should clarify that once an applicant meets all applicable 
LARS eligibility, readiness, financial security, and application requirements, the Commission 
expects the LARS Associated Transmission Customer to apply the same submission standards 
and timelines it applies to its own similarly situated projects. During stakeholder engagement, 
MISO responded to concerns about limiting application submission to LSEs by explaining that 
IPPs could participate as Interconnection Customers, even though the Associated Transmission 
Customer would initiate the application.50 That response, however, does not resolve the concern
