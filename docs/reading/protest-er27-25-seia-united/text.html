I. PROTEST
A. The LARS Proposal is unduly discriminatory because it allows LSEs to
decide if their competitors may participate in the LARS process.

MISO’s filing is part of its efforts to comply with the Commission’s June 18, 2026, Show Cause Order.7 As relevant here, the Show Cause Order found that MISO’s tariff may be unjust and unreasonable because it does not contain provisions allowing an interconnection customer to seek interconnection service that reflects the operational dynamics of serving electrically proximate load.8

Under the LARS Proposal, MISO would study load and generation pairs in parallel, with both study processes brought under a common application.9 To be eligible for LARS, an Interconnection Customer and the LARS Associated Load must provide an agreement demonstrating that the output of the LARS Generating Facility will serve the LARS Associated Load.10 This agreement, along with the other LARS requirements, would be part of the LARS Application submitted by the LARS Associated Transmission Customer,11 which is the “entity that procures Transmission Service for load and has a direct relationship with the load,” and “often a Load Serving Entity (“LSE”).”12 The LARS Associated Transmission Customer is the only party that may initiate the LARS Application.13

As MISO states, the LARS Associated Transmission Customer is often a Load Serving Entity,14 and in the MISO region, LSE are often vertically integrated utilities that have a generation affiliate and/or build their own generation.15 These entities have a competitive motive to discriminate in favor of their own facilities.16 Utility-owned generators are competing with independent power producers for the ability to be the power procurement solution for new large loads in MISO. MISO’s proposed LARS creates a process wherein that competition to serve a new LARS Associated Load is organized and refereed by one of the competitors – the Transmission Customer utility. Moreover, the Transmission Customer is often the same utility that owns the existing transmission infrastructure and is responsible for constructing the additional transmission facilities needed to accommodate the interconnection customer’s generation and associated load. In Order No. 2003, the Commission found that “public utilities owning or controlling jurisdictional transmission facilities had the incentive to engage in, and had engaged in, unduly discriminatory transmission practices.”17 Because interconnection is an element of transmission service, the Commission established standard terms and procedures to “remedy undue discrimination and preferences under Sections 205 and 206 of the Federal Power Act.”18 The Commission did state that it would allow Regional Transmission Organizations and Independent System Operators (RTOs/ISOs), such as MISO, to propose independent entity variations for pricing and non-pricing provisions.19 The Commission granted RTOs this flexibility because an RTO “is less likely to act in an unduly discriminatory manner than a Transmission Provider that is a market participant.”20 Despite this being a process under the MISO Tariff, MISO designed LARS to ensure it is the Transmission Customer—who has the incentive to discriminate against competitors—that controls access to the LARS process.

Yes, independent power producers can participate in the LARS process—if their competitors will let them.

B. The LARS proposal is unjust and unreasonable because it conditions
generators’ interconnection rights on specific large loads while risking double- counted transmission headroom, harm to earlier-queued projects, and

improper cost shifts.
i. LARS makes a generator’s rights depend on a specific large load,
exposing viable projects to operational and financial uncertainty if
that load departs.
MISO’s proposed LARS framework provides expedited Interconnection Service based on a generator’s relationship with a particular large load,26 but fails to address the very real possibility that the relationship may be altered over time due to business decisions of the large load, creating significant uncertainty regarding the long-term durability of a generator’s interconnection rights.27 Without any kind of transition mechanism, this uncertainty may force otherwise viable generating resources into an uncertain and potentially disruptive transition process. By transferring the risk of the load customer’s future business decisions to the generator, the proposal could penalize generators for circumstances they cannot control and thereby deter otherwise viable projects from using the LARS pathway.

Under the LARS Proposal, a generator’s eligibility for LARS Interconnection Service is tied to the continued existence of a specific associated large load for a substantial portion of the generator’s operating life.28 LARS provides off-ramps when an associated load departs.29 If the LARS Associated Load withdraws, ceases taking transmission service, or the required commercial agreement terminates during the first fifteen years, the generator’s LLGIA ultimately will be terminated and the generator must obtain new Interconnection Service through the Definitive Planning Process (DPP) to continue operating.30 This proposed structure creates a

material risk that a generator could lose the benefit of its expedited interconnection path through no fault of its own. Large-load facilities may cease operations, relocate, reduce demand, change suppliers, or otherwise alter their commercial arrangements for reasons unrelated to the generator’s performance. When this occurs, the generator faces uncertainty regarding its future operating rights and potential migration into the DPP process.31

MISO acknowledges that the proposed process could “strand a Generating Facility” with remaining useful life and create “significant long-term commercial risk” affecting project financing,32 yet its proposed structure does not resolve those concerns. The risk of stranding a generator is particularly acute given the long development timelines and financing horizons of generation projects. Developers, lenders, and investors require predictable, durable interconnection rights. Conditioning those rights on the continuation of a particular customer relationship introduces uncertainty that may undermine financing and increase project costs. In effect, the proposal requires developers to evaluate not only the viability of the generation project, but also the associated load’s long-term business prospects.

The proposed transition mechanism is woefully inadequate to address this stranded asset risk. If an associated load departs during the period in which the generator remains subject to the fifteen-year pairing requirement, the generator’s LLGIA ultimately may be terminated and the generator must obtain new Interconnection Service through the DPP to continue operations.33 MISO proposes only a temporary transition period of up to one year, during which the generating facility would remain subject to Quarterly Operating Limits (QOL) and Annual ERIS (AERIS) restrictions while seeking new Interconnection Service through the DPP.34 Although MISO states

MISO has not adequately demonstrated that the proposed LARS framework will preserve transparency and certainty for customers currently progressing through the Generator Interconnection Queue. While MISO states that protecting the DPP process was an express consideration during development of the proposal,39 there is limited detail regarding how transmission capability assumptions will be coordinated between LARS and DPP studies or how potential conflicts will be identified and resolved before stakeholders make significant commercial and development decisions.40

Under the proposal, LARS and DPP requests will be studied separately and using different assumptions.41 MISO specifically states that LARS studies will not include interconnection requests currently in the generator interconnection queue that have not executed GIAs.42 As a result, projects advancing through the two processes may be evaluated based on different assumptions regarding future system conditions and available transmission capability.43

III. ALTERNATIVE RECOMMENDATION
A. Alternatively, the Commission should clarify that the LARS application
process must be administered on a nondiscriminatory basis.

If the Commission disagrees with SEIA and United that MISO’s LARS proposal is unjust and unreasonable, or unduly discriminatory, and accepts the proposal, SEIA and United request, in the alternative, that the Commission expressly clarify that the LARS Associated Transmission Customer’s exclusive initial application role does not authorize discriminatory treatment of an otherwise qualified applicant. As discussed above, the Federal Power Act and Order No. 2003 require just and reasonable, nondiscriminatory access to jurisdictional transmission and generator interconnection service.49 To preserve those principles, the Commission should expressly require nondiscriminatory administration of LARS from the outset.

Specifically, the Commission should clarify that once an applicant meets all applicable LARS eligibility, readiness, financial security, and application requirements, the Commission expects the LARS Associated Transmission Customer to apply the same submission standards and timelines it applies to its own similarly situated projects. During stakeholder engagement, MISO responded to concerns about limiting application submission to LSEs by explaining that IPPs could participate as Interconnection Customers, even though the Associated Transmission Customer would initiate the application.50 That response, however, does not resolve the concern

An express clarification would provide notice of the governing standard and strengthen the basis for case-specific relief while preserving MISO’s proposed application structure.
