Comments of Advanced Energy United
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“Commission”) Rules of Practice and Procedures,[fn:1] Advanced Energy United[fn:2] (“United”) offers these comments in response to the Midcontinent Independent System Operator’s (“MISO”) proposal (“Transmittal”) to establish a Zero Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement (“ZGIA”) framework.[fn:3] United supports acceptance of the tariff revisions subject to a deferred implementation date coordinated with MISO’s forthcoming Large Load Addition Resource Study ("LARS") and large-load compliance filings.
United supports a broad portfolio of technologies, products, and services that enhance U.S. competitiveness and economic growth through an efficient, high-performing energy system that is reliable and affordable. United recognizes the ongoing reliability and resource adequacy challenges across the country, including in MISO. Improving the “speed to power”[fn:4] for co-located loads that will have little to no impact on the broader transmission system will help reduce pressure on an already backlogged interconnection queue. MISO’s proposed provisions provide a reliable and efficient framework for interconnection of large loads that could eliminate some of the reliability concerns associated with large load interconnection. These provisions will help to ensure just and reasonable rates for customers and maintain system reliability, consistent with Section 205 of the FPA.[fn:5] For these reasons, United requests that the Commission accept the proposed revisions.
Background
The United States is experiencing significant growth in large load interconnection requests, driven by data centers, advanced manufacturing, electrification, and other energy-intensive uses. This trend is evident in the MISO region, where concentrated new loads are placing increasing pressure on transmission planning and interconnection processes. In response to similar developments across organized markets, the Commission issued its Large Load Orders[fn:6] in each Regional Transmission Organization and Independent System Operator (“RTO/ISO”) region, seeking greater clarity regarding the rates, terms, and conditions applicable to flexible large loads, co-location arrangements, load served by behind-the-meter generation, and generating facilities located near or co-located with load.
MISO’s ZGIA proposal would establish an interconnection framework for generation resources that serve load interconnecting at the same substation while injecting little or no energy onto the broader transmission system.[fn:7] The proposal includes an associated study process, operational requirements, and market and resource adequacy provisions intended to support co-located generation and load configurations while maintaining reliability.[fn:8] MISO has explained that the framework is intended to improve speed to power for qualifying resources, reduce unnecessary network upgrade requirements where appropriate, and provide clearer responsibilities for interconnection customers and system operators.[fn:9]
Comments 
The Commission is charged with protecting the reliability of the nation’s transmission system, while acting in the best interest of consumers to ensure that rates under its jurisdiction are not “unjust, unreasonable, unduly discriminatory, or preferential.”[fn:10] Protecting the reliability of the transmission system has become an even greater focus for the Commission with the increase in demand from large load interconnection. MISO’s proposed ZGIA framework represents a constructive, and largely well-designed, approach to facilitating the timely interconnection of co-located generation and load while maintaining transmission system reliability and helping address growing demand from large loads. The proposal establishes important guardrails to improve speed-to-power while limiting broader system impacts and avoiding unnecessary infrastructure costs. However, several aspects of the framework require additional clarification to ensure effective implementation and avoid unintended reliability or cost-allocation concerns. In particular, the Commission should require MISO to delay implementation of the ZGIA to allow coordination with related large load and co-location efforts, specifically the forthcoming LARS proposal. The Transmittal does not adequately explain how these efforts will interact, creating uncertainty regarding their implementation and administration. Additionally, MISO should clarify how modeling differences between the ZGIA, the Definitive Planning Phase (“DPP”), and the MISO Transmission Expansion Plan (“MTEP”) will be managed and reconciled over time. While the Transmittal outlines a reconciliation framework intended to align these study models over time, questions remain regarding the operation of that framework and whether it fully addresses the impacts of resources and facilities studied through related processes. Lastly, the Commission should also require further explanation of the framework’s operational coordination requirements, including the extent of MISO’s ongoing visibility into and oversight of real-time coordination between a Generating Facility and its associated Load. Given these outstanding issues and MISO’s ongoing large-load reform efforts, the Commission should approve the proposal but require a later implementation date to allow these questions to be resolved and to ensure smooth coordination between the ZGIA and the LARS process.
Coordination Between ZGIA and LARS
MISO is in the middle of multiple large load efforts, and there is a lack of clarity around how exactly these efforts—namely the ZGIA and LARS processes—will function together; particularly from the standpoint of potentially using the same models or relying on the same capacity for these parallel processes. LARS and ZGIA are designed to be distinct yet complementary. The ZGIA framework applies specifically to generation connecting at the same substation and voltage level as existing or proposed large load.[fn:11] This co-location requirement narrows eligibility, but in exchange reduces potential Transmission System Impacts and the need for costly Network Upgrades. LARS, by contrast, is intended to address the scenarios that fall outside the ZGIA’s narrower co-location requirement; namely, new generation and large loads that are electrically proximate but not co-located at the same substation.[fn:12] MISO’s present proposal clarifies how these two efforts relate to each other in scope but does not fully detail how the tight operational coordination required under ZGIA will be functionally integrated with the LARS process.[fn:13] Given this lack of clarity, the Commission should accept the ZGIA filing but suspend its effectiveness until MISO has filed the full suite of large load efforts in response to the Commission's Show Cause Order, so that implementation of the large load changes can be appropriately coordinated. 
MISO recognizes the risk of uncoordinated movement between interconnection pathways and has built safeguards to address it, but only between DPP and ZGIA, not between ZGIA and LARS. Under the current proposal, a zero-injection large load generator that wishes to serve a different load with its ZG resource may subsequently proceed through the DPP process to obtain full interconnection service unencumbered by the restrictions of ZGIA, since the two interconnection pathways are separate; an Interconnection Customer may submit a separate Interconnection Request to obtain either Network Resource or Energy Resource Interconnection Service (“NRIS / ERIS”) for the same ZG Resource.[fn:14] However, a single project could make multiple, parallel attempts to interconnect through both the ZGIA and LARS pathways. This outcome would not only be administratively duplicative but would risk further congesting an already-strained interconnection queue with projects that are, in practice, incapable of moving forward under either process. Rather than resolving the queue backlog that both LARS and the ZGIA are intended to help address, uncoordinated implementation could compound it. MISO also appropriately limits the overlap between ZGIA and DPP. Specifically, MISO permits an Interconnection Request already in the DPP to withdraw and transfer into the ZGIA process only if it has not yet reached Decision Point II of its DPP cycle, and abides by a one-year waiting period before the transferred project may be reprocessed for ZG Interconnection Service.[fn:15] This safeguard is specifically aimed at deterring late-stage transfers that would disrupt the standing DPP process. MISO offers no comparable mechanism governing movement between LARS and ZGIA, even though the two are designed to serve closely related, and in some cases overlapping, categories of large load and co-located or proximate generation. It is thus unclear whether a single project could make multiple, parallel attempts to interconnect through both the ZGIA and LARS pathways. This outcome would not only be administratively duplicative but would risk further congesting an already-strained interconnection queue with projects that are, in practice, incapable of moving forward under either process. Rather than resolving the queue backlog that both LARS and ZGIA are intended to help address, uncoordinated implementation could compound it.
Absent an analogous mechanism to discipline movement between LARS and ZGIA, a single project could pursue LARS and ZGIA in parallel, or shift from one to the other late in either study process, without triggering any of the safeguards MISO has otherwise found necessary to protect the integrity and predictability of its interconnection queue. To aid the coordination of the ZGIA and LARS, MISO should adopt rules that prevent an Interconnection Customer from pursuing both pathways for the same or related generating facility and load in an uncoordinated or duplicative manner. Such rules could include creating a strict, straightforward exclusivity requirement providing that a given load and generation configuration may only be under active study in either LARS or ZGIA, but not both simultaneously. At a minimum, MISO should develop queue coordination rules that: (i) detail when a project must exit one process and commit to the other, like the safeguards already in place between the ZGIA and DPP; (ii) define a point in the LARS study process, analogous to DPP's Decision Point II, beyond which a project may not exit LARS and pursue a ZGIA (or vice versa) without a comparable withdrawal penalty, and (iii) set clear prioritization rules on how queue priority is determined for a project that transfers between the two processes.
A delayed implementation to coordinate with additional large load efforts is consistent with the Commission’s prior action in granting compliance extensions for PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).[fn:16] PJM was granted additional time to implement co-location-related compliance revisions so that large load efforts could be developed concurrently between the separate, interrelated show cause filings rather than on separate, uncoordinated tracks.[fn:17] The same level of coordination is required here: implementing the ZGIA framework on a timeline separate from LARS risks the same kind of inconsistency and duplicative effort that PJM is avoiding with the approved coordinated timing. Accordingly, United respectfully requests that the Commission approve the ZGIA but order a later implementation date for the ZGIA framework to facilitate enhanced coordination with the closely related LARS process and MISO’s forthcoming Show Cause compliance filing.
Modeling Coordination Between ZGIA, DPP and MTEP
United requests additional clarification regarding how MISO will manage modeling differences between the ZGIA study process, the DPP, and the MTEP process. As described in the Transmittal, the ZGIA model, like the DPP studies, will include “all Existing Generating Facilities”[fn:18]; meaning facilities from completed DPP cycles, the ERAS Interconnection Requests, the latest MTEP projects, and projects approved through the Expedited Project Review process.[fn:19] Unlike DPP studies, however, ZGIA models will not include interconnection requests that remain in the generator interconnection queue without an executed GIA.[fn:20] MISO explains that differences between the study models will be reconciled through the MTEP process;[fn:21] this will look like a multi-step reconciliation process to keep the ZGIA and DPP study models aligned over time. Before each ZGIA cycle, MISO adds to the ZGIA base case any projects that have obtained a GIA since the last base case was developed. Separately, once a DPP cycle concludes, MISO incorporates that cycle’s output, along with any completed ZGIA and EPR projects, into a new MTEP base case. Any transmission issues surfaced through that reconciliation are then addressed through the MTEP process, and the next DPP cycle proceeds using the updated, reconciled base case.[fn:22]
United agrees that this reconciliation process substantially mitigates concerns regarding coordination between the ZGIA and DPP frameworks. By incorporating approved ZGIA projects into the subsequent MTEP base case, MISO creates a mechanism through which later DPP studies should, in principle, account for the electrical impacts of previously approved ZGIA resources. However, two specific gaps in MISO’s explanation remain unclear.
First, while MISO identifies completed ZGIA and EPR projects as flowing into the post-DPP MTEP reconciliation,[fn:23] the Transmittal does not clearly state whether LARS- or ERAS-approved projects are incorporated through the same mechanism. Because ZGIA, LARS, and ERAS are all designed to address speed-to-power and facilitate related large-load and co-located resource configurations, the effectiveness of the reconciliation framework depends on all materially relevant facilities being reflected in subsequent planning and interconnection models, not solely ZGIA and EPR projects.
Second, even where reconciliation applies, the filing does not explain how cost responsibility is determined when a constraint surfaces through MTEP reconciliation rather than through a project-specific DPP system impact study. MTEP is MISO’s annual, forecast-driven regional planning process, built around load and resource forecasts and system-wide scenarios rather than the electrical footprint of any individual interconnection request;[fn:24] it is not clear that reconciliation through MTEP provides the same project-specific analysis, or the same basis for assigning upgrade costs, as a DPP study would. If a future generator interconnection request is located electrically near a previously approved ZGIA, ERAS, or LARS project, the resulting constraint may only be identified, and cost responsibility only assigned, if that later project happens to progress through a full DPP cycle; absent that, the filing does not explain how the constraint is captured or who bears the resulting costs.
The uncertainty surrounding cost allocation could run afoul of the Commission’s foundational cost causation principle by shifting costs beyond the cost causers and beneficiaries. To be just and reasonable and not unduly burdensome, costs must be assigned to those who cause them to be incurred and allocated roughly commensurate with the benefits received.[fn:25] The DPP cluster-based study process directly ties transmission constraints and Network Upgrades, identified through a DPP system impact study, to the specific Interconnection Requests whose electrical impacts necessitated them. It is unclear whether MTEP reconciliation preserves that same causal linkage. Because MTEP is a forecast-driven regional planning process rather than a project-specific interconnection study,[fn:26] a constraint identified through MTEP reconciliation may be treated as a general regional transmission need rather than attributed to the specific ZGIA, ERAS, LARS, or later-queued generator projects whose interconnection caused the constraint. If such upgrade costs are spread across the broader MTEP cost allocation framework rather than to the interconnection customers whose projects are the cost causer,[fn:27] those costs could be shifted to later-queued customers, load, or other stakeholders with no causal relationship to the constraint. Absent clarification of how MISO will preserve cost causation principles when a constraint is identified and addressed through MTEP not through DPP, the reconciliation mechanism risks producing outcomes inconsistent with cost causation.
Accordingly, United requests that MISO clarify: (1) whether LARS and ERAS projects are incorporated into the same base-case reconciliation process as ZGIA and EPR projects; and (2) how cost responsibility for upgrades will be determined when a constraint is identified through MTEP reconciliation rather than through a DPP system impact study. Greater transparency on these two points would help stakeholders evaluate whether the proposed framework can be implemented without creating unintended reliability or cost-shifting concerns.
Operational Coordination Requirements
The foundational principle of the ZGIA framework—that co-located generating facilities will not meaningfully inject onto the broader Transmission System—requires tight, near-real-time coordination between the Generating Facility and its ZG Associated Load. MISO has proposed a thoughtful set of tools to make this coordination technically feasible.[fn:28] However, the ZGIA framework has only been studied assuming the co-located facilities are injecting a de minimus amount.[fn:29] The Transmittal does not fully grapple with the practical difficulty of achieving this outcome in real time, and fails to describe how it will successfully manage these operational requirements in the tight time frame that would be necessary. Nor does MISO explain how it will maintain sufficient operational visibility into ZG Associated Load operations to determine the load’s expected operating profile and dispatch the associated Generating Facility accordingly. The Commission should therefore order MISO to address these unresolved operational questions prior to implementation.
To account for the physical reality that a Generating Facility generally cannot ramp down instantaneously in response to a sudden decrease in load demand, the framework establishes a “Transient Tolerance Window.”[fn:30] This period provides the necessary defined, time-bounded allowance for the physical lag inherent in generator ramping; transient injections occurring within this window do not constitute a violation of the ZGIA’s protection requirements.[fn:31]
As described in the Transmittal, the obligation to monitor the ZG Associated Load’s demand and respond within the Transient Tolerance Window rests on equipment and processes installed and operated by the Generating Facility itself, based on a bilateral relationship between the Generating Facility and its ZG Associated Load.[fn:32] The filing does not explain what visibility, if any, MISO will maintain into this coordination on an ongoing operational basis once a ZGIA is executed, as distinct from the one-time confirmation obtained during the ZG Impact Assessment. United requests that MISO clarify what ongoing operational visibility, if any, it will have into real-time coordination between a Generating Facility and its ZG Associated Load after the ZGIA is executed, and how compliance with the Transient Tolerance Window and associated equipment requirements will be monitored and verified on an ongoing basis, rather than solely through the initial ZG Impact Assessment.
Market Registration and Billing
MISO appropriately proposes to require Interconnection Customers with ZG Interconnection Service greater than 5 MW to register as market resources and to bill ZG Associated Load on a gross basis for transmission service, ancillary services, and other applicable charges.[fn:33] These changes will improve visibility into co-located load and generation, support accurate accounting of gross market volumes, and help ensure that similarly situated loads bear comparable cost responsibility. As MISO explains, some market charges, including ancillary services for load and administrative charges, are billed based on gross MW volumes in the Day-Ahead and Real-Time markets; requiring ZG Resources to register separately allows MISO to capture load and generation volumes independently and bill them appropriately.[fn:34] United agrees that this approach is necessary to ensure that large loads participating in co-location arrangements remain fully subject to the same transmission service and ancillary service obligations as any other Network Load, consistent with the Commission's concerns in the Show Cause Order.[fn:35] Requiring gross billing and market registration, rather than allowing netting between the ZG Resource and its ZG Associated Load, ensures that the ZGIA framework does not become a vehicle for co-located loads to avoid cost responsibility that similarly situated Network Load would otherwise bear. United accordingly supports these provisions as proposed and encourages the Commission to accept them without modification.
Conclusion 
For the reasons stated above, Advanced Energy United respectfully requests that the Commission consider these comments and accept MISO’s proposed ZGIA framework to facilitate speed-to-power for co-located generation and load configurations while maintaining system reliability and minimizing broader negative transmission system impacts, subject to the delayed effective date and clarifications requested herein.
