II. ANSWER
PJM’s IRAS is aimed at addressing resource adequacy concerns that have been
primarily driven by new data center load.12 Data centers are not only driving the
unprecedented load growth in PJM, they also have unique characteristics that present distinct challenges to the grid.13 Despite the clear and specific cause of these problems, PJM’s IRAS proposal—specifically the requirement that New Large Loads face

curtailment if they do not have Bring Your Own New Capacity (“BYONC”)—is broadly
targeted at all large loads over 50 MW. That covers new manufacturing and other
traditional industrial and commercial loads, as well as load increases of existing similar loads that bring total load above 50 MW.

Buckeye agrees with EKPC that this broad application of PJM’s IRAS proposal is
particularly problematic for Self-Supply LSEs, like Buckeye, that have planned for this

A. Buckeye is a Self-Supply LSE.
Buckeye, as a Self-Supply LSE, operates to ensure that it either owns or is entitled to sufficient generation resources to cover the needs of its member-electric distribution cooperatives and their retail members. For over 60 years, Buckeye has planned its generation portfolio to ensure that there are sufficient generation resources to serve not only its existing load, but organic load growth. The organic load growth Buckeye plans for includes increased demand needs at existing loads and the addition of new commercial and industrial facilities that would locate on its member-electric distribution

As a result of this planning, Buckeye currently owns or is entitled to generation output that exceeds its members’ current load requirements, plus reserves, and is sufficient to meet organic load growth for many years into the future. Buckeye’s distribution cooperative members, and in turn their retail members, have paid for this generation at-cost with the expectation that the generation in excess of current load will be available to cover organic load growth for which it was planned. To illustrate why PJM’s proposal is overly broad and not just and reasonable, assume a Buckeye has a retail member manufacturing facility with 45 MW of current load that has been in operation for decades, and that Buckeye has planned its generation portfolio based on this member’s existing load and potential future growth. This member (and others) has paid through its rates for Buckeye’s generation supply at cost for the decades it has been in operation. This member seeks to expand its operations by adding 6 MW of load, and 100 new jobs, increasing its overall load to 51 MW—within the range of future load growth for which Buckeye has planned and obtained generation. Despite Buckeye having excess generation in its portfolio built and paid for by its members for the very purpose of supplying this growth, Buckeye or the load must now go out and build or acquire an additional 6 MW of new generation to serve this growth or be subject to curtailment under PJM’s IRAS proposal. B. Buckeye supports limiting the definition of New Large Load to
data centers.
There is no question that the large imbalance PJM is experiencing between expected load growth and available generation resources is driven primarily by data centers.16 To combat this imbalance, PJM is proposing IRAS as an operational tool to address the reality that data centers are seeking to interconnect to the electric grid more quickly than generation and transmission can be built, making it difficult for PJM to maintain a reliable electric grid.17 But as the Ohio FEA recognized in its filing, PJM’s IRAS filing makes no distinction “between the computational loads that are directly responsible for the current state of resource adequacy—such as hyperscale data centers and cryptocurrency miners—and traditional manufacturing loads that drive American industry.”18

C. In the alternative, Buckeye supports removing existing loads
served by Self-Supply LSEs from the definition of New Large
Loads.
If the Commission does not limit the definition of New Large Loads to data center loads, Buckeye alternatively supports removing from the definition of New Large Load existing loads that are served by Self-Supply LSEs with sufficient generation resources in their portfolios. As noted above, Self-Supply LSEs have planned their generation portfolios to provide generation supply to their existing loads along with organic load growth. These Self-Supply LSEs, and their customers, have paid for this generation portfolio with the expectation that excess generation will be available to cover future growth.

It is not just and reasonable for PJM to now require Self-Supply LSEs, and in turn the loads they serve, to either be subject to curtailment or bear the cost of building new generation capacity for existing loads that expand over 50 MW. Establishing a narrowly tailored exception to the scope of New Large Loads for Self-Supply LSEs with sufficient excess generation available to support expansion of existing loads would avoid this problem. Similar to the discussion above, this modification of PJM’s proposal would be consistent with IRAS’s overall objectives, since it would still require data centers that are causing the resource adequacy problems identified by PJM to be subject to either BYONC or curtailment.

D. At a minimum, the Commission should adopt PJM ICC’s
proposal to apply New Large Loads only to existing loads than
increase by 50 MW or more.
The Commission should, at a minimum, adopt the PJM ICC’s proposal that the definition of New Large Load apply only to existing loads that increase by an increment of 50 MW, rather than existing loads that increase to over 50 MW. As proposed by PJM ICC: “When an existing Large Load facility expands, only load growth over 50 MW from the baseline should be subject to IRAS. (That is, a 50 MW facility should not be subjected to IRAS until it expands beyond 100 MW.)”24

This clarification would avoid the absurd result described above where an existing manufacturing load can increase its load by 6 MW (or less) and be subject to curtailment or be required to pay to bring 6 MW of new generation to the market. In addition, requiring BYONC for small, incremental load increases may be infeasible in many instances, given the lumpy nature of new capacity. This change would not undermine the purpose of IRAS and the resource adequacy concerns it is intended to address. As noted by PJM ICC, “[m]any of the new computational loads are significantly in excess of 50 MW, whereas 50 MW is not unusual for an industrial or institutional facility.”25

III. CONCLUSION
To the extent PJM does not address the issues described above on its own, Buckeye requests the Commission direct PJM to (i) revise the definition of New Large Loads to apply only to data centers, or, in the alternative; (ii) limit the definition of New Large Loads only to loads that are truly new, and exclude incremental growth of existing
