II. BACKGROUND
1
Q.
What is the background for this proceeding?
2
A. [Blazunas, Schafer, and Hammer] On December 18, 2025, the Commission issued its 3

order in PJM Interconnection. L.L.C., 193 FERC ¶ 61,217, at P 193 (2025) (“Order”). In 4

the Order, the Commission found that PJM’s Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) 5

“is unjust and unreasonable because it does not contain provisions addressing with 6

sufficient clarity or consistency the rates, terms, and conditions of service that apply to 7

generators serving Co-Located Load and Eligible Customers taking transmission service 8

on behalf of Co-Located Load.”1 Specifically, the Commission found PJM’s Tariff “unjust 9

and unreasonable because it does not include types of transmission service that reflect 10

Eligible Customers taking service on behalf of Co-Located Loads that are willing and able 11

to limit their use of the transmission system under certain conditions.”2 12

The Commission ordered PJM to offer three new forms of transmission service 13

under its Tariff, and identified these three new forms of transmission service as: (i) Firm 14

Contract Demand transmission service; (ii) Non-Firm Contract Demand transmission 15

service; and (iii) interim, non-firm transmission service. The Commission also established 16

a paper hearing to determine the just and reasonable rates, terms, and conditions for these 17

new transmission services. The paper hearing process provides for initial briefs, responses 18

to initial briefs, and replies to those responses.

IV. NEW TRANSMISSION SERVICE CATEGORIES AND RATE DESIGNS
1
A. Firm Contract Demand Transmission Service
2
Q.
What is Firm Contract Demand transmission service?
3
A. [Blazunas] Pursuant to the Order, under the new Firm Contract Demand transmission 4

service (“Firm Contract Demand Service”), Eligible Customers will have the right to 5

request transmission service on behalf of a Co-Located Load up to a specified megawatt 6

(“MW”) quantity (“Firm Contract Demand”). The Firm Contract Demand Service will 7

have the same reservation and curtailment priority as existing firm transmission services at 8

the level of the contract demand. However, unlike Network Integration Transmission 9

Service (“NITS”) customers, the Eligible Customer taking Firm Contract Demand Service 10

on behalf of the Co-Located Load is not permitted to withdraw energy from the PJM 11

transmission system beyond the firm contract demand level under this service. 12

Q.
What is the PJM Transmission Owners’ proposed rate design for the new Firm 13

Contract Demand Service?
14

Demand Service be based on zonal NITS rates and the underlying NITS revenue 16

requirements. Under this approach, Eligible Customers taking Firm Contract Demand 17

Service will pay the same base charge per MW as NITS customers, except that, unlike 18

NITS customers, their applicable billing determinant will be their specified firm contract 19

demand. The transmission charge for a Firm Contract Demand Service customer will be 20

calculated by multiplying: (i) the customer’s firm contract demand amount (in MWs) by 21

(ii) the applicable zonal NITS rate.

Q.
Why are the PJM Transmission Owners proposing to base the rate for Firm Contract 1

Demand Service on the NITS rate, as opposed to the firm Point-to-Point rate or some 2

other rate?
3
A. [Blazunas] The PJM Transmission Owners are proposing to charge the same rate for Firm 4

Contract Demand Service and NITS because the level of service provided to both 5

customers is, for all relevant purposes, the same. Both NITS and Firm Contract Demand 6

Service are firm services for which Network Upgrades will be planned and constructed as 7

necessary, with the only difference being that upgrades for NITS customers are identified 8

based on anticipated load growth while upgrades for Firm Contract Demand Service 9

customers will be identified based on their contracted demand level. Firm Contract 10

Demand Service customers will be able to serve their contracted demand through capacity 11

procured by PJM just like NITS customers and will not be required to designate specific 12

points of receipt and delivery.
13
There is no need or benefit to utilizing existing Firm Point-to-Point Service 14

(“PTPS”) rates. Firm PTPS rates are generally based on the same revenue requirement as 15

the NITS rate, and the nature of Firm Contract Demand Service as contemplated in the 16

Order is essentially a variation of NITS service with a specified contract demand level. The 17

Firm Contract Demand Service customer will be serving its full contract demand from all 18

resources on the grid, not on a point-to-point basis. Consequently, PJM will treat Firm 19

Contract Demand Service load during system emergencies in the same manner as load 20

using NITS, thus providing service to the Firm Contract Demand Service load on a 21

comparable basis with NITS load. Furthermore, Firm Contract Demand transmission 22

Service will enjoy the same firmness as NITS load, as well as the same ability to make use 23

PDF page 8

Exhibit No. PTO-0001
Page 8 of 39
of PJM resources to serve their full contract demand and ensure guaranteed grid energy 1

delivery. Given the similarities between NITS and Firm Contract Demand Service, it would 2

be inappropriate to charge Firm Contract Demand Service customers a rate lower than one 3

based on the NITS rate because doing so would result in subsidizing these customers at the 4

expense of other ratepayers.
5
Finally, in PJM, PTPS is generally only utilized for purposes of service at the PJM 6

border. That will not be the case for Co-Located Load customers taking Firm Contract 7

Demand Service to serve their on-site, contract demand within PJM. Basing the rate for 8

Firm Contract Demand Service on the zonal NITS rate also has the advantage of 9

minimizing changes to current PJM billing and settlements practices. As it does for NITS 10

now, for Firm Contract Demand Service, PJM will perform the billing function for 11

transmission, capacity, and energy charges, while electric distribution companies will 12

perform their current roles of submitting the metrics that PJM uses to calculate those 13

charges, such as customers’ peak load share information.

minimize or eliminate the need for the PJM Transmission Owners to modify their formula 16

A. [Schafer] As contemplated under the Order, the PJM Transmission Owners are proposing 7

to include a Grid Reliance Charge as part of the charges associated with the new Co- 8

Located Load terms and conditions under the PJM Tariff. The intent of the Grid Reliance 9

Charge is to reflect the inherent benefits that Firm Contract Demand Service and Non-Firm 10

Contract Demand Service customers receive as a result of their reliance upon the grid. It is 11

designed to recover the costs of delivering ancillary services, including the delivery of 12

black start and regulation, and providing synchronization and grid reliance support, thereby 13

ensuring Co‑Located Loads pay their appropriate share of the transmission system that they 14

rely on regardless of whether they withdraw energy from the grid. The Grid Reliance 15

Charge also recognizes the benefits associated with monitoring and other activities that 16

transmission owners will need to perform associated with the increased number of 17

protective systems that will be required for Co-Located Load customers.
18
As the Commission recognized, Co-Located Loads are electrically connected to the 19

transmission system (not islanded) and therefore benefit from the system and should 20

contribute to the ongoing operation and maintenance of the transmission system, consistent 21

with foundational cost causation principles. Commissioner Chang’s concurrence to the 22

Order stressed the need for a minimum charge for the two new contract demand services. 23

The Commissioner noted that a customer selecting a de minimis contract demand could 1

pay almost nothing despite its continuous reliance on the grid.
2
Even with a zero MW contract demand, a large Co‑Located Load still depends on 3

the grid for the delivery of various ancillary services and a suite of synchronization support 4

services, such as a stable frequency, inertia, stability services (damping, angle stability), 5

fault current capability, and ride-through and protection coordination. For example, adding 6

a 1,000 MW load to the transmission system inherently imposes electrical impacts at the 7

point of interconnection and beyond. In summary, zero net withdrawals are not indicative 8

of a load’s actual reliance on the grid. And, of course, a large Co-Located Load, such as a 9

data center, would not be interested in locating in PJM if the system did not also support 10

an extensive network of customers and communications infrastructure.

Q.
Do the PJM Transmission Owners recommend that the Grid Reliance Charge be 5

updated and if so, how frequently?
6
A. [Schafer] Yes. The PJM Transmission Owners propose that the resulting average Grid 7

Reliance Charge percentage be updated approximately every five years. A five-year term 8

is reasonable for several reasons. Transmission depreciation rates and O&M structures 9

change gradually over time; they do not generally fluctuate greatly from year to year. A 10

multi-year period therefore avoids unnecessary annual recalculations while maintaining 11

alignment with underlying cost structures. In addition, leaving the percentage in place for 12

five years promotes rate stability, reduces administrative burden for both utilities and 13

regulators, and provides customers with predictable charges.
14
Q.
Why is the PJM Transmission Owners proposed Grid Reliance Charge methodology 15

just and reasonable?
16
[Schafer] The proposed Grid Reliance Charge methodology is just and reasonable for a 17

number of reasons. First, the Grid Reliance Charge is intentionally limited to depreciation 18

and O&M. Depreciation reflects the annualized consumption of the transmission 19

infrastructure that enables customers to rely on the grid, while O&M reflects the ongoing 20

expense required to keep that infrastructure functional. By excluding return on rate base 21

and associated income taxes, the methodology produces a conservative result focused on 22

cost recovery rather than investment return. This provides transparency, administrative 23

PDF page 25

Exhibit No. PTO-0001
Page 25 of 39
simplicity, and stability. Second, it relies on publicly reported and audited accounting data 1

from those PJM Transmission Owners with formula rates that have reported O&M and 2

depreciation balances associated with assets in service.  This covers the vast majority 3

(approximately 95 percent) of transmission facilities in PJM. Third, it avoids re-litigating 4

or recalculating each utility’s rate base, return, or tax components. Fourth, it eliminates the 5

need for company-specific engineering allocations. Fifth, by expressing the Grid Reliance 6

Charge as a percentage of the total transmission revenue requirement, it scales 7

proportionally with each utility’s size and cost structure. Finally, the Grid Reliance Charge- 8

percentage averaging component further promotes administrative efficiency and rate 9

stability. Individual transmission owners may exhibit modest year-to-year variations in 10

account structure or expense classification. Averaging the calculated Grid Reliance Charge 11

percentages across all PJM Transmission Owners smooths those variations, reduces 12

volatility, and produces a reasonable proxy for the portion of transmission costs attributable 13

to grid reliance on a regional basis.

Transmission Planning (“TPL”) standards establish mandatory planning requirements to 5

ensure the Bulk Electric System can reliably withstand credible contingencies over 6

planning horizons. TPL-001 Transmission System Planning Performance Requirements is 7

the foundational and currently enforceable transmission planning standard. Its purpose is 8

to ensure that the Bulk Electric System is planned so it will operate reliably across a broad 9

range of system conditions and contingencies, from normal conditions to extreme events. 10

Compliance with the NERC TPL-001 requirements is important because they
11
ensure the transmission system is planned to reliably withstand credible outages and system 12

stresses, reducing the risk of cascading failures and large-scale blackouts. For transmission 13

owners and providers, TPL-001 compliance also minimizes regulatory and financial risk, 14

supports coordinated regional planning, and provides a defensible technical basis for 15

transmission investments needed to maintain long-term grid reliability.

Q.
Does the Order’s proposal to rely on SPS/RAS to accommodate Co-Located Load 17

transmission service requests present any reliability or planning concerns? 18

A. [Hammer] Yes. Under the principle of TPL-001, transmission planners must plan for load 19

that has the ability to draw power from the transmission system, not merely for load that is 20

expected or intended to do so under normal operating conditions. Reliance on SPS/RAS as 21

a substitute for timely network upgrades raises significant concerns under established good 22

utility practices and applicable NERC reliability standards. SPS/RAS are generally 23

Q.
Please explain the above-mentioned “full redundancy of SPS/RAS” in more detail. 13

A. [Hammer] In this context, “full redundancy of SPS/RAS” refers to a layered protection 14

framework under which reliability risks associated with Co‑Located Load taking the new 15

contract demand transmission services are addressed through both primary, 16

customer‑owned protection and an optional, transmission‑owner‑owned backstop, rather 17

than reliance on a single protection scheme.
18
Under this framework, the primary responsibility for limiting withdrawals from the 19

transmission system remains with the interconnection customer or Co‑Located Load, 20

typically through customer-owned SPS/RAS that are designed, operated, and maintained 21

in accordance with applicable NERC standards and reviewed by PJM. To help accomplish 22

this, the Commission should direct NERC to establish requirements that Co-Located Load 23

Exhibit No. PTO-0001
Page 37 of 39
Customers register with NERC so that those customers may be responsible for meeting 1

NERC’s reliability standards. These customer‑owned schemes serve as the first line of 2

defense and are expected to operate under normal and reasonably foreseeable contingency 3

conditions.
4
“Full redundancy” does not mean duplicative or parallel protection for every 5

contingency, nor does it imply that transmission owners must install SPS/RAS in all cases. 6

Rather, it means that, where system studies identify elevated reliability risk, transmission 7

owners should have the option to implement a transmission-owner-owned SPS/RAS as a 8

secondary backstop to protect the Bulk Electric System in the event of customer‑owned 9

scheme failure, mis-operation, or unavailability.
10
Q.
How does this concept of redundancy align with NERC reliability standards, 11

including PRC-012?
12
A. [Hammer] This approach is fully consistent with NERC Reliability Standard PRC‑012, 13

which expressly contemplates that SPS/RAS may be owned by Transmission Owners, 14

Generator Owners, or Distribution Providers, provided that ownership and accountability 15

are clearly defined. Importantly, PRC‑012 does not prohibit multiple SPS/RAS acting on 16

the same system condition, nor does it require exclusive reliance on a single owner’s 17

protection scheme. Allowing a Transmission Owner to own and operate a backstop 18

SPS/RAS therefore fits within the PRC‑012 framework.
19
Q.
Why is reliance on a single, customer‑owned SPS/RAS insufficient in some cases? 20

A. [Hammer] Transmission planning and operating standards, including TPL‑001, require 21

planners to account for credible contingencies and protection system failures, including 22

single points of failure and mis-operations. In configurations where Co‑Located Load 23

depends on SPS/RAS to limit transmission system withdrawals, exclusive reliance on a 1

single, customer‑controlled protection scheme can introduce reliability risk, particularly if 2

the equipment is unavailable due to outage or maintenance. In high‑impact scenarios, 3

failure of the primary SPS/RAS could result in unexpected load appearing on the 4

transmission system, potentially leading to overloads, stability concerns, or emergency 5

10
A. [Hammer] No. The concept of full redundancy does not shift responsibility away from 11

customers. The presence of a transmission-owner‑owned backstop does not relieve 12

customers of compliance obligations, nor does it entitle customers to rely on transmission- 13

owner‑owned protection in lieu of their own facilities.

Q.
Why is the ability to selectively curtail load essential to the contract demand 15

transmission service framework adopted by the Commission, and how is that 16

capability implemented in practice at the point of interconnection?
17
A. [Hammer] The Commission directed PJM to create contract demand transmission service 18

to reflect loads that are willing and able to limit their withdrawals from the transmission 19

system. Load taking contract demand transmission service is granted access to the 20

transmission system without corresponding reliability reinforcements to support the full 21

gross load, therefore, the reliability condition is curtailment.

Under certain system conditions, PJM and the PJM Transmission Owners have the 1

authority to shed the non‑firm portion of load, either preemptively or immediately 2

following a contingency. For example, PJM may arm a SPS/RAS to instantaneously drop 3

a large block of load upon the trip of the on-site generator, thereby preventing a thermal or 4

stability overload.
5
Where a point of interconnection serves both firm and non‑firm contract demand, 6

the interconnection must be designed to allow selective curtailment. Operationally, this 7

typically requires that firm and non‑firm load be served through separate breakers, feeders, 8

or other independently controllable facilities. The Transmission Owner may structure the 9

interconnection so that the non‑firm portion can be shed without interrupting firm service. 10

Examples could include:
11

Separate feeder lines or breakers dedicated to firm versus non‑firm load; 12


Partial load‑shedding schemes that trip only designated non‑firm MW; or
13

For large facilities such as data centers, multiple transformer banks, with some 14

designated as “firm service” and others as “non‑firm service,” allowing independent 15

tripping.
16
Critically, the ability to distinguish and shed the non‑firm portion of load 17

independently is a prerequisite for a contract demand service model. Curtailment cannot 18

be all‑or‑nothing; the firm portion of load is expected to remain in service except under 19

last‑resort system conditions. As a result, compliance may require the installation of special 20

protection or control equipment at the substation or within the customer’s facilities. 21
