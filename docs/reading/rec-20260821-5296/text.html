COMMENTS OF OHIO ENERGY LEADERSHIP COUNCIL 
 
 
Pursuant to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (the “Commission”) Rules of 
Practice and Procedure, Ohio Energy Leadership Council (“OELC”) respectfully submits these 
comments on the Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) proposed in this proceeding by PJM 
Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”). 
I. 
SUMMARY AND REQUESTED COMMISSION ACTION 
OELC supports the objective of maintaining resource adequacy and recognizes the need 
for timely, durable action where new load growth is genuinely causing incremental capacity needs. 
But the Commission should not permit the RBP or any associated definition of “Large Load” to 
turn existing, energy-intensive Ohio manufacturers into the financial backstop for load growth they 
did not cause. Accordingly, OELC respectfully requests that the Commission condition any 
acceptance of PJM’s RBP proposal on the following: 
1. 
Establish a separate, protective threshold for non-data-center customers. A non-data-
center customer—including traditional manufacturing, steelmaking, chemical, industrial, 
educational, institutional, information-technology, and retail customers—should be treated 
as a Large Load only if its Peak Load Contribution (“PLC”) is at least 150 MW. The lower 
50 MW threshold should not apply to non-data-center customers. 
2. 
Use a transparent, system-relevant measurement. For both threshold eligibility and any 
allocation that rests on load magnitude, PJM must measure PLC using the customer’s load 
during the established PJM system-peak hours used to develop capacity obligations—not 
the customer’s highest demand at any time during the measurement year.

3. 
Specify the implementation details in tariff language. PJM should identify the 
applicable peak-hour set, the data source and metering boundary, treatment of behind-the-
meter generation and demand response, the measurement period, notice and verification 
procedures, and a customer-level dispute process before any charge is imposed. 
4. Ensure New Generation is Incentivized. The Commission should modify PJM’s 
proposed resource-selection methodology to materially value new dispatchable 
generation’s reliability attributes. 
These protections are necessary for the proposal to satisfy the Federal Power Act’s just-
and-reasonable standard. Section 205 requires that all Commission-jurisdictional rates, charges, 
practices, and classifications be just and reasonable. PJM bears the burden to demonstrate that each 
proposed RBP practice—including its resource-eligibility, accreditation, offer-quantity, selection, 
commitment, and cost-allocation rules—meets that standard. 
II. 
OELC’S INTEREST AND EXPERIENCE 
 
OELC is a not-for-profit trade association that represents its members’ interests in utility 
and ratemaking matters in Ohio. Managing the cost and availability of energy is a crucial 
component of success for many businesses, and OELC advocates for competitive, reasonable, and 
transparent laws, policies, and utility rates through effective lobbying and legal advocacy. OELC’s 
work is directed to ensuring that Ohio remains an attractive place for businesses and enterprises to 
remain, grow, invest, and create jobs. 
For more than 30 years, OELC has helped its members understand and manage their 
electric and natural gas supply costs. OELC and its members have direct, long-standing experience 
with the practical effects of utility tariffs, rate design, energy legislation, and regulatory policy on 
Ohio businesses that must manage significant energy consumption and exposure to volatile energy 
costs. OELC’s members include energy-intensive manufacturing, industrial, educational, 
institutional, information-technology, and retail business customers in Ohio. Collectively, OELC’s 
members annually consume many billions of kilowatt-hours of electricity and are major

Commission should not adopt a rate design that creates those consequences without a clear and 
demonstrated causal connection to the RBP need.

III. 
A 50 MW THRESHOLD FOR NON-DATA-CENTER CUSTOMERS WOULD BE 
ARBITRARY, OVERINCLUSIVE, UNJUST AND UNREASONABLE. 
A 50 MW threshold may be an administratively convenient number, but it is not a 
demonstrated measure of responsibility for the extraordinary new-load conditions at issue. It would 
draw into the Large Load category an extensive range of established industrial facilities whose 
operational demand reflects manufacturing processes, long-standing production footprints, and 
employment-intensive operations—not a new, concentrated demand shock. A threshold must do 
more than identify customers that use a meaningful amount of electricity. It must identify 
customers whose incremental system-peak demand creates the need for the specific capacity 
procurement and associated charges.  
A 50 MW non-data-center threshold fails that test for a number of reasons. Energy-
intensive manufacturers use substantial electricity because they produce essential goods. Their 
demand is often steady, process-driven, and embedded in the long-established load profile on 
which PJM’s system planning, capacity obligations, and tariffs have been built. The fact that a 
steel mill, chemical facility, or other manufacturer exceeds 50 MW at times says nothing about 
whether it is a new load addition, whether it contributed to the relevant load adjustment, or whether 
it caused an incremental reliability need.  
By contrast, the RBP’s stated purpose is to address a shortfall associated with rapid 
additions of load. The Commission should not permit a proxy that converts the mere size of an 
established customer into proof of causation. That would make the charge overinclusive, sever the 
link between cost and responsibility, and expose existing manufacturers to a substantial new cost 
obligation based on a trait—electricity intensity—that is central to their productive activity and 
unrelated to the new-load phenomenon the RBP is meant to address.

Traditional industrial facilities—particularly steel and chemical producers—are 
foundational to domestic supply chains. They supply inputs used in infrastructure, transportation, 
construction, energy systems, agriculture, health care, and national-defense-related production. A 
durable national-security and national economic strategy requires that these industries have access 
to reliable and reasonably priced power. Reliability and affordability are complementary 
objectives, not competing ones. The Commission need not choose between supporting data-center 
growth and supporting domestic industry. It can do both by applying a definition that targets the 
new load that creates the incremental requirement while maintaining reliable service to the 
traditional industrial base.  
Penalizing existing manufacturers for energy intensity simply weakens the very industrial 
capacity on which the nation depends, while doing little to improve the signal sent to the new load 
that drives the need for additional capacity. Energy prices are already a decisive component of 
manufacturing competitiveness. Capacity charges and other demand-related costs are not absorbed 
in a vacuum. They increase the delivered energy cost of production, reduce operating margins, 
constrain capital spending, and affect decisions about production volumes, facility expansions, and 
employment. OELC’s members compete not only with other domestic facilities but with 
international producers that may operate under materially different energy, regulatory, labor, and 
capital-cost conditions.  
The Commission should not introduce a new cost burden on domestic manufacturing at a 
time when the RBP record itself demonstrates capacity-market scarcity: PJM reports that the 
2028/2029 Base Residual Auction cleared 6,831.3 MW UCAP short of the regional reliability 
requirement at the $325/MW-day market price cap.1 The very conditions that cause a backstop

procurement to be expensive make it all the more important to allocate its costs narrowly and

The likely effect of an overbroad 50 MW threshold is not confined to the affected bill. 
Energy-intensive manufacturers transmit higher production costs through supply chains, or they 
absorb them by reducing investment, output, or employment. Neither outcome advances reliability, 
affordability, or economic resilience. In the current inflation-sensitive environment, the 
Commission should avoid a tariff design that can add avoidable cost pressure to domestically 
produced materials and the goods made from them. 
PJM’s own filing provides the critical factual premise for a targeted, rather than 
indiscriminate, response. PJM states that its forecast predicts 32 GW of peak-load growth from 
2024 through 2030 and that 30 GW of that growth is projected to come from data centers.2 The 
filing also explains that the RBP is intended to address resource-adequacy challenges “primarily 
driven by the unprecedented addition of large loads, most notably data centers, in the PJM 
Region.”3 
The RBP record further shows that the projects PJM identified as prospective new large 
loads bear are distinct from historic energy-intensive manufacturers. PJM’s April 2026 request for 
information drew responses concerning 47 potential projects averaging 850 MW, with a median 
size of 300 MW.4 According to PJM the “RFI revealed substantial large load interest from 21 
entities representing 47 potential projects in the PJM Region averaging 850 MW each, with a 
median size of 300 MW.”5 That evidence supports a threshold that distinguishes exceptional new 
demand from the established businesses that are already integral to Ohio’s industrial economy.

The record points to data-center-driven load growth; it does not establish that existing non-
data-center customers with 50–149 MW of system-peak PLC are causing the resource-adequacy 
shortfall. Indeed, PJM acknowledges that its forthcoming filing, rather than the RBP filing itself, 
will propose a regional Large Load definition. The proper policy response is to align costs with 
new data-center load and its procurement choices—not to shift the burden to legacy industrial 
customers that did not make those commitments because they did not cause the underlying 
capacity shortfall. The Commission should require PJM to develop that definition on evidence, 
with an explicit distinction between new data centers and non-data-center customers. A 150 MW 
system-peak PLC threshold for non-data-center customers is a conservative and rational boundary: 
it is only one-half of the median project size PJM identifies in the record, yet it avoids recasting 
substantial numbers of traditional facilities as the type of extraordinary load the RBP was designed 
to address.  
IV. 
The Commission Should Require PJM To Use PLC Values Not a Customer’s Highest 
Demand at Any Time in the Measurement Year. 
The definition of Large Load will be no more rational than its measurement method. OELC 
strongly recommends that PJM measure load using a customer’s PLC values established during 
PJM system peaks, not the customer’s highest demand during any hour in the measurement year. 
Capacity procurement exists to ensure that sufficient resources are available when the PJM 
system faces its highest coincident peak demand. The relevant question, therefore, is not whether 
a particular customer ever reached a high demand level in an isolated hour. It is whether the 
customer materially contributes to the regional peak conditions that establish the system’s capacity 
need. A customer-specific annual maximum can occur in a season, hour, or operating condition 
that does not coincide with the PJM system peak. It may reflect a one-time production event, an 
outage recovery, a weather-related operational adjustment, commissioning activity, or another

PDF page 8

8 
 
idiosyncratic event with no meaningful relationship to the reliability condition the RBP addresses. 
Using that noncoincident maximum would overstate the customer’s capacity responsibility and 
produce classifications untethered from the need for incremental capacity. 
By contrast, system-peak PLC is a familiar and objective planning construct used by PJM 
for many years. It measures demand when capacity is most needed by the region. PJM itself relies 
on load adjustments and capacity-obligation concepts that are intended to assign changes in 
forecasted load to the zone or area where they occur. The Large Load definition should employ 
the same fundamental principle: identify load by its contribution to the relevant system condition, 
rather than by an isolated and potentially unrepresentative maximum peak demand. 
If a customer can be classified as a Large Load based on its highest demand at any time in 
a year, a single non-coincident operating hour could trigger a long-lived tariff consequence. That 
approach would be unpredictable, hard to audit, and unfair to customers with variable 
manufacturing processes. It could also perversely encourage customers to alter operations during 
individual high-demand hours without reducing their contribution to system peaks, thereby 
producing no genuine reliability benefit. 
The Commission should thus reject a methodology that confuses a facility’s maximum 
operating demand with its system-relevant contribution to capacity requirements. A capacity-
related classification must be based on capacity-related data. Accordingly, the Commission should 
direct PJM to file tariff language or an implementation manual, subject to Commission review, 
that resolves at minimum the following issues: 
1. 
Use of PLC Values. Identify the precise PJM peak-hour set and the period used to calculate 
the customer’s peak load for purposes of defining a “Large Load Customer,” including the 
process for publishing those hours and correcting data errors. OELC recommends that PJM 
be directed to use the existing PLC methodology; an annual maximum, an unannounced 
ad hoc interval, or a noncoincident peak should not be used.

9 
 
2. 
Net-load treatment. Specify the treatment of behind-the-meter generation, demand 
response, energy storage, and other load-management arrangements, including the data 
necessary to verify net system contribution. 
3. 
Customer notice and verification. Provide customers with advance notice of proposed 
classification, their underlying interval data and calculated PLC, a reasonable time to 
correct errors, and a transparent mechanism for challenging a classification before charges 
take effect. 
4. 
Temporal nexus to new load. For purposes of the RBP, require PJM and the relevant 
electric distributor to demonstrate that the customer’s qualifying PLC reflects new or 
incremental load relative to the forecast or baseline used to establish the relevant RBP 
target. Existing load should not be reclassified solely because it is large. 
These elements are indispensable. Without them, customers cannot forecast their exposure, 
evaluate the accuracy of their bills, or meaningfully participate in any state retail-cost-allocation 
proceeding. Ambiguity in the definition and measurement of load invites inconsistent results, 
disputes, and cost shifting—the opposite of the transparent and reasonable tariff administration 
OELC seeks. 
V. 
PJM’S RBP RESOURCE-SELECTION METHODOLOGY IS NOT JUST AND 
REASONABLE BECAUSE IT DOES NOT ADEQUATELY INCENTIVIZE NEW 
DISPATCHABLE GENERATION. 
In addition, the Commission should reject or condition the RBP’s resource-selection 
provisions. The question is not simply whether PJM can procure a numerical quantity of UCAP at 
the lowest levelized price. The question under Federal Power Act section 205 is whether the 
proposed procurement practice is just and reasonable in light of the specific resource-adequacy 
problem PJM says it is trying to solve. On this record, the answer is no. 
PJM seeks a long-term reliability backstop because the 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction 
(BRA) cleared materially short of the regional reliability requirement. A just-and-reasonable 
remedy must be designed to obtain the resources most capable of serving load during the 
reliability-risk conditions for which the backstop is being created. PJM’s proposal does not do so. 
It treats capacity as substantially homogeneous for selection purposes, although resources differ in

10 
 
their ability to start, dispatch, sustain output, perform in stress conditions, operate through 
extended events, and respond to changing reliability needs during a commitment that can extend 
through the 2042/2043 Delivery Year. 
Not all resources accredited in UCAP terms are equally useful for meeting a reliability 
shortfall. A resource capable of dispatchable, sustained generation during system stress provides 
a different reliability service from a resource whose capacity depends on load curtailment, 
customer participation, a limited operating duration, or changing conditions outside PJM’s direct 
operational control. That distinction is particularly important for a one-time, 15-year procurement 
intended to provide a durable reliability backstop. The Commission should direct PJM to 
separately evaluate and reflect in the methodology the dispatchability, firm availability, duration, 
fuel assurance, operational control, and demonstrated performance during the conditions that drive 
the RBP need. 
OELC understands that the 2028/2029 BRA Effective Load Carrying Capability (ELCC) 
values used in the proposal assign Demand Response an ELCC value of 91%, while assigning Gas 
Combined Cycle an ELCC value of 78%.6 If PJM uses those values as a dispositive measure of 
the relative capacity contribution of these resources for RBP selection, it sends the wrong 
investment signal. It can advantage demand response over new dispatchable combined-cycle 
generation even though the latter adds new, controllable supply capable of sustained operation. 
PJM has not shown why an accreditation comparison developed for the 2028/2029 BRA should 
govern a procurement expressly designed to address a longer-term reliability problem. 
The problem is not that demand response has no role in reliability. Properly contracted, 
measurable, and dispatchable demand response can provide tremendous value, and for many years

VI. 
CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS. 
OELC does not oppose the development of new resources to meet incremental demand. To 
the contrary, a reliable electric system is essential to OELC’s members. OELC’s concern is that 
the cost of that reliability must be assigned in a manner consistent with the actual source of the 
need. PJM itself represents that its proposal respects the distinction between wholesale and retail 
authority and that states will determine how costs assigned to a load serving entity are collected 
from retail customers. PJM further states that each state may use its own definitions of data centers 
and large loads when allocating RBP charges at retail.  
The Commission should ensure that PJM’s federal tariff framework facilitates, rather than 
frustrates, a properly targeted Ohio retail allocation. A clear 150 MW system-peak PLC guardrail 
for non-data-center customers will provide a principled and administrable floor of protection for 
existing manufacturers. It does not prevent Ohio or another state from adopting additional 
safeguards for its retail customers. Nor does it impair PJM’s ability to procure needed capacity 
from resources. It simply prevents the definition from capturing a broad group of established 
industrial customers based on a low, non-causal threshold. 
PJM’s record also makes clear that it expects states to protect existing customers when 
assigning RBP costs: the filing states that the appropriate retail customers are new large loads, 
including data centers, “and not residential customers or small industrial customers.” OELC agrees 
with the principle but urges the Commission not to treat it as limited to only “small” industrial

12 
 
customers. Traditional manufacturers do not become a cause of load growth merely because they 
are energy intensive. The requested 150 MW threshold ensures that the protection is meaningful 
for the existing industrial base. 
The Commission can advance resource adequacy without sacrificing affordability, 
domestic competitiveness, or the industrial base that remains indispensable to Ohio and the United 
States. Accordingly, OELC respectfully requests that the Commission condition its action to 
require: (1) a 150 MW system-peak PLC threshold for non-data-center customers; (2) PLC 
measurement based on established PJM system peaks, rather than any customer-specific peak 
during the measurement year, and a clear and transparent process for categorizing and notifying 
customers of those values; (3) a resource-selection methodology that materially values new 
dispatchable generation’s reliability attributes and, thus, encourages their development.
