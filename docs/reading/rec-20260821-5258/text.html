POST-TECHNICAL CONFERENCE COMMENTS
OF CAPITAL POWER CORPORATION

Pursuant to the Commission’s July 30, 2026 Notice Requesting Post-Conference Comment, Capital Power Corporation (“Capital Power” or the “Company”) supplements its previously submitted comments and submits these post-technical comments, along with the Notice’s prescribed list, to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission” or “FERC”) in the above-referenced docket.  Capital Power appreciates the opportunity to provide further commentary and commends the Commission for focusing this proceeding on actionable reforms to improve transparency, efficiency, and accountability in PJM Interconnection LLC’s (“PJM”) governance.

I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Capital Power is an independent power producer that entered the PJM market last year with its acquisition of Hummel Station and Rolling Hills, owning and operating approximately 2.2 GW of generation in PJM. At this point in the proceedings, the challenges and frustrations are well- documented, and the Commission was clear: reforms are necessary, and they are happening one way or another.1 The record reflects broad concern that PJM must be able to respond more

In that vein, Capital Power supports targeted, actionable reforms to PJM’s governance and stakeholder processes.

II. COMMENTS
A. The Commission Should Pursue Targeted Reforms, Not Wholesale Redesign of
PJM’s Governance Framework.

Capital Power agrees that PJM’s governance processes must be capable of producing timely, durable decisions. The July 23 technical conference reflected significant concern about whether PJM’s current processes can respond quickly enough to resource adequacy needs, load growth, transmission development, and market-design challenges. But speed alone should not be the measure of success. For investors in generation and related infrastructure, the critical question is whether PJM’s processes produce decisions that are transparent, durable, legally sustainable, and grounded in a full record. The Commission should therefore distinguish between reforms that improve process discipline and reforms that would reallocate substantive rights in ways that could

1. Stakeholder Process: Transparency, Accountability, and Certainty
Capital Power does not believe the Commission should begin from the premise that PJM’s sector-weighted voting structure is the principal cause of delay. Further, the Company does not support converting the stakeholder process into a purely advisory process. Indeed, it is paramount to preserve the benefits of stakeholder engagement and regional collaboration that have long characterized PJM governance. Destabilizing the stakeholder framework at this stage will only cause further delay and create more confusion in the already confusing process described later in this section. While independent power producers do not require guaranteed outcomes, they do need visible timelines, decision pathways, initiative ownership, and escalation rights, because the absence of procedural certainty increases regulatory risk, delays investment decisions, and undermines confidence in PJM’s stakeholder process.

The more practical concern for newer and growing market participants like the Company is that PJM’s stakeholder process can be difficult to navigate. Significant effort may be required to

Capital Power therefore recommends that PJM develop and maintain a public strategic work plan identifying priority initiatives, the responsible committee or PJM office, expected milestones, target decision dates, dependencies on other initiatives, and the expected filing pathway. PJM should also consider simplifying its committee structure or assigning broader mandates to fewer forums where issues are cross-cutting. 2. FPA 205 Filing Rights: Utilize a Well-Defined “Jump Ball” Mechanism
Capital Power does not support materially expanding PJM’s unilateral section 205 filing authority in a manner that would bypass existing stakeholder approval rights over major market- design provisions. Materially altering the allocation of existing section 205 filing rights, including by relocating substantive market-design or transmission-planning provisions into documents more directly controlled by PJM, could disturb the balance of authority that underpins stakeholder participation and reduce confidence that major market-design changes will be developed through a transparent, multi-sector process. With that being said, Capital Power recognizes the Commission’s concern that gridlock and supermajority thresholds may sometimes leave the Commission without a clear set of alternatives when urgent reforms are needed. A carefully designed “jump ball” mechanism could strike the right balance without rendering stakeholder participation advisory.

As an example, the Company looks to section 11.1.5 of ISO-NE’s Participants Agreement (the “jump ball” provision),2 which has helped optimize the Commission’s decision-making authority

3. Board Reform to Promote Accountability and Transparency
Capital Power supports reforms that strengthen the transparency and accountability of the PJM Board and specifically suggests two vehicles for doing so: public observation of non- confidential meetings and annual governance reporting. The Commission has long recognized independence as a core characteristic of RTO governance, and transparency is necessary to ensure that independence exists not only in form, but also in practice.6 The Commission has also emphasized the importance of RTO and ISO responsiveness to customers, stakeholders, and ultimately the consumers who benefit from and pay for electricity service.7

To be clear, enhanced Board accountability should not be confused with unilateral Board control over detailed market design. The Company asserts that the Board’s proper role is to set strategic direction, ensure that PJM management is prioritizing the region’s most significant risks, and hold management accountable for execution, while the detailed development of market rules should continue to occur through a transparent stakeholder process, subject to Commission review. Longer Board terms, reasonable maximum service limits, and qualification criteria that ensure familiarity with the PJM region would help stabilize Board oversight while preserving independence.

Capital Power supports greater transparency into Board decision-making via public observation of non-confidential strategic sessions where appropriate. Capital Power recognizes that not all Board matters can or should be conducted publicly. Board sessions should remain closed where necessary to address personnel matters, cybersecurity, physical security, enforcement, litigation, privileged legal advice, commercially sensitive information, Critical Energy/Electric Infrastructure Information, or other confidential information protected by applicable law or Commission policy. In addition, to further enhance visibility of the Board’s decision-making processes, Capital Power also supports requiring PJM to provide public explanations when the Board acts on significant market-design, resource adequacy, transmission planning, interconnection, or governance matters. PJM should provide a public report explaining the Board’s disposition, the legal and policy basis for the decision, the principal alternatives considered, the reasons any material alternatives were rejected, and the extent to which the Board’s action reflects or departs from stakeholder positions. Such reforms would not require the Board to become a market design committee or intrude into confidential matters. Rather, they would

enhance confidence that the Board is exercising its oversight role in a transparent, accountable, and independent manner.

Capital Power also recommends that PJM prepare an annual governance report. The Commission has repeatedly asked for evidence and data to understand where the issues with governance lie. An annual governance report would help create that evidence going forward by documenting where governance bottlenecks actually arise and whether reforms are improving the timeliness and quality of PJM decision-making. At a minimum, such a report should identify major initiatives considered during the year; the origin and subject matter of each initiative; committee referrals; elapsed time by stage; number and reasons for deferrals; stakeholder voting outcomes by sector and in the aggregate; Board dispositions; whether alternatives were considered; whether the Board accepted, rejected, or modified committee or stakeholder recommendations; filing dates; implementation dates; whether expedited procedures were used; and whether the initiative later returned for redesign.

For independent power producers and other investors, such information is directly relevant to market confidence. Investors do not require certainty that their preferred policy outcome will prevail. They do require reasonable visibility into how PJM prioritizes issues, where decisions are made, when decisions are expected, and why significant proposals advance, stall, or are rejected. A public governance report would provide PJM, stakeholders, states, and the Commission with a common factual basis for assessing whether governance reforms are improving accountability and process discipline without undermining PJM’s stakeholder-driven framework.

B. State Participation in PJM Governance Needs to be Early, Formal, and Structured, But Jurisdictionally Disciplined.

Capital Power supports earlier and more structured state engagement in PJM governance. Many of the region’s most significant challenges, including resource adequacy, transmission

development, interconnection delays, generation retirements, siting, permitting, and large-load growth, cannot be addressed effectively through wholesale market rules alone. States have direct responsibilities over retail service, resource policy, siting, permitting, and other matters that materially affect whether PJM can maintain reliability and accommodate load growth. State perspectives should therefore be incorporated before positions become entrenched and before major proposals reach the end of the stakeholder process.

Formal state involvement in RTO governance is not novel, and an examination of each model demonstrates the spectrum on which formal participation can exist. Although none should be imported wholesale into PJM, a tailored approach that uses aspects of each may strike the appropriate balance. SPP and its Regional State Committee (“RSC”), comprised of one designated commissioner from each state regulatory commission having jurisdiction, provides perhaps the strongest example of formal state authority. The SPP RSC has “primary” responsibility for determining regional proposals in areas such as transmission cost allocation, financial transmission rights, remote resources, and regional resource adequacy.8 SPP demonstrates that defined state authority over resource adequacy and cost allocation can be formalized without eliminating the RTO Board or stakeholder process.

ISO-NE’s model provides states a formal platform, through its New England State Committee on Electricity (“NESCOE”), while appropriately limiting its authority to unilaterally make tariff changes.  NESCOE represents the policy perspectives of the New England governors on resource adequacy and system planning and expansion, is funded through the ISO’s budget, and is recognized as the FERC-approved Regional State Committee for New England.9 But NESCOE does not have unilateral authority to implement tariff or market-rule changes and instead

PJM already has a foundation for such engagement through OPSI, which was created to promote and protect the public interest in reliable and reasonably priced electric service within the PJM Control Area, and provides a vehicle for coordinated state participation. Rather than creating duplicative or conflicting state processes, the Commission should encourage reforms that clarify and strengthen OPSI’s role, coordinate that role with any Governors’ Collaborative function, and provide transparent channels through which state-identified issues can be considered in PJM governance.

Capital Power therefore supports requiring PJM to consult with OPSI before making section 205 filings related to resource adequacy, regional transmission cost allocation, and other matters that materially affect state responsibilities. To further bolster the states’ voice, Capital Power also supports including state representation in the Board nominating process. A state role in the Nominating Committee would improve regional legitimacy and help ensure that Board candidates understand the state-federal interface that is increasingly central to PJM’s reliability, resource adequacy, and transmission challenges. At the same time, Board selection reforms should preserve Board independence and should not make Board members representatives of particular states or constituencies. Finally, any enhanced state participation should be coordinated and transparent. To the extent OPSI, the Governors’ Collaborative, consumer advocates, or other state representatives are given formal roles, those roles should be clearly defined to avoid duplicative processes, inconsistent late-stage interventions, or uncertainty over which state body speaks for which purpose. The objective should be early engagement, durable outcomes, and better information for PJM, stakeholders, states, and the Commission, not another layer of procedural complexity.

III. CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, Capital Power respectfully urges the Commission to pursue targeted PJM governance reforms that improve transparency, accountability, state engagement, and decisional efficiency while preserving meaningful stakeholder participation and the existing balance of section 205-related rights. Capital Power appreciates the Commission’s leadership in this proceeding and looks forward to continued engagement on reforms that support reliable
