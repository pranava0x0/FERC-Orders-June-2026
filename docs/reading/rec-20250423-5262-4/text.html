5. 
My testimony addresses two separate topics related to both the Commission’s Order and 
PJM’s Answer.  First, I examine the resource adequacy issues raised in the proceeding and the 
potential remedies offered by PJM and others.  I offer several potential constructive policies that 
the Commission could support to improve the performance of PJM’s resource adequacy construct 
in the face of rapid additions of large loads while also identifying those policies which the 
Commission should avoid.  Second, to facilitate the discussion of the appropriate means of 
allocating the costs of integrating large loads onto the system, I provide data that I have collected 
on the estimated integration costs of large loads onto the PJM system from 2022 onwards. 
6. 
I reach five primary conclusions as a result of my analysis. 
7. 
First, resource adequacy concerns associated with rapid large load additions such as 
datacenters stem from all large load additions, regardless of whether or not they are co-located, 
and are fundamentally driven by the large individual size of large load projects and their rapid 
development time from conception to energization.  These characteristics make load growth very 
lumpy, hard to predict, and may lead to load needing service before the market can respond with 
additional new capacity.  PJM’s market design, including its competitive capacity market, the 
Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”), and other elements such as its interconnection queue and load 
forecasting function must continue to evolve to meet the new challenges posed by the rapid growth 
in large load additions. 
8. 
Second, however, the RPM market design has not exhibited any general market failure nor 
are load large additions a reason to jettison the RPM altogether.  The RPM has performed very 
well historically in terms of maintaining resource adequacy at cost-effective prices for customers.  
The RPM remains the best underlying platform for meeting the challenges created by large load 
additions, and certain improvements could better optimize it to meet this challenge.

Third, there are a number of concrete steps that the Commission and PJM can take to improve 
the RPM’s efficacy in reliably, speedily, and cost-effectively integrating large load.  These steps 
particularly focus on facilitating the timely entry of short lead-time supply; providing better signals 
for the entry of all types of supply in response to changing trends in load growth; and removing 
barriers to entry for all resource types.  First, PJM should continue efforts to improve the accuracy 
of price formation in both its energy and capacity markets, with particular emphasis on removing 
out-of-market price distortions that lead prices to diverge from system fundamentals.  Second, 
PJM should improve its forecasting of large load by standardizing and making mandatory the 
process whereby TOs report new large loads to PJM, and it should incorporate large load into the 
load forecast more frequently than the present once-per-year cadence and provide as much 
transparency and detail on this process as possible.  Third, PJM should continue to improve the 
speed at which new projects are able to clear the generation interconnection queue and the 
responsiveness of the queue to emergent capacity supply needs.  This should foremost take the 
form of streamlining, automating, and accelerating the current queue process as much as possible.  
If further enhancements beyond improving the base queue process are needed, PJM could consider 
adding a fast-track interconnection process similar to the current one-off Reliability Resource 
Initiative (“RRI”) that offers accelerated treatment for capacity supply resources that have the 
greatest impact on resource adequacy and can enter quickly that would be triggered when PJM has 
an acute need for incremental new supply.  Fourth, PJM should seek ways to allow datacenters 
and other large loads to participate in the RPM as demand response as much and as easily as 
possible, such as by harmonizing the PJM and EPA rules regarding run-time limits for backup 
diesel generators.  Finally, PJM should consider adding an improved backstop reliability 
procurement mechanism to the RPM that seeks to procure additional short-lead or higher-cost 
capacity supply resources in cases where the RPM does not clear sufficient supply to meet a 
minimum adequate level of resource adequacy. 
10. Fourth, PJM and the Commission should avoid policies that undermine, distort, or 
circumvent PJM’s markets.  In particular, PJM should avoid policies that tie load’s entry to it 
bringing with it self-supplied or contracted new supply, or that offer benefits or subsidies to 
generators that are tied to new entrant loads.  Such policies distort markets because they effectively 
subsidize a certain set of supply resources – new resources tied to new entrant loads – whose 
reliability attributes are not otherwise distinguishable from other capacity supply.  In addition to

discriminating against existing resources in favor of new ones, this harms the market by 
suppressing prices, reducing entry of non-advantaged supply resources, and furthering the 
potential exit of existing resources, all of which undermine reliability and frustrate the original 
objective of reliably integrating new load.  One of the options PJM offers as potential avenues for 
large load integration – Options 6 – is of this nature and should be rejected.  Another of PJM’s 
options – Option 7 – as currently written also carries the potential to undermine markets and 
discriminate against non-favored resources because it appears to contemplate giving PJM broad 
discretion to condition new large load entry on vague PJM-determined resource adequacy concerns 
with new load entry under such conditions only allowed if the load provides PJM with interruption 
rights, distorting capacity prices when a clear signal for new entry and retention of existing 
resources is needed.  While it is reasonable to reflect the ability and willingness of large loads to 
interrupt in the load integration process, interruption rights should not be a condition for new entry 
based on resource adequacy concerns. 
11. Finally, with respect to costs of integrating large loads into the transmission network, I find 
that recent historical integration costs are generally proportionate to the size of the load seeking to 
enter the market, but costs are extremely variable even after accounting for size differences.  This 
variability generally appears to be due to unique locational factors that vary from load to load such 
as the position of the load within the broader system topology and the physical environment around 
the new load site.  While load size is a significant determinant of the cost of integrating large loads, 
location clearly matters a great deal and can cause the cost to vary in idiosyncratic and 
unpredictable ways.  This location-dependence suggests that sending the correct incentives via 
transmission rates to new load to locate at efficient places on the grid is important for integrating 
such load efficiently with respect to total system costs. 
II. RESOURCE ADEQUACY ISSUES RELATED TO ADDITIONS OF LARGE LOADS TO THE PJM 
SYSTEM 
A. RESOURCE ADEQUACY CONCERNS RELATED TO LARGE LOAD 
ADDITIONS ARE REASONABLE, BUT DO NOT REFLECT A GENERAL 
FAILURE OF MARKETS, OR OF THE CAPACITY MARKET IN PARTICULAR, 
THAT JUSTIFY PJM’S PROPOSALS 
12. Both the Commission and PJM have voiced concerns about the resource adequacy 
implications of rapid additions of large loads to the PJM system, both with respect to large loads 
that are co-located with generation specifically, as well as large loads more generally regardless

that there would otherwise be a resource adequacy “imbalance.”  These proposed options appear 
to be a response to the general resource adequacy concerns arising from rapid large load additions, 
but their discriminatory and market-circumventing elements suggest that PJM desires more control 
than its markets afford or lacks confidence that PJM’s existing resource adequacy construct is fully 
up to the task of bringing forth the supply needed to maintain resource adequacy in the face of 
increasing load growth driven by large load additions.

14. While questions about resource adequacy in the face of rapid load growth are valid, it is 
important to first get the right problem diagnosis and then develop solutions that address the 
problem directly, without harming markets or causing other unintended consequences. 
15. First, at bottom, the potential for resource adequacy problems related to large load additions 
arise primarily from the very fast and very lumpy load growth caused by large load interconnecting 
with the system, regardless of whether or not it is co-located with generation.  From a resource 
adequacy perspective, a megawatt of load added to the system creates a need for the same amount 
of incremental supply regardless of whether the load is co-located with generator or not.  And even 
in configurations where the load does not take network service and takes supply from a generator 
that does not participate as a capacity resource, the net effect is the same – the system has a need 
for incremental supply because it must replace the generation offering direct supply to the load.  
Thus, any consideration of resource adequacy issues driven by large load should consider all such 
load, regardless of whether it is co-located with generation.   
16. Second, the resource adequacy concern surrounding large loads arises from the desire or need 
on the part of large load customers to enter the market faster and in larger quantities than many 
types of capacity supply resources can respond.  New datacenter loads can be constructed and 
come online in as little as two to three years and can be as large as two gigawatts or potentially 
even more at a single point of interconnection.  And the emerging business models surrounding 
artificial intelligence and other datacenter applications place high value on the ability to rapidly 
deploy large amounts of additional datacenter processing capacity.  Figure 1 below demonstrates 
the speed at which data center load can potentially enter the market, as reflected by PJM’s evolving 
load forecasts from January 2023 to January 2025.  Over this period the forecast for the 2027 
summer peak, just over two years from the present date, increased by about 10 gigawatts (from 
154 to 164 gigawatts).  As of 2023, these loads were not part of the planning process or in the load 
forecast used to set demand for the RPM, but over a two-year span they progressed development 
to the point where they are now projected to enter in about two years from the present.  Increases 
in the load forecast for later years suggest this trend is likely to continue.

B. BOTH SHORT AND LONG LEAD TIME SUPPLY RESOURCES CAN 
CONTRIBUTE TO MEETING THE CHALLENGE OF LARGE LOAD 
GROWTH 
22. Facilitating entry of short-lead time capacity supply resources is a critical element to 
improving the responsiveness of the RPM to large load additions. While long-lead-time 
conventional generation assets form the majority of PJM’s existing capacity supply portfolio, 
certain types of capacity supply resource are able to enter the market on shorter lead times that are 
comparable to the 2-to-3-year development cycle for new datacenter load.  First, demand response 
resources of all types can typically enter service less than three years after the start of initial 
development of the resource.  Unlike most resource classes, demand response does not need to 
pass through the interconnection queue.  When qualifying their resources for the RPM Base 
Residual Auction (“BRA”), demand response providers are allowed to offer on the basis of a 
projected marketing plan rather than actual contracted DR customers, effectively deferring the 
marketing and contracting of the underlying DR resources to the three-year period between the 
BRA and the delivery year.  And finally, the physical investment required to qualify a new demand 
response resource is typically extremely modest compared to generation resources and is thus not 
subject to the equipment supply chain and permitting challenges that can delay more traditional 
resources.   
23. Second, demand response provided by curtailment of new entrant datacenters themselves is 
a particularly compelling possible source of new short-lead time supply because by definition it 
will have the same lead time and similar size/scale to the new entrant load.  A recent study from 
the Nicholas Institute at Duke University found that a significant amount of datacenter load can 
reliably integrate with electric systems across the country without the need to incrementally expand 
transmission or generation infrastructure if that load agrees to curtail in a small percentage of 
hours.  Specifically, the study found that if new datacenter loads were willing to curtail in 0.5% of 
the hours in which they would normally operate, 98 GW of new load nationally and 18 GW in 
PJM (equivalent to about 11% of PJM’s current peak) could be integrated without the need to 
incrementally expand transmission or generation.11

24. Third, even if they are not able to curtail, new datacenter loads often have backup diesel 
generators or batteries that can allow them to serve as demand response if they can be run in 
response to an instruction by PJM and thus be deployed under the same conditions and rules as 
other emergency or pre-emergency demand response resources. 
25. And finally, storage resources, while they must pass through the interconnection queue, 
typically have much shorter development cycles than other types of generation because they are a 
scalable, land-use-efficient, off-the-shelf technology with no fuel infrastructure needs and much-
reduced permitting requirements relative to traditional generation.  Taken together, these pools of 
potential short-lead supply represent a significant potential tool for integrating large loads while 
maintaining resource adequacy. 
26. And while long-lead conventional generation cannot immediately respond to “surprise” load 
growth as shown in Figure 1, it still can play a significant role in maintaining reliability over the 
longer term under such conditions.  Long-lead-time generation is able to respond to major 
directional trends as they emerge in the fundamental data such as the load forecast, and in market 
price signals.  But to do so to the greatest extent, such resources need the best, most accurate 
fundamental data possible along with a robust market that produces accurate competitive market 
price signals. 
C. THE COMMISSION SHOULD SUPPORT POLICIES THAT FACILITATE 
ENTRY OF NEW SUPPLY WITHOUT HARMING MARKETS 
27. Given this backdrop, there are several Commission actions and policies that could both 
facilitate the addition of short-lead-time capacity supply when needed while also increasing the 
responsiveness of longer-lead conventional resources, all without harming the underlying market. 
28. First, the Commission should support ongoing efforts by PJM to improve the accuracy of the 
competitive market prices that generators and other capacity resources rely on to signal whether 
to enter, remain in, or exit the market.  This should clearly include the RPM but also energy and 
ancillary services markets as well, which are an important source of revenue for many capacity 
resources.  Generally, the Commission should support policies that drive market prices to 
accurately reflect the underlying fundamentals and reliability requirements of the system as they 
evolve over time.  Examples of this include PJM’s recent changes to the RPM which improved the 
accuracy of its reliability modeling and resource accreditation to reflect the marginal contribution

29. Second, while forecasting load in the current environment is challenging, Figure 1 and the 
PJM analytic work and committee proceedings surrounding load forecasting strongly suggest that 
improvements to the load forecast are possible to provide more information, and more accurate 
information on future large load additions to market participants.  PJM currently relies on 
Transmission Owners to provide estimates of future large load additions to their systems, based 
on their interactions with customers.  This process is not fully standardized across the various TOs, 
nor is it mandatory that all TOs provide an assessment of the large load situation in their zones 
every year (even if there is no activity).  As a result, PJM must make a number of judgment calls 
about whether to include specific loads, and the timing which with the loads enter the market, in 
forming the load forecast.  The large load situation may be uncertain in parts of the RTO because 
not all TOs have provided an assessment.  This ultimately reduces transparency and confidence in 
the resulting forecast.  Further, this process only occurs once per year, which means that in the 
current environment of rapid load growth the load forecast becomes obsolete within a couple 
months of issuance.  Creating detailed, consistent, mandatory rules around when and how TOs 
must report load additions to PJM would help improve the accuracy of and confidence in the load 
forecast, and increasing the frequency with which large load additions are reported to PJM from 
once per year to two to four times per year would improve the responsiveness of the information 
for market participants.  Finally, PJM should publicly provide as much detail and transparency as 
possible on the large load information provided to it by TOs and on any adjustments and 
assumptions it makes in incorporating the information into the load forecast.

D. THE COMMISSION SHOULD AVOID POLICIES THAT ARE UNDULY 
DISCRIMINATORY AND UNDERMINE MARKETS, INCLUDING PJM 
OPTION 6 AND OPTION 7 (AS PROPOSED) 
35. Two of the policy options outlined by PJM in their Answer, Option 6 and Option 7 (as 
proposed), are discriminatory and have distortionary and market-circumventing aspects that apply 
in a much broader set of circumstances than the supplemental procurement concept discussed 
above.  Without modification, these policies discriminate in favor of new resources over existing 
ones and are likely to harm the market, ultimately undermining reliability and increasing customer 
costs. 
36. When considering how to handle large load additions, the Commission should generally 
avoid policies that discriminate among similarly situated resources and circumvent, distort, or 
otherwise undermine PJM markets.  Policies that offer discriminatory subsidies or advantages to 
certain resource classes based on their vintage or the entities that they contract with are examples 
of this.  Such policies are counter-productive to the goal of ensuring resource adequacy in the face 
of rapid load growth because they undermine the very markets that are critically needed to bring 
forth supply to meet increasing demand, and thus ultimately risk a breakdown of markets 
altogether and a need to rely on high-cost, non-market approaches to an ever-increasing extent.  
To the extent that a policy does go outside the normal operation of markets it should be carefully 
designed to minimize market impacts and only go into effect when the reliability need is critical, 
such as the supplemental procurement concept I discuss above. 
37. Related to this, the Commission should also avoid policies that seek to ration new demand 
entry due to resource adequacy concerns either by outright denying or delaying demand entry or

PDF page 22

20 
 
conditioning entry on the resource effectively bringing with it supply that matches its need.  
Fundamentally such policies harm markets and economic activity more generally because they 
impede the ability of new demand that produces economic value to enter in a timely manner.  If 
new entry is conditioned upon or incentivized  to bring new supply resources with the load, it is 
likely to subsidize the new resources in a manner that will undermine and harm the market, , and 
will discriminate against the supply (both new and existing) that is dependent upon the market. 
38. In violation of these principles, PJM’s Option 6 proposes that new large loads that build or 
contract with new or otherwise-retiring capacity supply resources (whether co-located or not) 
would secure favorable treatment for those supply resources in the PJM generator interconnection 
process.  PJM suggests that this favorable treatment could take the form of accelerated 
interconnection studies and/or enhanced provisional interconnection service options that 
accelerate the ability of new generation to achieve commercial operation.17  The core problem with 
Option 6 is that it offers a thing of value – favored treatment in the generation interconnection 
process – in exchange for an action – contracting with a new large load - that is unrelated to the 
underlying cost and resource adequacy aspects of a given capacity supply resource, and is only 
available to a new resources that are able to secure a contract with a large load. 
39. Favorable interconnection queue treatment is unquestionably of high value in the present 
tight market environment: resources that get through the queue quicker incur lower financing costs 
during development and are able to access market revenues sooner than resources without this 
advantage.  But this advantage would only be conferred on a subset of resources that 1) are new 
(or would otherwise retire, although expedited interconnection service is not of value to retiring 
resources), and 2) are able to secure a contract with (or are self-built by) a new large load resource.  
Neither of these features has any bearing on the ability of resources to physically supply capacity, 
nor does it have any bearing on their underlying cost of providing capacity.  Such treatment is 
discriminatory because it offers a benefit to a specific subset of new resources while denying that 
benefit to existing resources and to new resources that are unable to contract with new large loads.

40. Consider two otherwise-identical gas combined cycle generation resources, one

each resource will be identical, and but-for any favored interconnection treatment awarded by PJM 
in connection with this proposed policy, their costs of construction and operation will be similarly 
identical.  PJM Option 6 would nonetheless discriminate between these two resources, effectively 
lowering net cost of entry of the with-contract resource while not conferring such benefits to the 
other resource.  This approach is functionally equivalent to an out-of-market payment or subsidy 
(for the same resource adequacy services) which effectively lowers the cost of the with-contract 
class and thus distorts and depresses market economics for all other resources (including all 
without-contract new resources and all existing resources).  The ultimate endpoint of such a policy 
is to drive all resource additions to seek contracts with large load and depress economics for other 
resources, leading to reduced new entry outside of the large load contract path and potentially 
retirements of existing resources.  Moreover, unlike the supplemental procurement concept that I 
previously outlined, PJM Option 6 as proposed has no limitations that would restrict its application 
to only those instances when it is needed to avoid a resource adequacy shortfall, meaning that the 
discriminatory treatment and market distortions that it causes would be applicable under all types 
of market conditions.  It also potentially lays the foundation for entities serving large loads – which 
would the incumbent TOs if they prevail in their tariff interpretation – to obtain an advantage in 
the generation queue for proposed new build (developed by the TOs or others) that is tied to a 
specific load rather than the market as a whole.   
41. Option 6 is essentially a form of vintage pricing in that it offers an effective subsidy for a 
favored class of new resources, while denying that subsidy to non-favored new resources and 
existing resources.  This lowers the effective cost of procuring capacity from the favored resources 
relative to their competitive level, which results in depression of market clearing prices below the 
competitive level within the RPM market clearing framework.  This depresses revenue for non-
favored resources with no offsetting benefits, which ultimately chills new entry from resources 
that are unable to contract with new large loads and potentially leads to market exit by existing 
resources, undermining reliability and reducing overall market efficiency.  For these reasons, the 
Commission has indicated a preference for utilizing non-discriminatory, market-based solutions

that pay a single clearing price to similarly-situated resources to the greatest extent possible.18  
This principle remains correct and should lead the Commission to reject Option 6. 
42. PJM already has a market that is intended to bring forth new supply and a recently-reformed 
non-discriminatory interconnection queue process.  And as discussed above, there are a range of 
improvements that can be made to markets to improve their ability to accommodate new demand 
from large loads.  To the extent that PJM seeks to differentiate between resources within the queue 
cycle process, that differentiation should be made based on the underlying contribution to resource 
adequacy that resources provide, such as the factors considered in the RRI process, and not based 
on contractual ties with specific loads that are unrelated to the underlying resource adequacy 
contribution of the resource.  Or it should be targeted narrowly to address a demonstrated resource 
adequacy shortfall, as with the supplemental procurement discussed above.  Accordingly, Option 
6 should be rejected.  To the extent that the Commission nonetheless considers PJM Option 6 it 
should at a minimum seek to limit its application to periods where the RPM clears at its price cap, 
and it should require that any generation resource benefiting from expedited interconnection under 
Option 6 be subject to the Minimum Offer Price Rule so as to reduce the detrimental price 
suppressing effect of any subsidy.

47. I have analyzed the estimated costs incurred by PJM TOs to integrate new large loads onto

48. As PJM discusses in their answer, when a singular new large load such as a datacenter seeks

potentially a new substation or upgrades to an existing substation, and sometimes more remote 
system reinforcements to reliably accommodate the load within the TO’s zone.  Once the TO has 
proposed a solution in the form of a Supplemental Project, PJM conducts a “PJM Do No Harm” 
study to “evaluate whether a proposed Supplemental Project will adversely impact the reliability 
of the Transmission System as represented in the planning models used in other PJM reliability 
standard studies.”23  If the PJM Do No Harm study finds that the proposed Supplemental Project

the 1,000 MW range, integration costs can range from as low as $70 million to as much as $240 
million.  In general, this high degree of variation is present across the full range of load sizes. 
54. The high degree of size-independent variation in large load integration costs suggests that 
locational factors specific to each load are a major additional driver of cost and can either result in 
much lower or higher costs than would be expected on average for a load of a given size.  A review 
of the nature of the actual supplemental projects that form this data confirms that this is the case.  
Key factors driving load integration costs include the location of the large load site relative to 
existing transmission elements, the terrain and land use around the large load site, whether 
integrating the load requires construction of a new substation or allows use of an existing 
substation, and whether or not the existing transmission system in the local region is robust enough 
so as not to require additional reinforcements to reliably integrate the load.   
55. It is clear that location matters a great deal for and can cause the cost to vary in idiosyncratic 
and unpredictable ways.  This location-dependence suggests that sending the correct incentives via 
transmission rates to new large loads to locate efficiently is important for minimizing total system 
costs.
