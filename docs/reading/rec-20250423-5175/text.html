COMMENTS OF THE EDISON ELECTRIC INSTITUTE

I. BACKGROUND
The electric grid has long provided an extraordinary platform to reliably and affordably serve the nation’s electric customers.  Among other things, the electric grid supports and facilitates the deployment of resilient, reliable sources of power, at scale, to address customers’ needs.  After an extended period of relatively low demand growth rates, the country’s electric sector has entered an era of rapid transformation and unprecedented and sustained load growth. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation (“NERC”), among others, has observed

that load and demand on the nation’s electric system will continue to increase as a result of many factors, including growing digitization and electrification, as well as the “changing resource mix and increased reliance on variable resources and natural gas.”4  The Commission, in its most recent State of the Markets Report, stated that “[r]egions continue to confront challenges in assuring resource adequacy – challenges caused by extreme weather events, a changing resource mix, and shifts in load profiles.”5  The report also noted that most RTO/ISO peak demand forecasts have shown a trend towards faster load growth in recent years, shifting from prior projections of flattening or declining growth to projections of increasing growth.  A key facet of this growth in many regions is the emergence of new large load additions (e.g., from data centers).  Growth from this segment of the power sector is forecast to persist well into the future, and many regions are likely to see increases in large new loads seeking to connect to the electric system over the next several years.  For example, a recent Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report estimated that data center load growth tripled over the past decade and is projected to double or triple by 2028.6  A 2024 report by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) projected that data centers will grow to consume up to 9.1 percent of U.S. electricity generation annually by 2030, compared to an estimated 4 percent today.7  At the same time, the electric industry is in the midst of a transformation, both in terms of the generation mix serving customers and customer makeup.  The electric system resource mix is evolving rapidly.

Ensuring that the nation’s electric companies can accommodate these generational changes and continue to serve customers in a reliable and affordable manner will require new and upgraded energy infrastructure at all points on the electric value chain: generation, transmission, and distribution. II. COMMENTS
a. Electric companies stand ready to lead energy infrastructure
expansion.
Electric companies are best positioned to lead the expansion of energy infrastructure driven by the dynamics previously described.  This year, EEI members will invest $200 billion to make the energy grid smarter, more dynamic, more flexible, and more secure; to diversify the nation’s energy mix; and to integrate new technologies that benefit customers.  Electric companies have extensive knowledge of the electric system, deep, collaborative relationships with states and stakeholders, and, for decades, managed the development of infrastructure projects that take years to design, finance, and build.  Each of these capabilities is essential in assuring efficient, cost-effective, and timely infrastructure additions.  Moreover, experience in

b. The existing regulatory model can accommodate significant demand
growth.
The regulatory model that governs the electric sector is long-standing, well-understood and provides transparent processes that ensure prudent investment to serve customers.  This model successfully accommodated significant demand growth at different periods in the nation’s history and can continue to do so in the current environment.  In addition, this model provides stability and facilitates valuable partnerships between electric companies, regulators, stakeholders, and customers.  It will be a critical supporting element in meeting this extraordinary period of new demand growth.  Further, this regulatory model has been carefully developed over more than one hundred years and, as a result, electric companies, customers, and regulators have long-established approaches for working collaboratively to plan the supply additions and system expansions required to serve customers.  When they function properly, these approaches foster collaboration that ensures the necessary investments are made, infrastructure is developed, and critical public services are provided.

This regulatory model has, among other things, helped to build an electric system that evolves and modernizes as it integrates new supply resources and demand (e.g., data centers). This networked system captures economies of scale and allows electric companies to meet new demand efficiently and cost-effectively, as demonstrated by the reliable addition of thousands of data centers across the nation.  Indeed, as noted above, America’s electric companies have been at the forefront in partnering with new large loads, such as data centers, to enable timely interconnections to the grid.  These instances demonstrate electric companies’ ability to proactively plan and invest in transmission and generation infrastructure that can facilitate large load development while achieving broader benefits for existing customers and communities.

As the Commission recognized in its February 20th order in this proceeding, there are many different co-location configurations. Co-location, depending on configuration, provides optionality to serve data centers and may support data center development necessary for economic development and national security.  This could include:

• Access to an integrated network of supply resources that permit cost-efficient operations
• Holistic and comprehensive system planning that identifies new grid infrastructure to ensure reliable delivery of power

• Provision of black start and ancillary services
• Cybersecurity and physical security measures and protections will all contribute benefits to co-located load that is connected to the electric system.

c. Principles for the Commission to bear in mind as it evaluates next
steps.
As the Commission evaluates the comments it receives in response to the Show Cause Order and contemplates its next steps, EEI asks that it keep several key principles in mind.  First and foremost, the Commission should strive to ensure that reliable system operations and resource adequacy are maintained and enhanced over the long term.  At the same time, co- location policies should be grounded in cost-causation principles, ensuring that data center customers pay just and reasonable rates for service they use and protecting existing customers from any potential undue and unfair shifts in costs.  The Commission should also respect the jurisdiction of state regulatory authorities and consider how collaboration with state commissions, and RTOs/ISOs where applicable, may be valuable in addressing specific co- location arrangements.  Adherence to these principles will ensure that rules and regulations will enable electric companies to continue their long history of delivering reliable and affordable service to customers and meeting the new system demands in a cost-effective, efficient, and timely way.
