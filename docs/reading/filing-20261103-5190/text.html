A. 
MISO Has Not Demonstrated That Its Proposal To Leave The Substance Of 
The Standards To Its BPMs Is Consistent With The FPA 
1. 
MISO Overstates The Detail Provided In The Tariff 
MISO argues that proposed Section 37A “contains the core obligations” applicable to Large 
Loads and Computational Loads, while the BPMs will merely supply the “technical and 
administrative detail” needed to implement them.17  According to MISO, the Rule of Reason does 
not require “every engineering parameter, data format, testing protocol, or implementation 
procedure to be frozen in a filed tariff.”18 
MISO vastly overstates the level of detail included in its proposed tariff revisions.  While 
MISO maintains that it is merely proposing to leave implementation details to the manuals, the 
detail omitted from the tariff goes far beyond engineering details and technical data submission 
requirements.  To the contrary, MISO’s proposed tariff revisions effectively grant MISO authority 
to impose new reliability requirements on transmission customers serving Large Loads, but 
without providing any detail regarding the substance of these reliability requirements.   
MISO’s treatment of ramp smoothing illustrates the problem.  MISO explains for the first 
time in its Answer that associated generation, energy storage, uninterruptible power supply 
systems, and other load-management technologies may be used to smooth a customer’s changes 
in demand.19  None of this detail appears in the tariff.  The only reference to ramp smoothing in 
MISO’s proposed tariff revisions is a single statement in Section 37A.7 that ramp limits shall

B. 
MISO Has Not Demonstrated That Its Proposed Large Load Definition Is Just 
And Reasonable Or Provides Customers Adequate Notice 
1. 
MISO Does Not Resolve The Treatment Of Distribution-Connected 
Loads 
As DCC and other parties have pointed out, MISO’s proposed definition of Large Load 
could be interpreted as extending to loads interconnected to the distribution system.35  Although 
other tariff revisions proposed by MISO appear to contemplate transmission-interconnected load, 
the definition of Large Load is ambiguous.36  To eliminate any ambiguity, DCC and other parties 
urge the Commission to direct MISO to revise the definition of Large Load to explicitly reference 
a voltage threshold and/or interconnection to the MISO transmission system.37 
In its Answer, MISO argues that these concerns are misplaced and that Section 37A does 
not apply to “distribution-connected load that is not taking MISO Transmission Service.”38 While 
DCC appreciates MISO’s effort to provide additional clarity, MISO’s statement does not resolve 
the ambiguity DCC identified or eliminate the need for tariff language explicitly limiting the 
definition to load interconnected to the MISO transmission system.39  In practice, utilities take 
transmission service to serve all of their loads, including those interconnected to the distribution 
system.40  As a result, MISO’s statement could be interpreted as implying that the proposed 
definition of Large Load extends to customers that are interconnected to the distribution system 
merely because the energy used to serve these customers flows over the transmission system.

MISO Has Not Demonstrated That Its Transition Framework Will Minimize

1. 
MTEP/EPR Status Is Not A Reasonable Proxy For Commercial 
Reliance 
MISO maintains that its proposal to exempt from ramp and ride‑through requirements 
certain Large Loads that are associated with a transmission project approved in the MISO 
Transmission Expansion Plan (“MTEP”) or submitted for Expedited Project Review (“EPR”) prior 
to certain dates will “limit the potential need to revisit equipment selections and operating 
arrangements made in reliance on existing requirements[.]”56  But MISO fails to demonstrate that 
there is any nexus between when a transmission project is approved in the MTEP process or 
submitted to the EPR process and the timing of when customers make binding investment 
decisions.  In practice, customers may execute utility service agreements, secure financing, procure 
major equipment, and make other irreversible business decisions well in advance of when 
associated transmission is approved in MTEP or submitted into the EPR process.57  While limiting 
the exemption to customers based on the status of transmission facilities in the MTEP and EPR 
processes may be administratively convenient for MISO, MISO has not provided any evidence 
that its proposal will avoid disruption to existing arrangements consistent with the Show Cause 
Order.    
MISO also does not respond to DCC’s showing that this mismatch will result in materially 
different treatment of projects based solely on whether they require transmission upgrades through 
the MTEP or EPR processes.58  A substantially developed project that does not require such 
upgrades would receive no transition protection unless it commences service by December 4, 2026,

MISO’s principal defense of its framework is that it is preferable to a “project-by-project

and that “developing prescribed contractual terms and requirements would be unworkable for
