I. 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
The Indicated TOs agree with the recommendation of PJM that co-located end-use loads 
should be treated as load in front of the meter and designated as Network Load.6  Co-located load 
should be treated as load in front of the meter and designated as Network Load because it will 
ensure that such loads are appropriately studied, charged, and factored in operationally by PJM 
and transmission owners.  Doing otherwise would undercut reliability and exacerbate 
affordability concerns.  This approach offers the most reliability benefits and a cost allocation 
framework that addresses charges for use of, and reliance on, the transmission system.  
Otherwise, a customer that co-locates avoids “wires charges”—the fixed costs of the poles, 
wires, transformers, and substations that comprise the transmission and distribution network.  
These avoided costs are then subsidized by other customers, allowing co-location customers a 
free ride.  Beneficiaries of the transmission system must pay their share to avoid inappropriately 
shifting costs to other customers.

Assessment (“2024 LTRA”) that, in PJM, “[t]he trends in demand growth and resource additions

B. Co-location of Load 
Co-located load is network or point-to-point load that has contractual arrangements to be 
served by an adjacent generation resource that is physically interconnected to and synchronized 
with the transmission and/or distribution grid.  Indeed, it must be interconnected to the grid.17  
Co-located load uses and depends on the grid, causing changing flows on the grid from second to 
second.  There is an alternative arrangement where a load and accompanying generation never 
use the transmission or distribution system—they are islanded—but neither the Show Cause 
Order nor Commission jurisdiction extend to islanded non-interconnected arrangements.  Load 
cannot be both connected to the transmission and/or distribution grid and “fully isolated,” in the 
manner certain generation and large load interests have asserted.18

Indeed, any characterization of co-located load as “fully isolated” is false and misleading 
unless the load is not connected to either the transmission or distribution system.  If loads were 
“fully isolated” from the grid, then both the generation and the load would be islanded from the 
grid, and the PJM Tariff would not apply to them at all.  No interconnection under the PJM 
Tariff process would be needed.  The Indicated TOs consider all the arrangements being 
contemplated or requested as being interconnected with and synchronized with the transmission 
and/or distribution grid and thus not “fully isolated.”   
Although the existing PJM Tariff already accommodates co-located load, the 
Commission may have an interest in providing further clarity.  If so, a definition of “co-located 
load” could be added to the PJM Tariff that clarifies the expectations and obligations associated 
with such load.  Specifically, the Indicated TOs suggest that the following definition could be 
added to the PJM Tariff:  
“Co-located Load” shall mean load that has contractual 
arrangements to be served by an adjacent generation resource that is 
physically interconnected to and synchronized with the transmission 
or distribution grid.  Co-located Load is either Network Load or load 
being served using Point-to-Point Transmission Service, as 
applicable.  Such adjacent generation resource shall not meet the 
definition of Behind The Meter Generation.  Arrangements for Co-
located Load must have separate metering for load consumption and 
generator output as required under PJM Tariff, Part III.

would provide additional clarity to those directly impacted by co-located load arrangements.  At 
the same time, this proposed language would ensure that PJM and its transmission owners have 
adequate visibility of the loads affecting the transmission system for planning and reliability 
purposes.

III. 
DISCUSSION 
A. Classification of Co-located Load as Network Load Is Just and Reasonable. 
The PJM Tariff, in its current form, allows for co-location of load and generation on 
terms that are just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential.  The PJM Tariff 
allows end-use load and generation to co-locate expeditiously and on terms that are also fair to 
all other users of the grid and commensurate with cost causation principles.  To allow this, the 
PJM Tariff, consistent with the pro forma OATT, provides for two types of transmission service 
to be used in accommodating co-location arrangements: Network Service and Point-to-Point 
Service.  Both long-standing types of transmission service recognize that those that benefit from 
the transmission system must contribute to its costs.  
The rate design in PJM, incorporated into the PJM Tariff, permits owners to recover, on a 
postage stamp basis without consideration of the distance between load and generation, the 
embedded costs of their respective portions of the transmission grid from network customers.20  
As the Commission has explained, “PJM recovers the full revenue requirements of existing 
facilities (embedded costs) through its firm transmission rates.”21   
This concept of embedded costs of the transmission grid and associated cost 
responsibility of load based on fairness principles predates Order No. 888,22 FERC’s landmark

B. Co-Located Load Benefits From Essential Services Provided Using the Grid.

charges to users of the grid for essential reliability services, such as ancillary services.  As PJM 
acknowledged, there is no question that “[a]lthough co-located load is located behind the [point

System when consuming power and therefore benefits from the use of the Transmission System 
and Ancillary Services.”27  The same is true even when the co-located load is only connected to 
the distribution system, because ancillary services are required by both the transmission and 
distribution system operators to maintain the integrity and stability of the transmission and 
distribution system, as well as the power quality.28  In other words, co-located load cannot 
receive the benefits of such essential reliability services without the physical and synchronous 
interconnection to the transmission (and/or distribution) system.  These services are fundamental

C. All Load, Including Co-located Load, Is Used to Plan the Transmission 
System.

circumstances and challenges currently facing PJM’s capacity market would not benefit from 
further intervention and reform due to co-located load arrangements at a time when there are 
considerable resource adequacy concerns in the PJM footprint and there are efforts being made 
to advance certainty in the capacity market design.

iii. 
Please explain whether the existing Tariff rules are sufficient to ensure 
resource adequacy if increasing numbers of large existing generators 
choose to co-locate with load; 
 
Adding load located at a generator site to the system, like adding any other new load, introduces 
challenges to the existing resource adequacy problem.  But as with other such load, existing PJM 
Tariff rules should be sufficient to study the impacts as long as co-located load is recognized as

iv. 
What deactivation or interconnection modification studies should PJM

Co-located end-use load should be studied under the relevant state jurisdictional process for new 
load additions and PJM’s role should be limited to the standard transmission planning process for 
all new load additions.  Additionally, the loss of capacity from an existing generator seeking to 
enter into a co-location arrangement should be studied by PJM.  Because the federal role in end-

that want to connect to the grid should go through the established PJM study processes to ensure 
the reliability of the grid.  As stated above, if an existing generator proposes modifications to 
enable it to operate in a co-located arrangement, then any transmission upgrades related to the 
generator modifications would need to be paid by that generator.

All load additions create new demands for energy regardless of their location.  The resource

vi. 
Under what circumstances can PJM direct the operators of co-location 
arrangements to shed such load if deemed necessary to preserve system 
reliability during a declared emergency? 
 
If treated as Network Load, co-located end-use load can only be shed by PJM when required under 
the relevant NERC and PJM standards as documented in PJM’s Manual 13 on Emergency 
Operations.  The general terms and conditions governing service to co-located end-use load are 
regulated by states, which are free to offer optional or mandatory interruptible service rates.   
 
We note that failure to meter the co-located load will lead to anomalous results.  For example, if 
the generator trips offline without disconnecting from the transmission system, the unmetered load 
“appears” on the system and draws power from the grid.  In an extreme case, this can create a 
system emergency that requires load drop—which must come from other network load that did 
not cause the emergency.

In general, having reliable backup generation can benefit any end-use load by providing alternative 
sources of power in times of system emergency.  That capability can also benefit other end-use 
load customers if it can and is used for their benefit as well.  However, the presence of on-site

iv.

modified to best accommodate or encourage co-location arrangements to

No federal tariff changes are needed to promote co-location because the current PJM Tariff and 
state regulatory regimes can and do accommodate reliable and economic co-located configurations 
under existing rules.  If there is a hurdle to locating end-use load at generation sites, that hurdle is 
not the lack of a rate discount, but of confusion over the applicability of the established tariffs.  
The best way to ensure cost-effective co-location is to maintain consistent rules: treat co-located 
load as any other load on the system.  The Indicated TOs also note that states may choose to 
promote co-location arrangements through, for example, appropriate distribution tariffs or tax 
incentives—whether the states choose to do so is not within FERC’s jurisdiction or control.

vi. 
We also encourage parties to address any national security implications 
associated with co-location arrangements, including with respect to the 
specific issues discussed in this show cause order, and what actions, if 
any, the Commission should take in response. 
 
Allowing co-location arrangements to move forward rapidly and without regulatory ambiguity is 
the best way to achieve the national security goals of this Commission and this administration.  
The most straightforward way to accomplish that goal is simply to recognize that co-located load 
is Network Load like all other load on the system and should pay for Network Service like all other 
customers.  A clear statement from this Commission that the existing rules work, and that co-
located load can be served under existing rules is the fastest path to data center interconnections 
needed to win the artificial intelligence (AI) race.  In contrast, development of new forms of service, 
and efforts to mesh those new forms of service with the existing rules, will certainly get bogged 
down in extended litigation that will impede efforts to develop AI. 
 
Any delays associated with interconnection of co-located load experienced thus far have not been 
because the rates do not provide for artificial exemption of the arrangements from transmission 
rates, but because of efforts to dispute the applicability of those rates.  Importantly, this does not 
preclude PJM and the PJM TOs from considering and pursuing beneficial enhancements as the 
business areas surrounding co-location continue to evolve.  As previously noted, the Indicated TOs 
are attempting to consider potential beneficial enhancements collectively and with PJM, consistent 
with existing rights and responsibilities.

co-location arrangements with one or a few large customers?

There are two issues here: generation costs and transmission costs.  In general, the continued 
obligation, if any, of generation to serve classes of end-use customers who pay for that generation 
is a matter of state jurisdiction and concern.  Transmission costs are certainly a matter of FERC 
concern.  The addition of end-use load, including co-located load, at established cost-based rates 
typically benefits other customers with respect to transmission costs, because the shared embedded 
costs of the grid are paid by all customers, so that new load additions spread those costs more 
broadly.  Treating co-located end-use load like all other load additions by requiring them to take 
either Network Service or Point-to-Point Service ensures that other customers are protected.  In 
contrast, creating a new class of service where transmission costs are not paid, particularly one 
that exempts those customers for either paying for services or simply for reliance on the shared 
system, does not ensure equitable treatment of other customers on the system.
