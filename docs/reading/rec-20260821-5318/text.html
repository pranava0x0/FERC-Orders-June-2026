I.​
INTRODUCTION 
Voltus, Inc., (“Voltus”) is a distributed energy resource (“DER”) aggregator operating in 
all nine North American wholesale electricity markets. Voltus is a registered curtailment service 
provider (“CSP”) with PJM Interconnection, L.L.C., (“PJM”) aggregating the flexible attributes 
of retail load across the footprint and offering capacity, energy, and ancillary services products 
into PJM markets. Relevant to the provision of capacity, Voltus has participated in all Reliability 
Pricing Model (“RPM”) since BRA 2022/23 (held in early 2021) and has delivered its capacity 
commitments as Load Management in the associated delivery years. Voltus also has experience 
transacting PJM-accredited capacity bilaterally. Voltus’s commercial experience developing 
demand-side capacity resources closely informs these comments.

Given that RBP is highly likely to proceed, Voltus’s main concern is that the current 
framework unduly discriminates against demand-side resources that can provide near-term but 
durable capacity. First, the criteria for Qualifying RBO Offset (“QRBPO”) will count 
“reactivated” generation and storage resources against a zone’s RBP procurement target but not 
reactivated DR/DER sites. Not only is this difference in treatment unexplained, it also ignores 
existing arrangements with DR sites willing to reactivate on the basis of large load revenues that 
overcome the economic and operational dynamics that led the sites to drop-out in the first place.  
Ignoring these arrangements risks overprocurement and concomitant socialization of 
more-than-the-necessary costs to the PJM system. Second, the Stage 1 “gating criteria” for RBP 
offers do not recognize the actual commercial development pathways of distributed demand-side 
resources. If unchanged, they will function as a fence, not a gate, with the result that few if any 
demand-side resources will actually offer, subverting PJM’s intention to afford demand-side 
developers an opportunity to secure long-term price certainty to enable broad and deep new 
investment in behind-the-meter assets. 
Voltus addresses each of these two issues, and provides targeted tariff modifications to 
address them, in the sections below.

A.​ The Proposed Tariff Language Is Ambiguous as to the Eligibility of New 
Capacity at Existing Locations and the Renewed Capacity of “Deactivated” 
Locations 
​
PJM defines QRBPO resource eligibility in proposed Tariff, Attachment DD, 
section 18.2: 
(a) Qualifying RBP Offset UCAP MW is capacity that (i) has not 
previously supported a capacity commitment or been committed in an FRR Plan 
as of the commencement of the Reliability Backstop Procurement; and (ii) is 
supplied by a resource that satisfies each of the criteria set forth in Tariff, 
Attachment DD, section 18.2(b). 
 
(b) Resources Eligible to Supply Qualifying RBP Offset UCAP MW 
 
A resource eligible to supply Qualifying RBP Offset UCAP MW must 
have an expected commercial operation date, or is reasonably expected to be an 
Annual Demand Resource or DER Capacity Aggregation Resource that will be 
registered with the Office of the Interconnection and under a contract to perform 
based on PJM dispatch instructions, by no later than June 1, 2032 and be: (i) a 
new build generation resource including new resources that obtain Capacity 
Interconnection Rights transferred from deactivated resources or resources that 
have announced deactivation as of April 10, 2026; (ii) the incremental capacity 
created by an uprate of existing Generation Capacity Resources, but only to the 
extent such incremental capacity is created through an increase in such 
Generation Capacity Resource’s installed capacity and increase in Capacity 
Interconnection Rights associated with that Generation Capacity Resource; (iii) 
the incremental capacity created by the utilization of Surplus Interconnection 
Service; (iv) repowering of a deactivated generation resource that deactivated 
prior to April 10, 2026; (v) incremental capacity, whether due to greater Unforced 
Capacity or a higher level of Capacity Interconnection Rights, created by an 
existing Generation Capacity Resource’s changing fuel type to a more efficient 
fuel type; or (vi) Planned Demand Resource or Planned DER Capacity 
Aggregation Resources, as described in the RAA, Schedule 6 and Schedule 6.2.

Thus, to be a QRBPO UCAP MW, a unit of capacity must not have previously supported 
a capacity commitment or FRR Plan (18.2(a)(i)), must be expected to be operational in time for 
Delivery Year 2032/2033 (18.2(b)), and must be backed by a resource falling into any of the 
categories defined in 18.2(b)(i)-(vi).  
The first five of these categories pertain to generation/storage resources, and only the last 
covers DR/DER. On the generation/storage side, it is explicitly specified that incremental 
capacity tied to existing resources (18.2(b)(ii)-(iv)), and the “repowering of a deactivated 
generation resource” (18.2(b)(v)) may provide new capacity eligible as QRBPO if the other 
requirements of 18.2(a)-(b) are met. By contrast, the single specified category for DR/DER, 
“Planned Demand Resource or Planned DER Capacity Aggregation Resources” (18.2(b)(vi)) 
leaves open whether (1) incremental capacity at existing locations and (2) reactivations of 
locations that once did but are not currently participating in aggregations, could provide 
QRBPO-qualified capacity.   
B.​ A Logical Reading of the Proposed Tariff Language Allows Incremental 
Capacity at Existing Locations and “Reactivated” Locations for QRBPO 
Purposes 
Pulling out the pertinent 18.2(a)-(b) language, PJM has defined DR/DER-backed 
QRBPO UCAP MW as 
 
(a) . . . capacity that (i) has not previously supported a capacity commitment or 
been committed in an FRR Plan as of the commencement of the Reliability 
Backstop Procurement . . . (ii) supplied by a resource that . . . (b) . . . is reasonably 
expected to be an Annual Demand Resource or DER Capacity Aggregation 
Resource that will be registered with the Office of the Interconnection and under a 
contract to perform based on PJM dispatch instructions, by no later than June 1, 
2032 and [is] Planned Demand Resource or Planned DER Capacity Aggregation 
Resources, as described in the RAA, Schedule 6 and Schedule 6.2. 
 
The requirement of 18.2(a)(i), that the capacity must not have previously supported a 
capacity or FRR Plan commitment, cannot imply that incremental capacity at existing locations 
and the capacity contributions of reactivated locations are precluded. If it did, then incremental

capacity from generation storage resources and the repowering of deactivated generators would 
also be disqualified, nullifying the specified categories in 18.2(b)(ii)-(v).  
Nor does “Planned Demand Resource or Planned DER Capacity Aggregation Resource” 
in 18.2(b)(vi) independently limit eligibility to locations that have never before participated. In 
RAA Schedules 6 and 6.2, Planned Demand Resources and Planned DER Capacity Aggregation 
Resources sit in opposition to Existing Demand Resources5 and Existing Planned DER Capacity 
Aggregation Resources6. The categories are relevant for the reporting of existing and planned 
MW in the aggregator’s pre-auction Sell-Offer Plan that determines the maximum MW that an 
aggregator can bid into an RPM auction. An Existing Demand Resource comprises “end-use 
customer sites that are registered for the current Delivery Year with PJM” as of the time of 
Sell-Offer Plan submission. Any other site goes in as Planned Demand Resource. Similar logic 
holds for DER Capacity Aggregation Resource. Thus “planned” resources may include 
incremental capacity at existing locations and reactivated locations.  
C.​ Consistent with the Purpose of the QRBPO Construct, PJM Should Include 
Incremental Capacity at Existing Locations and the Capacity of Reactivated 
Locations as Eligible to Supply QRBPO UCAP MW 
Enabling incremental capacity at existing locations and the capacity of reactivated 
locations supports PJM’s goals, furthers the principle that large loads should cover their own 
costs, and reflects the consensus reached during the CIFP process.

First, a core function of QRBPO is to reduce the volumes that must be centrally procured 
whose costs are socialized across the system. (PJM does not propose, and may be jurisdictionally 
unable, to charge large loads directly for the costs of the RBP, and other ratepayer classes may 
end up exposed depending on retail rates, large load default risk, etc.) As such, PJM has 
“defin[ed] [the QRBPO eligibility] criterion broadly to capture all meaningful sources of new 
UCAP serving new load that contribute to resource adequacy and prevent double procuring for 
new load that is bringing new capacity to the system” (emphasis added).7 This reflects a dual 
purpose to encourage and/or enable robust bilateral contracting while ensuring such contracting 
is recognized against the RBP procurement target and associated cost allocation. Such 
recognition benefits all ratepayers by reducing the total RBP costs to which ratepayers may be 
potentially exposed. The recognition also benefits large load buyers in the private market 
because capacity they procure bilaterally, if eligible for QRBPO, reduces their exposure to the 
unknown costs of the RBP over a lengthy, fifteen-year term. Ensuring inclusive and comparable 
recognition of various sources of net-new capacity avoids tipping the scales in favor of particular 
resource classes—in this case generation/storage over DR/DER. Because uprates and 
reactivations have been included on the generation/storage side, they should equally be included 
for DR/DER-backed QRBPO. 
Second, PJM’s intention is that “[t]he RBP Resource eligibility criteria are comparatively 
more restrictive than the criteria for Qualifying RBP Offset UCAP MW.”8 PJM has explicitly 
proposed to allow asset-backed “uprates” as eligible to support RBP resources: per proposed 
Tariff, Attachment DD, section 18.5(c), an RBP Demand Resource may “[n]ot be reliant on or 
composed of registered location(s) that previously supported a Demand Resource that provided

A.​ The Gating Criteria Are Far More Stringent for DR/DER Than 
Generation/Storage, Ignore How DR/DER Resources Are Commercially 
Developed, and Are Practically Infeasible 
PJM proposes that the evaluation of RBP offers proceed in two stages, with the first stage 
designed to confirm that “the proposed resource will be successfully completed and able to 
provide capacity.”10 Sellers must present evidence “demonstrat[ing] that the resource is likely to 
be fully developed and the seller has taken all necessary steps (at this stage) to succeed” 
(emphasis added).11  
​
For generation and storage resources, the required evidence comprises “[a] critical path 
construction schedule”; “[e]vidence of 100% Site Control . . . for a term of at least three years” 
(emphasis added); financing and permitting plans; “[s]igned memoranda or other documentary 
evidence that the developer has or will procure the major equipment necessary; evidence of 
experience constructing similar projects or of past or present contracts with a partner with such 
experience; “evidence of delivery infrastructure arrangements in the form of a notice of intent or 
attestation” (emphasis added) for natural gas, where applicable; and, for resources outside of 
PJM, evidence of firm transmission arrangements up to the border of the PJM region, where such 
evidence may be “a confirmed transmission service request with completed system impact 
study” (emphasis added).12 These criteria match PJM’s intention to ensure that the seller has 
taken all necessary steps for this stage of development by recognizing that a developer several 
years ahead of expected commercial operation date (“COD”) may not have obtained all 
necessary permits and financing, may not have already purchased all major equipment, and may

The RBP is precisely an instrument designed to provide long-term price certainty to spur 
the realization of the potential capacity locked in projects currently in the planning phase, whose 
final development is uncertain. It is by design that the RBP gating criteria for storage/generation 
seek rigorous evidence of an advanced stage of project planning, but do not require evidence of 
the final investments and commitments that are prudently made only once clarity on the revenue 
stream is in place.   
​
By contrast, DR/DER RBP offers must “[i]dentify, as part of its RBP Sell Offer, for the 
full length of the fixed term, the location(s) and associated participation contract(s)”13 and will 
only be considered if they include “[e]xecuted customer agreements or binding commitments 
covering the proposed RBP UCAP MW commitment, with identified locations, customer names, 
and expected curtailment/injection capability per site” (emphasis added).14 
​
As Voltus wrote in the Executive Summary to its Stage 4 CIFP Proposal, “This [gating 
criterion] inverts the development sequence. Customer contracts are entered into against a known 
price and volume, the output of the auction. Requiring them before the auction removes the 
commercial basis on which they would be executed.” Voltus generally executes contracts with 
new suppliers—the retail energy customers providing the curtailment and/or injection 
capabilities that comprise the capacity resource—on the basis of BRA clearing prices. DR and

DER resources are aggregations, and there are practical constraints on the ability to execute 
potentially hundreds of underlying contracts by October 21.  
While Voltus does contract with the locations it aggregates for multi-year terms, those 
term lengths are far less than fifteen years and, with new locations, would generally be executed 
closer to the first year in which the location would participate and receive revenue rather than 
years ahead. It is (1) infeasible to ask retail energy customers to enter fifteen-year contracts 
without known pricing up to several years before participation is set to begin, (2) commercially 
impractical for an aggregator to commit to fixed pricing with up to thousands of suppliers prior 
to knowing even whether it has cleared the RBP, and (3) generally difficult to achieve this term 
length with a supplier whose primary use case for the energy it might curtail or the DER whose 
capabilities it might provide is something other than earning wholesale market revenues. 
Changing rules and evolving market conditions introduce uncertainty as to the “terms” of 
participation (e.g., dispatch frequency). Fifteen years is a long time for a retail energy user, who 
may be industrial, commercial, small commercial, or even residential, to commit to changing its 
home or business operations when called upon by the system operator.  
​
This does not mean that aggregators like Voltus cannot commit to fifteen-year terms. 
Rather, the function of an aggregator is to accumulate and blend the capabilities of a large 
number of individual energy users to produce a reliable resource, sometimes known as a Virtual 
or Distributed Power Plant (“VPP” or “DPP”). Underperformance of a segment of the portfolio 
may be probabilistically balanced by overperformance of another segment, and “churn” is 
preemptively accounted for. 
​
Applying this logic to a fifteen-year RBP commitment, the aggregator would be 
operationally capable of “refilling” the committed UCAP with other eligible sites when a subset

of underlying sites choose not to renew their contracts. This is in contrast to a generation or 
storage resource; if such a resource becomes unavailable or shuts down prior to the end of the 
fifteen-year commitment, the resource owner does not have the ability to straightforwardly 
replace the UCAP with other new development. Note also that RPM deficiency charges 
discipline the aggregator from bidding unrealistic volumes since RBP commitments expose the 
aggregator to significant underdelivery penalties through the RPM mechanism.  
B.​ Voltus Recommends Reasonable Gating Criteria Appropriate to the 
Pre-Auction Stage of Development 
Voltus’s recommendation on the DR/DER gating criteria address the twin concerns 
developed in the previous sub-section: (1) requiring executed contracts at the bid stage inverts 
commercial development logic, and (2) requiring fifteen-year contracts with all suppliers sets up 
an undue barrier that ignores the aggregator business model. As to the first, Voltus proposes that 
the gating criteria for DR and DER be restructured to distinguish between pre-auction criteria, 
which should reflect the investment commitment made to reach auction readiness, and 
post-auction criteria, which should reflect the execution steps that follow a successful auction 
outcome. As to the second, Voltus proposes that contracts may run for a shorter term with the 
expectation of renewal and/or replacement with other eligible net-new capabilities. Voltus 
recommends the following redlines to proposed Tariff, Attachment DD, 18.5(c)-(d), defining 
DR/DER resource eligibility:
