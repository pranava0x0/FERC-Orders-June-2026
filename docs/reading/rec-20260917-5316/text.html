P3 is baffled by this logic.   PJM should know and understand that Reliability Must Run

volume of megawatts that PJM would require to offer into the auction at zero.   It only stands to 
reason that each additional MW that is required to participate as a price taker will only drive 
more megawatts to demand out of market support or leave PJM as markets are increasingly 
undermined by regulatory interventions into a market that aspires to be competitive.  As 
resources continue to be driven from PJM’s markets, resource adequacy issues will worsen in 
PJM, and consumers will pay more overall.  If the Commission is serious about having a 
functionally competitive market in PJM that will deliver on the vision of reliability at the least

1. RMR Contracts Are Fundamentally Distinct from RBP Capacity 
 
Importantly, PJM misreads its own precedent.  For example, the case PJM cites on pages 
47 and 48 clearly limited the applicability of the RMR zero offer rule to only two BRAs.7  While, 
that ruling was extended by two years to accommodate a known transmission issue, PJM’s 
suggestion that all RMRs are required to offer as price takers for all time is simply not true based 
on PJM’s tariff or FERC orders.   
 
But even if PJM’s preferred reading of FERC’s orders and its tariff were correct, its 
reliance on RMR precedent is misplaced because RMR contracts and RBP resources serve

completely different structural functions.  RMR resources are localized, short-term, stop-gap 
measures designed to keep retiring, uneconomic generators online temporarily to resolve 
immediate transmission or operational reliability constraints.  RBP capacity is a multi-year, 
forward procurement mechanism involving long-term commitments (up to 15 years) intended to 
bring significant volumes of new generation online to satisfy steep, multi-gigawatt load growth 
(largely driven by data centers).    
Conflating short-term retention of legacy units to resolve transmission constraints with 
long-term, multi-gigawatt new entry ignores the difference in scale and market impact.   RMR 
contracts are designed to be short-term propositions for relatively small amounts of power (about 
2 GWs in the case of Brandon Shores and Wagner), RBP resources are not.   RBP resources are 
designed to be on-going and can be significantly larger, potentially over 6.8 GWs. Dumping 
potentially thousands of megawatts of long-term RBP capacity into RPM auctions as price takers 
will severely distort market-clearing signals at a regional scale, far beyond the hyper-local 
impact of a typical RMR contract – which is time bound and will result in the unit leaving the 
market.   Apples and oranges come to mind. 
2. "Two Wrongs Don't Make a Right":  RMR Precedent Is Limited and Should Not Be 
Expanded 
PJM’s primary defense for price-taker treatment rests on the Commission’s precedent 
regarding RMR resources. However, doubling down on a historically flawed policy does not 
make it just and reasonable.   P3 extensively protested PJM’s policy reversal to include RMR 
units in capacity auctions as price takers.    As P3 offered at the time, “While P3 appreciates the 
desire to address the concerns raised by state and consumer interests, if approved, the proposed 
changes will further send PJM down a road that will only prolong the successful resolution of the

issues that created the RMR in the first instance while unnecessarily compromising reliability.”8   
P3 regrets this prognostication being accurate and fears that PJM is pushing the Commission 
further down this reliability-threatening and more costly path. 
PJM itself previously recognized the market-distorting impacts of injecting zero-priced 
RMR offers into the supply stack and the long-term resource adequacy and cost impacts that 
such an action could have.  Forcing out-of-market supported resources to offer as price takers 
creates artificial supply at a $0 price point which will push existing resources out of PJM 
exacerbating the current resource adequacy shortfall and consumer cost issues.  Adopting this 
approach for RBP capacity expands a limited construct, compounding market disruption and 
undermining market efficiency and resource adequacy across the RTO.  As PJM offered prior to 
its policy reversal, “requiring participation of a deactivating unit in the capacity auction under the 
existing RMR agreements could distort the price signal and fail to incent the new build …… 
suppressing this price signal now is likely to result in greater reserve shortfalls in the future.  
Additionally, suppressing this price signal now could discourage other forms of resources, such 
as Demand Response and other resources that may be available on shorter notice from increasing 
their market participation precisely at the time they are most needed.”9    
Since PJM is clearly aware of the damage that mandated price taking capacity has on the 
markets, resource adequacy and ultimately consumer costs, it is troubling that it is asking FERC 
to yet again endorse and significantly expand a policy that is antithetical to sound market design.

4.  Long-Term Price Suppression Destroys Market Performance and Reliability 
P3 echoes and supports the arguments raised by NRG Energy and Constellation:  price-taker 
treatment for out-of-market capacity destroys the long-term integrity and performance of the 
capacity market; resource adequacy will suffer and costs will increase as a result.  
When RBP supply is forced into RPM auctions at $0/MW-day, it artificially shifts the supply 
curve to the right without a corresponding shift in auction demand, crashing RPM clearing 
prices.  The long-term ramifications of this artificial suppression are severe:  
 
Premature Exits: Depressed clearing prices prevent existing, uncontracted merchant 
generators from recovering their avoidable costs, forcing premature retirements or exits 
of needed capacity.  
 
Deterrence of Unsubsidized New Entry:  Investors will not commit merchant capital to 
new supply if the capacity market signals are systematically suppressed by multi-
gigawatt backstop procurements offered at zero.  
 
Perpetual Backstop Dependence:  By chilling competitive investment and accelerating 
retirements, PJM creates a self-fulfilling crisis where the competitive market continually 
clears short, forcing PJM to rely indefinitely on administrative backstop procurements. 
 
Resource Adequacy Issues in PJM will be Exacerbated:  Reliability will most 
certainly be compromised as existing resources are incented to exit, and new capacity

5.   A Just and Reasonable Path Forward 
Most concerning, PJM is asking FERC to take a further step down what is an 
extraordinarily slippery slope.  Resources that are receiving out of market financial support need 
to be appropriately recognized in the capacity market if the capacity market is going to remain 
the engine for reliability at the least cost for consumers in PJM.   This is the same challenge that 
was presented in MOPR where the Commission had to wrestle with the question of state 
subsidies distorting wholesale market pricing.  In the instant case, the question is how to handle 
the participation of resources that PJM is procuring to meet system load outside of the capacity 
market. 
Fortunately, unlike the MOPR, there are easy answers to this question.  For example, 
NRG and Constellation include proposed paths forward in their pleadings in this docket.  
Another simple to implement path forward would be to have RBP resources offer at their 
contract prices.  Specifically, RBP resources will have an easily identified contracted price that is 
reflective of the capacity cost necessary to support the resource.  That price will also be at or 
below a price cap set by the Commission in this proceeding.  The Commission can maintain the 
fidelity of the market by having the capacity participate in the market at its contract price.11  The 
Commission could easily direct PJM to make this change as part of its compliance filing and in

In the alternative, FERC could defer the question of RBP capacity participation in 
auctions to a future proceeding.  PJM and FERC will not know how much capacity is procured 
through the RBP process or the location of that capacity until later this year after the auction has 
been completed.  In addition, all RBP capacity can come online as late as June 1, 2032.  Finally, 
and importantly, the auction that will occur in December of this year is for the 29/30 delivery 
year which is bound by both a cap and a floor and, by PJM’s own declaration, “...there will need 
to be substantial load reduction for the auction to clear at a price lower than the ‘collar cap.’”12  
In other words, if any RBP capacity participates in the December auction, that capacity is highly 
unlikely to impact the outcome.  Given all these factors, the need to resolve the significant 
question of how best to reflect RBP capacity in capacity auctions going forward for potentially 
two decades can easily be addressed in a separate, more thoughtful, context. 
The Commission should provide PJM guidance that RBP resources are not sufficiently 
similar to the limited RMR precedent that PJM cites and direct PJM as part of its holistic 
resource adequacy market review to consider the question of how RBP capacity participates in 
capacity auctions in a way that reflects its capacity value and does not undermine the integrity of 
the market for the resources that are not receiving out of market support.  If the Commission 
does not want to defer the question to that initiative, P3 would suggest that the question be 
addressed in a paper hearing that commences after the quantity and location of RBP capacity is 
known and concludes before the BRA in May 2027 for the 29/30 delivery year.

6.   At Minimum, the Commission Must Reject PJM’s Logic 
In the event that the Commission is inclined to accept PJM’s proposal to include RBP 
capacity in the auction as price takers immediately, P3 urges the Commission to reject PJM’s 
assertion that the Commission is indifferent to the price suppression impacts and that RMR 
contracts are the same as RBP contracts.   RMR contracts and RBP commitments are 
fundamentally different propositions and should not be subject to the same regulatory structures.   
The Commission recognized the importance of limiting mandated price taking capacity in 2025 – 
it should do so again here.    
CONCLUSION 
The Commission should not accept PJM’s attempt to justify zero-dollar price-taker 
treatment for multi-gigawatt, 15-year Reliability Backstop Procurement commitments by hiding 
behind short-term Reliability Must Run precedent that is explicitly very narrow and relies on 
fundamentally broken logic.  Conflating temporary legacy unit retention with long-term new 
generation entry is not justified; it completely ignores market fundamentals and threatens to 
entrench administrative price suppression across the entire regional footprint.  By systematically 
depressing capacity clearing prices, this proposal will accelerate premature plant retirements, 
chill vital merchant investment, and trap the region in a self-fulfilling crisis of perpetual resource 
adequacy shortfalls, backstop dependence and increased overall costs.  Something the 
Commission should be highly motivated to avoid. 
The Commission must reject PJM’s proposal to treat out-of-market backstop capacity as 
zero-priced supply and preserve the long-term integrity of the wholesale market.  At minimum, 
FERC should require RBP capacity as suggested by NRG or Constellation or to offer at its actual 
contract price, sending a clear signal that out-of-market revenues cannot be leveraged to
