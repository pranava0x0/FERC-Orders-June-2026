1. PJM’s Existing Tariff May Be Unjust and Unreasonable
We preliminarily find that PJM’s existing Tariff may be unjust and unreasonable because the existing reliability backstop provisions appear to be insufficient to prevent the grave resource adequacy concerns that led PJM to file the instant proposal in the first place.  We think it clear that PJM’s ordinary instrument for procuring sufficient capacity to meet PJM’s reliability requirement, the RPM, has failed to keep pace with the unprecedented influx of large load into the PJM Region in recent years. Like offset capacity, RBP Resources (i.e., resources that bid into the RBP auction) must have never cleared in an RPM Auction and must have an expected in-service date by June 1, 2032.42  PJM, however, states that because RBP Resources have longer-term commitments and price certainty—as compared to resources that provide offset capacity—PJM proposes more restrictive criteria for RBP Resources to limit them to those resources that have demonstrated a high likelihood of success in reaching commercial operation in the near term and remaining available throughout the commitment period.  Thus, some categories of resources eligible to be offset capacity cannot be RBP Resources, including resources that increase UCAP by switching fuels and ELCC class but do not bring on new ICAP.  Similarly, any incremental capacity created by new Capacity Interconnection Rights (CIRs) must also be associated with an increase in ICAP. (d) PJM’s Proposal May Be Unjust and
Unreasonable
For purposes of determining whether a proposed cost allocation method is just and reasonable, both “[the Commission] and federal courts have long understood the [FPA’s] just-and-reasonable requirement ‘to incorporate a cost-causation principle.’”249  Under the cost causation principle, all Commission-jurisdictional rates and charges must “reflect to some degree the costs actually caused by the customer who must pay for them,”250 and costs must be allocated in a manner that is at least roughly commensurate with benefits.251  Compliance with this principle is evaluated by “comparing the costs assessed against a party to the burdens imposed or benefits drawn by that party.”252  Courts and the Commission have acknowledged that adherence to the cost causation principle does not require that cost allocation methods balance costs and benefits with “exacting precision.”253  Instead, the Commission may approve a given cost allocation framework if it “has an articulable and plausible reason to believe that the benefits are at least roughly commensurate with” the assigned costs.254  Consistent with this longstanding precedent, we apply the cost causation principle to evaluate PJM’s proposed method for allocating RBP costs.

ROSNER, Commissioner, concurring:
I concur in today’s order because, at its core, our responsibility to the 67 million people in PJM is to keep the lights on.  While the record reflects a wide range of views on how best to address the emerging reliability challenges, the downside risk of inaction far outweighs the risk of an imperfect solution.  It is not a close call.

The limited set of guidance we give PJM to revise its proposal will enable PJM to assign costs to those responsible (i.e. to the zones with data center growth) for the resource adequacy shortfall, consistent with cost causation principles, and it is my hope that PJM will act on this guidance swiftly so that needed generation may be procured. Many of the issues driving this filing extend beyond PJM’s jurisdiction to solve alone.  States and retail authorities must play an active role – particularly in establishing large-load retail tariffs, enforcing collateral requirements, and establishing clear rules for which customers should be curtailed first when circumstances require it (Hint: homes, hospitals, and schools should never be curtailed before data centers). In the longer term PJM and its stakeholders must return to market fundamentals. Load and supply should have strong incentives to hedge, manage their risks, and procure the capacity they rely upon.  Market discipline – not ad hoc backstops – must again become the primary safeguard for reliability.
