II. DESCRIPTION OF CO-LOCATION PROJECT OPTIONS

8. As a general matter, co-located loads rely on direct connection to co-located generation facilities for the delivery of energy without reliance on grid transmission facilities or Network Integration Transmission Service (“NITS”).

9. A number of the PJM options, however, contemplate continued connection of the load to the grid, even following the loss of the co-located generating facility. These include PJM Options 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, and 8. In those cases, the co-located load will rely on the grid and NITS for energy.

10. In contrast, in PJM Option 4, the co-located load will not be able to draw energy from the grid. Instead, fully redundant relaying will trip the co-located load in the event of (i) the unplanned loss of the co-located generation or (ii) a reduction in generation level, thereby preventing the co-located load from being able to draw energy from the grid.  In such configurations, no energy will flow under any circumstances from the co-located generation facilities over grid transmission to the co-located load and, therefore, no grid transmission service is used by or required by the co-located load. III. PROTECTIVE RELAY SCHEMES
21. As discussed, Options 4 and 5 will require protective relay schemes that will prevent load from drawing energy from the grid. These protective relay schemes can be designed so that there are completely redundant primary and backup protective schemes to perform this function.

PDF page 9

7

22. Such a configuration uses electric current transformers to measure the flow of power from between the co-located load and the grid, which is communicated with relays via fiber optic channels that will trip behind-the-meter circuit breakers associated with the co-located load when the electric current transformers sense the power flow from the grid to the co-located load. This can occur in the event of the sudden loss of the co-located generating unit or following a reduction in the level of energy produced by the co-located generating unit so that it is not fully supplying the co-located load.  There are two circuit breakers placed in series supplying the co- located load, so that if one fails, the second will still open the circuit. Each circuit breaker has two trip coils such that either the primary or backup protective relay can successfully trip the load. All elements of the protective scheme, including the electric current transformers, the fiber channels, and the trip relays, are redundant resulting in a single-failure-proof design.  The protective scheme referred to above in Figure 1 will trip the co-located load from the grid in three cycles (0.05 seconds) following a trip of the co-located generating unit or after sensing that the energy produced by the co-located generating unit has been reduced to a level such that the co-located load is attempting to draw energy from the grid.

23. PJM raises the concern that “[t]he challenge with an arrangement where there is load located behind a generator Point of Interconnection lies in how such a configuration or special protection scheme could be designed to never fail . . . .”2  PJM plans for N-1 conditions followed by a single protection system failure, such as a stuck breaker, and requires transmission system upgrades when such events result in violations of applicable NERC standards. In this instance, a stuck breaker becomes a second contingency. In most instances, the solution to stuck breaker criteria violations is to add a redundant series breaker, making the failure of both breakers

following an N-1 event a third contingency. PJM does not plan for or require upgrades for such third contingencies, nor would it be reasonable to do so, short of events that would result in a cascading outage of grid facilities. The protection system, discussed above, is fully redundant and, while it cannot be guaranteed to never fail, it is unreasonable to treat the failure of both primary and secondary relay system facilities following the loss of a generator as anything other than a third contingency.  In fact, when I was directing transmission planning for PJM, I cannot think of an instance in which we treated such a situation otherwise.  In practice, protection schemes are regularly employed on the PJM transmission system, and the successful operation of such protective systems is a common observance within PJM.  There is no technical reason from my perspective to expect that protective relaying in the context of co-location would perform any less reliably.  If anything, suggesting that protective relaying – a common and technically sound tool for managing the grid – cannot be used in the context of co-located configurations or must be designed to a different standard strikes me as an unduly discriminatory position.

26. In PJM’s Option 1, a new Network Load would be fully dependent on NITS for delivery of capacity and energy, would be included in the PJM load forecast, and would be modeled and planned for in future RTEP cycles. This is the manner in which the vast majority of load is connected to the PJM grid.

27. In PJM’s Option 2, a new load and the co-located generator are separately metered. The load is considered Network Load and the generator is a capacity resource. While energy will flow directly from the generator (when it is in service) to the load without flowing over the facilities of the PJM grid, the load would be dependent on NITS for delivery of capacity and energy at some times, would be included in the PJM load forecast, and would be modeled and planned for in future RTEP cycles. The dependence of the load on NITS covers all circumstances, whether the generator is in or out of service.

28. PJM’s Option 3 is based on the existing BTMG construct and can involve two configurations where load and generation can be co-located or connected via the distribution system of a municipal utility.  The existing PJM Tariff bills for transmission and related services based on the netting of such load with its BTMG.

29. In PJM’s Option 4, a new load is co-located behind a generating resource. As shown in Figure 1 above, the connection is radial, behind the Point of Interconnection of the generator. Therefore, energy can only flow directly from the co-located generator’s grid voltage substations to the behind-the-meter co-location project’s substations (at the same grid voltage level) and, from there, over step-down transformers to the co-located load connected at 34.5 kV. There is no

other transmission path from the co-located generation to the co-located load, i.e., there is no parallel path for energy to flow out from the co-located generator onto the transmission grid and back, eventually, to the co-located load.  Because the associated special-purpose relay scheme will remove the co-located load from the grid in the event of a sudden loss of the generating facility, no energy will flow to the co-located load over the transmission grid following a contingency; no new transmission infrastructure will be required on the grid to facilitate the interconnection and to ensure reliability for existing load customers other than what might be identified in the Necessary Study performed to determine whether the generator’s modification to serve the behind-the-meter load has any reliability impact, and no NITS service will be provided by the grid to the co-located load. Excess energy, beyond the level required by the load, will flow from the generator to the grid. Accordingly, the load is not dependent on NITS for delivery of capacity and energy, would not be included in the PJM load forecast, and would not be modeled and planned for in future RTEP cycles.

30. In PJM’s Option 5, a new load is co-located behind a generating resource in the same manner as Option 4. As shown in Figure 1 above, the connection is radial, behind the Point of Interconnection of the generator and, therefore, energy is delivered from the generator directly to the load without flowing on any grid transmission facilities. Excess energy, beyond the level required by the load, will flow from the generator to the grid. Protection mechanisms are in place to prevent the delivery of energy from the grid to the load in the event that the generator is offline. The significant difference with Option 5 is that the load can take back-up service from PJM with advance authorization. The load is not dependent on NITS for delivery of capacity and energy while the primary generation source is in service, but may rely on transmission facilities of the PJM grid for back-up service, depending on the electrical location of the back-up

31. With respect to PJM Option 4 and PJM Option 5, PJM notes that co-location
arrangements may involve the sale of power from generators to intermediaries and then to load under certain circumstances.4 This, however, is a contractual difference, not a physical, electrical, or interconnection design-based difference. The delivery of energy from generation to load in co-location arrangements such as PJM’s Option 4 and Option 5 does not involve the flow of that energy over the transmission facilities of the grid. This fact is a function of the design of the interconnection between the co-located load and generation and is not impacted by the ownership of the generation facility or the co-located load or any contractual relationship between those parties. BTMG arrangements and co-located load arrangements can be designed from an engineering perspective in exactly the same manner. In one, the load is typically greater in magnitude than the generating facility. In the other, the load is typically lesser in magnitude than the generating facility. The physics of energy delivery are the same in both arrangements and are not related to contractual arrangements or rate structures.

It could be implemented as a transitional service while required transmission system upgrades are under construction.

b. Synchronization
33. Comparing PJM’s Option 4 and PJM’s Option 5 with the BTMG construct (the basis for PJM Option 3), clearly both arrangements involve load and generation that is synchronized to the grid. However, synchronization does not require or suggest that energy delivery from that generation to that load flows on the transmission facilities of the grid. Synchronization is clearly important and even necessary to the stability of the generating facility and the ability of the generating facility to deliver energy to the co-located load on an on-going basis, but that does not change the fact that the energy does not flow on the transmission facilities of the grid.

34. Whether synchronization could be conceptualized and designated as an ancillary service, one thing is clear: it is not an ancillary service within PJM. The ability to provide other ancillary services, if they are required or used, is facilitated by synchronization, but one is not the equivalent of the other. Synchronization may allow black start service to be provided, but if black start service is not required or used by a co-located load, the fact that the load is synchronized to the grid does not make the service required or used. In response to question b.vi., PJM states that “[s]ynchronizing to the grid means the generator matches the voltage, frequency, and phase of the entire grid (including all load connected to the system) before connecting to the grid in order to facilitate safe and stable power transfer. As noted above, co- located load is connected to and synchronized with the multi-state bulk power system. At bare minimum, it is benefiting from ancillary services.”5 Load benefits from being synchronized to the grid, but not from services that it does not use. One standard on which charges for grid services can be properly based is use of those services. This applies to both transmission service and ancillary services. Synchronization, alone, should not be the standard for such charges.

c. Other Grid Services
I have previously analyzed grid services in connection with a co-located load configuration that, while synchronized to the grid, provides for disconnection from the grid when the co-located generation facility is unavailable.  In short, my prior analysis shows that co- located load configurations that include sufficiently redundant protective relay systems, e.g., configurations consistent with PJM’s Options 4 and 5, prevent the physical flow of power to the co-located load and do not use transmission service from the grid.6  Moreover, my prior analysis shows that these configurations do not impose burdens on the PJM system that would require the procurement of additional amounts of ancillary services.7

35. Further, the PJM Tariff, which establishes the procedures for calculating charges for services provided in PJM markets, dictates for all of the services discussed herein, that the obligation of a behind-the-meter co-located load is based on the amount of such load, netted by the generation that is operating behind-the-meter.

36. My presentation here is an engineering analysis that, among other things, considers whether non-grid connected co-location configurations consistent with PJM Options 4 and 5 impose additional burdens on the PJM grid.  I note that I do not take a position here, nor did I in my prior analysis, whether the co-located load under the non-grid connected configurations of PJM Options 4 and 5 may nevertheless benefit indirectly by virtue of synchronization through the host generator’s interconnection to the grid. To date, however, FERC has not allocated charges for any such synchronization benefits.
