Pre-Technical Conference Statement 
Jacob Finkel, Office of Governor Josh Shapiro, Deputy Secretary of Policy 
 
 
Thank you, Chairman Swett and Commissioners Rosner, See, Chang, and LaCerte, for 
convening this important conversation on the future of PJM.  On behalf of the PJM Governors’ 
Collaborative,1 I was jointly nominated to this panel by Delaware Governor Matt Meyer, District 
of Columbia Mayor Muriel Bowser, Illinois Governor JB Pritzker, Indiana Governor Mike Braun, 
Maryland Governor Wes Moore, New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill, North Carolina Governor 
Josh Stein, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro, and Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger. 
 
This Technical Conference is necessary because of a fundamental gap that has emerged 
between those who are accountable for reliability and affordability in our region—the states and 
PJM’s Board of Managers—and those with significant authority over many of the solutions: the 
private Members.  Those Members have a responsibility to pursue their own financial interests, 
and to their credit they do so zealously, but their collective input has failed to produce optimal, or 
often even coherent, direction at a time when clear, principled leadership is urgently needed.  This 
failure matters far beyond the halls of Valley Forge, because the outcomes at PJM deeply affect 
the economic development of the PJM states and, even more importantly, if PJM stumbles, it 
directly threatens the health and safety of 67 million Americans.

To address this, PJM’s governance structure—which has not been substantially revisited 
in the three decades since it became an ISO—must be updated to realign authority with 
accountability. 
First, PJM’s Board must be given the independence from its stakeholders necessary to 
enable clear and decisive action—and be charged with a renewed duty to act solely on behalf of 
the common interest, not the private interest of any Member—while the stakeholder process is 
reformed to become more nimble and more appropriately focused on issues where in-depth 
stakeholder engagement lends itself to positive outcomes. 
Second, the governance structure needs to incorporate meaningful input from PJM’s 
most elemental regulatory partners, the PJM states, who have ultimate responsibility over 
resource adequacy and transmission siting in our region.   
Without these two elements, we have repeatedly seen PJM struggle to craft durable 
solutions to the growing resource adequacy challenges our region faces.  Governance solutions 
cannot themselves deliver resource adequacy, but more effective governance is a necessary first 
step to the development of suitable market reforms. 
The PJM stakeholder process does serve to bring those with expertise and real-world 
understanding of how individual businesses and sectors work together for shared discussion.  
That input is necessary and it needs to continue in the right context.  However, the stakeholder 
process should not be expected to perform an executive role it cannot effectively serve.  Its 
complexity—which has grown to Byzantine proportions over the last three decades—renders it 
incapable of being an effective decision-making body for an organization that must tackle fast-
moving challenges. 
 This should not be a surprise: no one should expect a diverse group of 1,146 self-interested 
Members to make decisions rapidly or boldly.  That is why it is impossible to imagine any public

corporation using such a process to make vital decisions.  Simply put, such leadership should be 
the job of PJM’s Board2 and, in some areas of shared responsibility, of the PJM states. 
This does not require wholesale elimination of the Members’ input, but rather, better

determine PJM’s strategic direction, but should help to perfect the solutions that the PJM Board 
and the states identify are necessary.  That realignment of authority and accountability is critical, 
because the private pecuniary interest of many Members will necessarily differ from the broader 
public interest that PJM, as a creation of this Commission, should be expected to deeply respect.3  
Tracing back to Order 888, PJM’s raison d'être is emphatically not to maximize the profits of its 
individual stakeholders.4  The core mission of PJM, and every RTO, remains “to promote 
efficiency in wholesale electricity markets and to ensure that electricity consumers pay the lowest 
price possible for reliable service.”5  
To effectuate this necessary realignment in leadership, the PJM Governors’ Collaborative 
(“Collaborative”) supports increasing the independence of PJM’s Board, making common-sense 
improvements to the mechanics of the stakeholder process, and providing the states with a 
meaningful role in decisions that affect areas of shared responsibility.   
The Collaborative has worked closely with our State Commission colleagues in the

reform.  Any set of solutions must include a strong and impactful role for both the Governors as

Proposal for Reform 
Competitive markets, designed properly, deliver real value to customers.  But experience 
in recent years has shown repeatedly that PJM’s current governance process is failing to deliver 
on that promise.  As already noted, improved governance will not by itself stabilize confidence in 
PJM’s markets, nor will it spontaneously resolve the resource adequacy challenges we must 
collectively address in short order.  But it would be self-defeating to attempt to modify those 
markets without first addressing the governance shortcomings that led us to this juncture.   
For that reason, the Collaborative strongly supports this Commission’s focus on 
governance, not as the end goal, but as a necessary first step to ultimately improving market 
confidence and function.  Two interconnected governance reforms are necessary: PJM must be 
empowered to act more decisively and the states must have a larger role in reaching decisions of 
shared importance.  
 
1: Empowering PJM to Lead 
Today, PJM faces challenging real-world conditions, including from large load additions, 
yet the PJM Board has too often been unwilling to act decisively, even in areas where it possesses 
the authority to do so.  In order for the Board to provide effective leadership, its independence

must be bolstered, the transparency of its decisions improved, and the function of the stakeholder 
process streamlined and more clearly defined. 
• 
The Board’s independence must be safeguarded, while becoming more 
accessible and transparent.   
1. The Board members’ terms should be extended to five or more years, with service 
normally capped at ten or less years.  This is needed to insulate Board members 
from considerations of retention when weighing difficult decisions. 
2. Today strict limits exist on whom the Board may communicate with, including 
restricting conversations with the states, making PJM staff the Board’s primary 
source of information.  Those well-meaning restrictions serve in practice to 
hamper the Board’s engagement with a wider range of perspectives.  The 
appearance and reality of undue influence can be avoided, as it is in other RTOs, 
without such stringent limitations.  The Board should have more regular and 
structured contact with Members and other stakeholders, avoiding backroom 
conversations while gaining more in-depth understanding from all sides. 
3. That increased contact should be accompanied by improved transparency, 
including full reporting and disclosure of any stakeholder meetings, Board 
meetings being opened to the public, published Board votes, and minutes 
containing the Board’s decision-making rationale for key votes.  
4. There should be a preference, but not a requirement, that Board members reside 
within the PJM region, adjusting conflict of interest rules to encourage the 
selection of qualified Board members who have direct experience with the 
consequences of decisions facing PJM.  At the same time, new conflict restrictions 
should bar Board members from seeking employment with PJM Members for at 
least one year following their term on the Board.

5. Section 7.2 of the Operating Agreement delineates seven categories of required 
experience for Board members.  Those limited requirements—narrowly focused on 
specific financial, technical, and professional expertise—no longer matches the 
scope of PJM’s responsibilities.  This represents a blind spot in the Board’s outlook 
and undermines its independence by unduly prioritizing Board members with 
experience that matches the interests of the private Members.  These requirements 
should be updated to require at least one Board member with past experience in 
public service and at least one with direct exposure to the impact of RTO decisions 
on the 67 million customers PJM serves. 
6. PJM benefits from excellent in-house counsel, but to maximize independence, the 
Board should retain its own non-conflicted counsel.  The Board should entrust that 
counsel with any negotiations that affect its authority, including with the Members 
and the states. 
7. Finally, alongside these measures to bolster its independence, the Board’s duty 
should be clearly defined for the first time.  The Board should be expressly charged 
with acting to benefit the public interest of the region as a whole—as the Board 
chooses to conceive of it—not to advance the private interest of any individual 
Member. 
• 
The stakeholder process should be reformed to be more effective and 
efficient in providing clear input to the Board.  This means ensuring that 
voting rules provide a consistent neutral framework throughout the process by using 
sector-weighted voting throughout the stakeholder process and eliminating affiliate 
voting at the lower level.  Voting thresholds should be reduced to encourage more ways 
to get to “Yes.”  Alongside these improvements, voting on all matters of substance 
throughout the process—including at the lower-level committees—should be entirely

2: A Meaningful State Role 
The Collaborative embraces the need, as PJM CEO David Mills recently publicly 
requested, for states to assume more responsibility for resource adequacy decisions, and 
accordingly for those decisions to be shared to a greater degree between the states and PJM 
going forward.6  For this deeper collaboration to occur successfully—and its success is vital to the 
entire region and all market participants—the states must first be given real authority in the PJM 
process that accords with their unique role as sovereign partners.  Specifically:  
• 
For matters of shared responsibility, PJM should agree to file an alternative 
state proposal when the RTO intends to submit a 205 filing.  In every other multi-
state RTO (i.e., ISO-NE, MISO, SPP), states have means to influence aspects of shared 
jurisdiction through the exercise of the RTO’s 205 filing rights under certain 
circumstances.  In order to build a durable partnership with the states on issues of shared 
responsibility, PJM should agree to utilize the rights it holds under section 205 of the 
Federal Power Act when a supermajority of OPSI members prefer an alternative to a 
proposal that PJM intends to file.  In those cases, PJM should voluntarily file that OPSI 
proposal alongside its own (a so-called “jump ball” filing).  This jump-ball capability is a 
vital tool to foster a more collaborative process between the states and their RTO.  
Experience in each of PJM’s sister multi-state RTOs has shown that such arrangements 
are effective in producing more collaborative state/RTO partnerships and that, in practice, 
alternative filings are rarely necessary because state input is afforded respect as RTO

PJM’s tariff should recognize and fund the PJM Governors’ Collaborative, 
representing the 13 governors and the Mayor of the District of Columbia.  The 
Governors’ proper role should be big-picture: working with the Board to establish the 
long-term strategic direction of the RTO, communicating preferences that serve the public 
interest, and encouraging action, agreement, and forward progress, not to block 
ideas.  This complements the technical expertise of OPSI’s utility regulators—making 
OPSI the best-suited state entity to exercise jump-ball filing rights—and the more 
narrowly focused residential ratepayer advocacy of the Consumer Advocates of the PJM 
States.  The Collaborative must be provided sufficient funding in the PJM tariff to serve 
three primary functions. 
1. Develop and bring problem statements and issue charges directly to the PJM Board, 
avoiding the need to receive supermajority support from PJM Members to initiate 
stakeholder processes on issues that are important to the states.  If the Board elects to 
accept an idea, it may be developed through a standard or expedited process like the 
Critical Issue Fast Path (CIFP). 
2. On occasions when the Members are unable to reach consensus and no stakeholder 
proposal receives a supermajority vote in the Members Committee, the Board may 
solicit a timely indicative vote by the Collaborative.  This would be a non-binding 
expression of the states’ preferences on a given subject—not limited to the areas where 
OPSI would hold jump-ball filing rights, as described above—and would be offered to 
the Board at the Collaborative’s discretion in those instances when Members cannot 
agree.

Conclusion 
The challenges that PJM faces today cannot be solved by the RTO or the Members 
alone.  Both have crucial roles to play, but only by working collaboratively with the states can 
these issues be coherently addressed.  Simply put, durable solutions are impossible without 
better alignment of authority with accountability, which means a more independent Board, a 
more nimble stakeholder process directed at the right type of engagement, and greater 
involvement from the states in PJM’s decision-making processes.  Such durable solutions are 
needed, and needed quickly. 
The Governors’ Collaborative seeks near-term action to address these issues so that we 
can collectively move forward in improving the resource adequacy situation of our region.  To 
effectuate that, the Collaborative looks forward to engaging vigorously with the Commission, the 
Members, and PJM itself in this important conversation in the days ahead.
