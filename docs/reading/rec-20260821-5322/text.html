A. RBP Overview
As proposed by PJM, the RBP is a one-time capacity procurement targeting an Unforced Capacity (“UCAP”) shortfall identified in the 2027/2028 and 2028/2029 RPM Capacity Market Auctions. A. The PJM RBP Proposal is Not Just and Reasonable.
a. PJM’s RBP Proposal is Unjust and Unreasonable Because the Resulting
Rate Impact is Uncertain.
Under section 205 of the FPA, rates must be “just and reasonable.”67 Section 205(c) of the FPA “requires rate filings to recite ‘the … practices … affecting such rates and charges.’” The Commission’s regulations also require rate schedules to set forth “clearly and specifically … the classifications, practices, rules and regulations affecting such rates, charges, classifications, services … ”68 The PJM RBP Proposal does not provide sufficiently bounded and causation-based inputs for determining which LSE is responsible for RBP charges. The PJM RBP Proposal does not explain how qualifying offsets or opt-outs follow the responsible LSE such that its allocation appropriately can be calibrated or reduced to zero. Nor does it make clear when collateral obligations attach. Relying on the Electric Distributor and RERRA to make determinations or otherwise effectuate downstream processes does not replace the need to ensure the appropriate wholesale entity is allocated the costs. Therefore, the cost allocation put forward as part of the PJM RBP Proposal fails to meet this standard, and is not just and reasonable.

The Commission has rejected proposed cost allocations when the proposals, like the PJM RBP Proposal here, lack specificity or do not allow impacted customers to be able to estimate the cost impact. In California Power Exchange Corp., for example, the Commission rejected the cost allocation plan of the California Power Exchange Corporation (“PX”) for its Block-Forward Market because the plan “lack[ed] specificity with regard to items which will not be directly assigned” and “ha[d] not linked the allocation methodologies to particular costs to be allocated.”69

Collateral requirements are just and reasonable only when the underlying obligation is sufficiently knowable, measurable, and attributable to the responsible party before collateral is required. Here, however, an LSE’s RBO MW and resulting RBP Charges may depend on subsequent Electric Distributor reporting, state or RERRA allocation determinations, PJM’s treatment of offsets and opt-outs, and the potential application of default Daily Unforced Capacity Obligation allocations. III.  THE ROLE OF COLLATERAL
10. This section establishes the standards that govern collateral and margining in commercial and organized markets. Those standards are not matters of individual judgment. They are set out in published guidance issued by the Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures of the Bank for International Settlements together with the International Organization of Securities Commissions, adopted by the Financial Stability Board as one of twelve key standards essential to financial stability, and, for the organized wholesale electric markets specifically, in the orders and credit policy of this Commission. PJM’s own Attachment Q is constructed on the same architecture. I set out those standards here so that the proposed RBP credit requirement can be measured against them in the sections that follow.

11. Collateral secures replacement cost, not the contract. It is a risk instrument, not a revenue instrument. The governing international standard defines the exposure being secured as replacement-cost risk — “the risk of loss of unrealised gains on unsettled transactions with a counterparty,” where “the resulting exposure is the cost of replacing the original transaction at current market prices.” CPSS-IOSCO, Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (April 2012) (“PFMI”) ¶ 2.5.

12. Collateral is accordingly held “to cover current and potential future exposures.” PFMI ¶ 1.13. Principle 4 requires that credit exposures be effectively measured, monitored and managed, and Principle 6 requires that a margin system be “risk-based and regularly reviewed.”

Because replacement price moves with the market, margin levels must be “commensurate with the risks and particular attributes of each product, portfolio, and market” the infrastructure serves. 28. RBP Sellers submit Sell Offers, receive commitments for a fixed fifteen-year term, and post collateral under section 18.8(a) equal to 20% of the offer price times committed UCAP MW times 365 days, for the first three Delivery Years.

29. A seller that fails to achieve commercial operation is subject to RBP shortfall charges under section 18.6(d), assessed daily on the full committed UCAP at a rate of 20% of the commitment price, for up to three years. 30. Under section 18.6(e), if committed MW fail to come online for three Delivery Years the commitment is rescinded for the remainder of the fifteen-year term. The resource is then neither compensated nor subject to further shortfall charges. Seller exposure is therefore bounded at three years of shortfall charges, and the seller’s collateral is sized to that same quantity. That administrative cap on the seller’s assessed liability is distinct from the economic replacement exposure experienced by load when the capacity does not arrive, which I address at paragraphs 90 to 95.

31. Load Serving Entities allocated RBP Charges pay the contract-for-differences settlement for the full fifteen-year term and post collateral under section 18.8(b) at $1.5 million per RBP UCAP MW. Only LSEs in zones with a non-zero Load Adjustment are allocated RBP Charges.

B.  Parties without obligations
33. Electric Distributors determine the allocation of RBP Obligation MW among LSEs within their zone or area. Proposed Reliability Assurance Agreement, section 7.2C. They incur no payment obligation and post no collateral.

C.  The residual tier
34. Under Operating Agreement section 15.2.2, where a Member defaults on its payment obligations the other PJM Members may be required to pay a share of the defaulted amounts. That exposure extends to Members with no Load Adjustment and no RBP Charge allocation.

D.  How a default travels
35. On an LSE default, section 18.9(a) provides that the defaulted RBP Charge “will be allocated to all remaining Load Serving Entities responsible for paying RBP Charges based on their pro-rata share,” and that such amounts “may be offset by the RBP Collateral posted by the defaulting Load Serving Entity.” Reallocation is mandatory; application of the defaulter’s collateral is permissive.

36. On a seller default, section 18.9(b) provides that where an RBP Resource defaults on payment of shortfall charges, “the corresponding credits will not be allocated to the Load Serving Entities that would otherwise receive the RBP shortfall credits.” Load bears the loss directly.

37. The two provisions are asymmetric in effect. A load default is recovered from other load. A seller default leaves load uncompensated, and after rescission under section 18.6(e) load receives nothing for the remaining twelve years of a fifteen-year commitment.

38. Under section 18.6(f), a commitment follows the resource on a change of ownership and continues through the end of the fixed term. E.  Which party is harmed by which failure
39. The RBP has three distinct failure modes, and they harm different parties at different times. The first is a failure by a Load Serving Entity to post the required collateral on the stated date, which is a liquidity event occurring forty-five days before the Delivery Year begins. The second is a failure by a Load Serving Entity to pay an RBP Charge, which is a credit event arising during the Delivery Year. The third is non-delivery by an RBP Seller, which is a physical event. The first two are both financial but are not the same: they are triggered by different conditions, arise on different dates, and are secured differently. Collateral secures the second. It does not secure the first, because the first is a failure to provide the collateral itself. Collateral is ordinarily sized to the loss it prevents, so these distinctions are prior to any judgment about the amount of either requirement.

40. A payment failure by an LSE does not affect whether capacity is delivered. The committed resource remains committed and the procurement proceeds. What occurs is a reallocation. Under section 18.9(a) the defaulted RBP Charge is allocated among the remaining Load Serving Entities responsible for paying RBP Charges; because RBP Charges are assessed against every zone/area holding a share of the RBP Target, that population is footprint-wide rather than confined to the zone in which the default occurred. Under Operating Agreement section 15.2.2 any residual reaches the wider membership. The parties harmed are therefore other load. Within a zone/area, the Electric Distributor assigns RBP Obligation MW in the first instance to the Load Serving Entities serving the Large Loads whose forecast growth generated the Load Adjustment; where it does not, section 18.8(b)(i)(B)(2) reaches load on Peak Load Contribution, which is native load. The Large Loads are not themselves parties to the RBP and bear no share of the reallocation under section 18.9(a).

41. Non-delivery by an RBP Seller has the opposite incidence. The capacity for which the procurement was conducted does not arrive, and the reliability the RBP was designed to secure is not obtained. That harm falls on the loads the procurement was designed to serve. PJM has described a framework under which RBP supply matched to specific Large Loads would be credited as a Bring Your Own Capacity showing for Interim Resource Adequacy Service purposes, but the filed provisions establish no mechanism by which an RBP commitment is matched to a particular Large Load or Load Serving Entity, and I express no view on how such a credit would be determined. The harm falls on load generally, both through the resource adequacy shortfall and financially: under section 18.9(b) load does not receive the shortfall credits, and after rescission under section 18.6(e) load receives nothing for the remaining twelve years of a fifteen- year commitment.

42. Neither the tariff nor the filing quantifies the second of those harms. Shortfall charges under section 18.6(d) are assessed for up to three years at a rate fixed by reference to the commitment price; they are not a measure of the cost of replacing the capacity that did not arrive. PJM identified the same gap in its own analysis, stating that a default in year three of a fifteen-year contract leaves twelve years of capital cost uncovered, and that the current tariff does not calculate liquidated damages. I address the consequences of that gap in Sections VII and IX.

VI.  OPINION 1 — The magnitude has no precedent in PJM
61. This opinion addresses magnitude. The Commission’s standard has two sides: an RTO must minimize the risk that a default is socialized among non-defaulting participants, while “ensuring that the credit or collateral requirements are not so stringent that they unnecessarily inhibit access to the marketplace.” Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 126 FERC ¶ 61,153, at P 19 (2009). Magnitude is the measure of the second. In what follows I compare the proposed requirement to the collateral PJM actually holds, to the relationship PJM maintains between collateral and market activity, and to the annual charge the requirement secures. On each comparison the proposed requirement stands apart from anything in PJM’s experience.

VII.  WHETHER THE REQUIREMENT SECURES A MEASURED EXPOSURE
71. This section evaluates whether the proposed collateral secures a measured risk position, and whether the amount is sufficient or deficient under plausible future price outcomes. The standards set out in Section III require that collateral be sized to replacement cost, which is a function of the price at which the position would be replaced and the period over which the exposure is held; that the requirement be recalculated as exposure moves and released as it declines; and that a participant be able to determine what the requirement will cost it. I apply those elements here.

A.  The requirement is absent during the period of greatest uncertainty

X.  THE COST OF CARRYING THE REQUIREMENT IS MATERIAL AND IS NOT
RECOVERED
122.
This section addresses the cost to a Load Serving Entity of holding the required collateral. That cost is not a charge under the tariff and appears in no figure PJM has presented. It is nonetheless a real cost, it is borne by load, and it is a direct consequence of the amount and form of collateral PJM has proposed.

123.
The analysis in this section rests on an assumption that I state expressly. It assumes that the Load Serving Entity is able to fund the required amount — from balance sheet cash, from committed credit capacity, or by incurring debt — and it measures only the cost of doing so. It does not address whether an entity of a given size can obtain funding of the required magnitude at all, and it does not attempt to quantify the effect on an entity’s credit standing of assuming an obligation of this size. Those questions are distinct from the carrying cost and I address them separately in Section XI, where I examine the financial capacity of one investment-grade Load Serving Entity against the allocation PJM’s illustrative figures would assign to it. The figures in this section are therefore a lower bound: they represent the cost to an entity for which funding is available on the terms assumed.

A.  Only two forms of collateral are permitted
124.
RBP Collateral is defined as “either (1) a cash deposit, including any interest thereon, or (2) a letter of credit issued for the benefit of PJM or PJMSettlement.” Surety bonds are excluded, although PJM accepts them elsewhere subject to per-issuer limits, and section 18.8 provides no unsecured credit allowance. An LSE therefore has two options, and the cost of the requirement is the cost of whichever it elects.

B.  Cash
125.
An LSE posting cash must fund the amount. The cost is its own cost of funds, less whatever return the deposit earns. Attachment Q provides that interest on cash accrues to the benefit of the Participant, without specifying a rate. PJM’s audited financial statements for 2025 indicate a credited return of approximately 3.8%, below the effective federal funds rate for the same period, and interest is credited to a member account periodically rather than continuously. 126.

An LSE that posts cash therefore bears the difference between its cost of funds and that credited return. For an investment-grade load-serving entity with a cost of funds in the range of 5% to 6%

— the range addressed at paragraph 130 — the net cost of posting cash is therefore approximately 120 to 220 basis points on the amount posted. A lower-rated entity would bear a higher cost. I do not use the 9.5% after-tax weighted average cost of capital PJM applied in deriving the Credit Rate. As explained at paragraphs 129 through 132, that figure describes the cost of capital for merchant generation investment, not the cost of funds of the load-serving entities required to post the collateral.

C.  Letter of credit
127.
A letter of credit does not require the LSE to fund the amount, but it consumes committed credit capacity and is priced accordingly, as a spread over a reference rate together with a facility fee. The cost is a function of the issuing bank’s pricing and the borrower’s credit, and the filing places

At 100 basis points the annual cost is approximately $15,000 per RBP UCAP MW; at 200 basis points, approximately $30,000; and at 300 basis points, approximately $45,000. A letter of credit fee is not the same quantity as a corporate bond yield, and I do not equate them. I use these values as sensitivities rather than as estimates of the price any particular load-serving entity would obtain. 128.

I do not state a single blended cost across the two forms. PJM presently observes a mix of approximately thirty-five percent cash and sixty-five percent letters of credit across its markets, but that mix is observed against a total collateral balance that has never exceeded approximately $13.1 billion across every market and every participant, and against a period in which PJM's own reporting records the extension of unsecured credit alongside it. XII.  THE SOCIALIZATION OF A DEFAULT INCREASES THE OBLIGATION OF THE
REMAINING LSEs AND REACHES MEMBERS WITH NO ALLOCATION
153.
This section addresses where a loss goes. The Commission has identified the prevention of socialization as the function collateral performs: where collateral posted by a defaulting party is insufficient, the remaining exposure and costs are socialized across the membership. The standards set out in Section III order those two things — the defaulting participant’s own margin absorbs the loss first, and mutualization is what remains after it is exhausted. Section 18.9(a) does not expressly establish that sequence. The proposed provisions also contain two distinct reallocation mechanisms, operating on different triggers and different scopes, and the tariff does not distinguish them.

A.  Section 18.9(a) does not expressly require collateral to be applied before reallocation 154.

Section 18.9(a) provides that a defaulted RBP Charge “will be allocated to all remaining Load Serving Entities responsible for paying RBP Charges based on their pro-rata share,” and that such amounts “may be offset by the RBP Collateral posted by the defaulting Load Serving Entity.” Reallocation to non-defaulting parties is stated as mandatory. Application of the defaulting party’s own collateral is stated as permissive.

155.
That differs from the sequence the standards describe and from the sequence PJM applies elsewhere. Under Attachment Q, collateral posted against an obligation is applied to that obligation upon default, and mutualization under Operating Agreement, section 15.2.2 is the residual. Section 18.8 collects $1.5 million per MW from load, and section 18.9(a) does not commit PJM to apply it to the loss against which it was collected.

156.
I do not suggest that PJM would decline to apply the collateral. The point is that the tariff does not require it. The collateral posting is mandatory, while section 18.9(a) states its application to a defaulted RBP Charge in permissive terms. If PJM intends that the collateral be applied first, the filed text does not state it.

B.  Two reallocation mechanisms of different scope
157.
The proposed provisions reallocate an obligation in two circumstances, and the scope differs. Where a large load does not materialize or ceases operation, the obligation is reassigned within the zone. Where an allocated Load Serving Entity fails to pay, section 18.9(a) reallocates the defaulted charge to “all remaining Load Serving Entities responsible for paying RBP Charges,” language that on its face reaches the entire footprint rather than the zone. The tariff does not state that the two operate on different scopes, and nothing in section 18.9 confines the payment-default reallocation to the zone in which the default occurs.

158.
PJM described the treatment of a load that does not appear during the stakeholder process. In materials presented to the Reliability Backstop Critical Issue Fast Path on June 10–11, 2026, PJM stated that where a load does not materialize or shuts down before the end of the RBP term, the corresponding large load obligation must be reassigned, and identified three options: the Electric Distributor may assign the obligation to another Load Serving Entity; if it does not, and other Load Serving Entities in the zone/area hold assignments, the obligation is allocated pro rata among those remaining entities by zonal share; and if the Electric Distributor makes no assignments in the zone/area, “the costs will be reallocated across all load in the zone on a pro rata basis using the Obligation Peak Load Contribution MW (the same methodology used to allocate RPM costs).” Design Presentation,  at slide 30. PJM stated in the same materials that if states have not established frameworks to allocate costs to new data center loads, “it is unclear to which customers those costs would be assigned,” and that as a backstop PJM will allocate to all load in the zone, “including non-large loads,” using existing Peak Load Contribution assignments. The tariff states no standard governing how the Electric Distributor is to make the assignment, and no mechanism by which a Load Serving Entity may determine in advance whether it will hold one. What the tariff does specify is where the quantity comes to rest if the assignment is not made: on all load in the zone/area, including load that is not large load. In the NVEC zone/area the two steps reach the same entity. H.  Conclusion
174.
In my opinion the proposed provisions do not prevent the socialization of a default. They establish the paths by which residual losses are reallocated. The collateral posting is mandatory, while section 18.9(a) states its application to a defaulted RBP Charge in permissive terms. Where forecast Large Load does not materialize, the obligation remains within the zone and, under the final allocation step PJM describes, may ultimately be allocated to native load by Peak Load Contribution. An LSE payment default is addressed separately under section 18.9(a), which reallocates the defaulted charge among all remaining Load Serving Entities responsible for paying RBP Charges. The remedy PJM designed for the cooperative overbuy problem is conditioned so as to exclude cooperatives whose rates are state-regulated. And the residual extends to Members who take nothing from the procurement and post nothing against it.

XIII.  THE FILED CREDIT CONSTRUCTS WERE NOT EXAMINED IN THEIR FINAL FORM
175.
This section addresses the process by which the credit provisions developed. I raise it because credit and collateral were material design issues throughout the stakeholder process, because materially different approaches were proposed by PJM and other stakeholders, and because the methodologies ultimately filed were not examined in their final form. The Commission has held that credit requirements emerging from an RTO stakeholder process must still be reviewed for whether they are unduly burdensome. Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 126 FERC ¶ 61,153, at PP 20-21 (2009). The relevant question for my analysis is what opportunity existed for stakeholders to examine the magnitude, mechanics and liquidity consequences of the credit requirements ultimately proposed.

A.  PJM proposed using its existing credit framework for LSEs
176.
PJM addressed LSE credit requirements in its stakeholder materials on at least two occasions. In the Design Presentation, PJM stated that upon notification by the Load Serving Entity of known changes to its load, “the credit, and collateral requirements for LSEs are expected to be managed by the current credit processes,” and that those processes “include, but are not limited to, credit evaluation, peak market activity (PMA) recalculation and use of other mitigation tools, such as posting of credit support and/or unreasonable credit risk collateral calls (UCRs).” The same language appears in PJM’s Stage 4 proposal paper of June 30, 2026.

177.
That was a description of PJM’s existing credit framework applied to a new charge. It contained no fixed rate per megawatt, no aggregate requirement, no single posting date, and no stated departure from Peak Market Activity. As discussed above, Peak Market Activity based on historical billing would not by itself have captured the prospective exposure created by a new fifteen-year RBP obligation, and that limitation was raised during the stakeholder process. But the approach PJM described retained the principal elements of its existing credit framework: credit evaluation, recalculation of expected exposure through PMA, and the use of additional mitigation tools where the calculated exposure was not adequately secured. Nothing in the June 10–11 or June 30 materials identified a Credit Rate of $1.5 million per RBP UCAP MW or an aggregate LSE collateral requirement of approximately $10.2 billion.

B.  Other stakeholder proposals addressed buyer credit differently
178.
Credit and collateral were not absent from the stakeholder debate. Other proposals addressed the issue directly and proposed materially different approaches. The Joint EDC and Data Center Coalition proposal, which ultimately received the greatest sector-weighted support, tied procurement to the ability of the subscribing LSE or Large Load to satisfy the applicable credit requirement at the time PJM committed supply resources. An LSE unable to satisfy that requirement could not subscribe MW into the procurement. The Joint Consumer Advocates likewise identified credit and collateral as an issue in the procurement.

179.
The Coalition described the objective of its credit methodology in exposure terms. It proposed a requirement intended to ensure that “any emerging buyer default exposure is recognized early, funded continuously, and contained entirely within the LSE buyer” that subscribed to or received the RBP obligation. It also contemplated that the credit requirement would attach when the RBP commitment was made and “adjust[] over time.” That approach therefore contemplated both measurement of emerging exposure and adjustment of the requirement as that exposure changed. 180.

The Joint Consumer Advocates likewise identified demand-side credit and collateral as a necessary element of the RBP design, but proposed a different structure. Their June 30 executive summary stated that demand-side credit and collateral “should be established before procurement,” rather than assigned later. Their final recommendations to the Board again called for PJM to “[e]stablish demand-side credit and collateral prior to procurement.” They recorded PJM staff’s position on that issue as “collateral timing/assignment is later,” and identified as a source of stranded cost risk the absence of any opportunity to require Large Loads to commit to pay their RBP obligations and to post collateral with the Electric Distributor. 181.
The significance of these proposals for my analysis is not that the Commission should have preferred one stakeholder proposal over another. It is that the stakeholder process had identified buyer credit as a material RBP design issue and produced materially different approaches to addressing it. PJM proposed use of its existing credit framework, including credit evaluation and recalculation of exposure. The Coalition proposed that emerging buyer-default exposure be recognized early, funded continuously and contained within the subscribing buyer, with the requirement adjusting over time. The Joint Consumer Advocates proposed establishing demand- side collateral before procurement. None of those approaches proposed the fixed $1.5 million per MW requirement ultimately filed.

PJM stated that a fifteen-year tenor at a 9.5% discount rate “equates to ~seven years.” Its worked example applied a multiplier of 7.31, producing approximately $21.4 million for a 100 MW commitment at $400/MW-day, or approximately $214,000 per MW. 183.

PJM materially revised that construct in its final June 30 proposal. The Stage 4 proposal paper states the seller requirement as “Max ($20, 0.2 x bid/clearing price) x MW UCAP committed x 365 x 3 (years),” and explains that PJM was “proposing to require credit support equivalent to three years of the RBP shortfall charges.” Compared on a common basis, at the maximum willingness to pay of $555/MW-day the June 10–11 formulation with its 7.31 multiplier produces approximately $296,166 per MW, while the June 30 formulation produces $121,545 per MW — a reduction of approximately 59 percent. Section 18.8(a) as filed adopts the June 30 formulation without further change.

That presentation modeled the procurement as a fixed-for-float swap and estimated potential future exposure across the fifteen-year term. PJM defined potential future exposure as the maximum exposure of economic value to a counterparty at a future point in time, at a stated confidence level such as 95 percent. The same presentation set out the credit profile of the population that would be required to post. Section 18.8(b) as filed adopts none of those criteria for determining the initial LSE requirement. It does not measure the LSE’s expected RBP settlement exposure; it does not establish the initial requirement through Peak Market Activity or another exposure calculation; it does not vary the Credit Rate according to participant credit quality; and it does not establish a methodology for determining when additional or alternative mitigation is required. Instead, the initial requirement is determined by multiplying a fixed $1.5 million Credit Rate by the applicable quantity of RBP UCAP MW. Nor does the filed construct reflect the exposure profile PJM itself modeled, or the credit quality PJM itself measured. Potential future exposure as PJM estimated it rises and then declines to zero across the term; the Credit Rate is a single figure that does not decline at all. And the same $1.5 million per MW Credit Rate applies to each Load Serving Entity that receives an RBP obligation, without regard to the credit classification PJM assigned to that entity.

The absence of stakeholder endorsement does not itself determine whether a credit requirement is reasonable. The Commission has rejected a credit provision that had been developed through an RTO stakeholder process and approved by its Board, holding that it “cannot accept any proposal from the stakeholder process that is unduly burdensome or unduly discriminatory.” Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 126 FERC ¶ 61,153, at PP 20-21 (2009) (quoting Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 114 FERC ¶ 61,222, at P 19 (2006)). Stakeholder review provides an opportunity for the entities subject to a credit requirement to identify its operational, financing and liquidity consequences and to test the methodology used to measure the exposure before it is implemented.

H.  Conclusion
198.
In my opinion, neither of the credit constructs ultimately filed received stakeholder examination in its final form. Seller credit was materially revised in PJM’s final June 30 proposal, after which no further stakeholder meetings were held. The fixed $1.5 million per MW LSE requirement was not included in that final proposal and first appeared in the July 31 filing.

199.
This is significant because the stakeholder process had not ignored credit risk. PJM and other stakeholders had proposed different means of addressing it. Those approaches included credit evaluation, recalculation or continuing measurement of exposure, adjustment of the requirement over time, additional mitigation when needed, and establishment of buyer credit before procurement. The filed LSE methodology did not select among those approaches. It instead established a fixed Credit Rate derived from the gross contractual value and omitted the exposure- based criteria that had been identified during the stakeholder process.

200.
For a credit construct of this magnitude, those differences are material. The stakeholder process provided no opportunity to examine the $1.5 million per MW methodology against the matters addressed in this affidavit: its relationship to measured exposure; the quantity to which the Credit Rate applies; the availability and cost of permitted collateral; the mechanism by which collateral is reduced as exposure declines; the financial capacity of the entities required to post it; the consequences of a simultaneous collateral call; and the allocation of loss following default. The filed $1.5 million per MW methodology therefore was not subjected to stakeholder examination against those considerations before the filing.

XIV.  CONCLUSION
201.
I believe the matters set out above point to a straightforward characterization of what section 18.8 does. PJM took the gross contractual value of a fifteen-year commitment, halved it, and called the result collateral. Nearly every difficulty addressed in this affidavit follows from that single step. 202.

Collateral, as PJM administers it everywhere else in its markets, requires three things: an identified position that would have to be replaced on default, a price at which it would be replaced, and a period over which the exposure is held before replacement is complete. Attachment Q is built on those elements. RPM auction credit, FTR credit and Peak Market Activity are each measured against them, recalculated as conditions change, and released as the exposure declines. Section 18.8 identifies none of the three. It states a rate and a date.

PDF page 106

35

203.
The seller requirement confirms the diagnosis rather than qualifying it. Both sides of the RBP face the same quantity and the same two prices. The obligation is denominated in the same RBP UCAP MW, and the amount that moves between them in any Delivery Year is the difference between the RBP price and the RPM clearing price for that year. The economic consequence being analyzed on each side therefore depends on the same two prices and the same committed quantity. Symmetry in the exposure does not produce symmetry in the collateral. Section 18.8(a) requires a seller to post approximately $121,545 per MW, while section 18.8(b) requires load to post $1.5 million per MW against the same quantity and the same prices — roughly twelve times as much. Measured against the two Delivery Years whose differential is known within twenty days of commitment, the seller posts less than the exposure and load posts about nine times it. As set out in Section VII, the two requirements also imply contradictory RPM price paths: the seller figure is adequate only if RPM clears at or above approximately $532.80/MW-day on average, and above approximately $564.12 across the thirteen years that remain unpriced, while the load figure corresponds to the exposure only if RPM clears at or below approximately $281.03. No single price path satisfies both. Two requirements derived from one measured exposure would not diverge in this way. These did not begin from one.

204.
The timing of the load requirement is the clearest demonstration that it is not calibrated to exposure. Commitments bind when results post on December 2, 2026. The 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction has already cleared at $325.00/MW-day, so the first Delivery Year's exposure is known at the moment the commitment binds. The 2029/2030 results post on December 22, 2026, twenty days later. Within three weeks of commitment, therefore, two Delivery Years are priced, and on the adjusted target of approximately 6,000 MW the exposure they represent is approximately $1.0 billion, taking the 2029/2030 year at the $333.44 proxy discussed above. Against that quantified exposure PJM requires no collateral at all for approximately seventeen months, until roughly April 17, 2028. It instead requires approximately $9.1 billion on the same target against an exposure it has never quantified, beginning at the point when a substantial part of the position has become knowable and is no longer in doubt. A requirement that is absent where the exposure is measurable and largest where it is unmeasured is not a risk instrument. 205.

The remaining difficulties are consequences rather than independent defects. Because the requirement is not tied to a measured exposure, there is no quantity against which to apply it, and the tariff supplies three that do not agree. Because the filed methodology does not measure the exposure, it provides no exposure-based basis for reducing the requirement as that exposure declines, and section 18.8(b)(i)(A) provides only that the Credit Rate “may be reduced.” Because the amount is not sized to a risk, the cost of carrying it — approximately $102 million to $307 million a year against approximately $573 million billed to load in the first Delivery Year — appears in no figure PJM has presented and is recovered by no provision of the tariff. And because it falls on a single date, in two instruments, drawn from the same limited group of issuing institutions, without regard to the balance sheet of the entity obligated to post, it creates the correlated exposure that a credit policy exists to prevent.

206.
The point I made at the outset holds at the end of it. A security deposit is sized to the damage a tenant might do, not to the rent due over the life of the lease. A landlord who demanded six months’ rent on a twelve-month lease would be asked what damage he expected, and would be expected to answer. PJM has not identified the damage.

207.
None of this suggests that the RBP requires no credit support. It plainly does. Section 18.9(a) socializes a defaulted RBP Charge pro rata across the remaining Load Serving Entities, and under Operating Agreement, section 15.2.2 the residual reaches Members holding no allocation at all. A requirement set too low leaves native load bearing the cost of another party’s default. That is the harm collateral exists to prevent, and it is the harm the Commission identified in the Policy Statement on Electric Creditworthiness as the function of collateral to avoid.

PDF page 107

36

208.
But the error runs in both directions, and that is what makes the matter difficult rather than simple. A requirement set too high inhibits access to the market the RTO was formed to operate, and where it is large enough relative to the balance sheets on which it falls, it creates the systemic exposure it was meant to prevent. The Commission has held that “the role of an RTO is to balance the competing goals of minimizing risk to non-defaulting market participants while, at the same time, ensuring that the credit or collateral requirements are not so stringent that they unnecessarily inhibit access to the marketplace,” and has rejected a credit provision on that ground notwithstanding that it emerged from an RTO stakeholder process and was approved by that RTO’s board. Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 126 FERC ¶ 61,153, at PP 19, 21 (2009). The NOVEC zone illustrates that the second error is not hypothetical here: an allocation of approximately $1.95 billion falls on a distribution cooperative reporting total assets of approximately $1.716 billion and cash and temporary investments of approximately $243.2 million.

209.
That two-sided character bears directly on what can be done with the filing. The appropriate requirement cannot be determined from the filed formula alone. It requires the exposure to be measured — the position, the replacement price, the close-out horizon — and it requires the resulting number to be tested against the financial capacity of the entities that would post it. That second step cannot be performed on this record. The balance sheets of the obligated Load Serving Entities are not before the Commission. Neither is the letter of credit capacity available to them, the cost at which they could raise cash, or the effect of a simultaneous call on their existing facilities. Those are the facts on which any sound construct depends, and they reside with the parties rather than with PJM or the Commission.

210.
The stakeholder process ordinarily provides an opportunity for that examination. As set out in Section XIII, credit and collateral were considered during that process, but the specific $1.5 million per MW methodology first appeared in the July 31 filing and therefore was not subjected to stakeholder examination before filing. It was not tested there against the elements set out in Section III, against the quantity to which it applies, against the cost of the permitted forms of collateral, or against the capacity of the entities on which it falls. In my opinion the deficiency in the filed construct is not a matter of calibration that could be corrected by adjusting the rate. It is that the analysis on which any rate would rest was not performed, and the present record does not contain the participant-specific financial and liquidity information needed to perform it.

RBP price of $555.00/MW-day. Annual differential per MW equals (555 − P) × 365, where P is the RPM clearing price in $/MW-day. Undiscounted, consistent with ¶ 79. LSE side. Seller side, full term. Seller collateral of $121,545 per MW equals 0.2 × $555 × 365 × 3. It covers the cumulative fifteen-year exposure only if: (555 − P) × 365 × 15 = $121,545 → 555 − P = $22.20 → P = $532.80/MW-day

Seller side, remaining thirteen years.
