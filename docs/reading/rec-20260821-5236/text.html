I. Background
On July 23, 2026, the Commission convened a Chairman and Commissioner-led technical conference to examine PJM’s governance structure and stakeholder processes, addressing Board independence, Section 205 filing rights, stakeholder process reform, and state participation. The Commission invited post-conference comments on or before August 21, 2026, and indicated that an Alternative Dispute Resolution process will commence on September 1, 2026. Through its counsel, PJMICC participated in the July 23 technical conference, emphasizing support for PJM’s existing governance framework and sector-weighted voting; advocating targeted reform rather than wholesale restructuring, including reforms that would facilitate new resources and investment in the PJM grid; and identifying the need for customer cost-impact assessments and after-the-fact review mechanisms.

Subsequently, PJM circulated its mediation term sheet proposing eight reform measures, including proposing to assume from PJM Members additional Section 205 filing authority, making the Members Committee advisory to the RTO on all matters, extending Board terms to nine years, and creating state “jump ball” Section 205 filing rights regarding resource adequacy. PJM also proposed an MOU Framework with the states that addresses resource adequacy, large-load management, and transmission planning.

II. Areas of Common Ground
PJMICC supports PJM’s willingness to streamline committee structures and create expedited processes, provided that the process continues to solicit and consider diverse viewpoints. These proposals are directionally consistent with PJMICC’s longstanding concerns about siloed treatment of related issues and the cost of engagement in the stakeholder process for those with limited resources. The proposed elimination of the Markets and Reliability Committee, coupled with the proposed shift in voting at lower-level committees to sector-weighted voting, represents a reasonable balance. Taken together, eliminating duplicative committee-layer changes could improve efficiency while preserving meaningful stakeholder input and the sector-weighted framework. This would allow stakeholders to focus on consequential issues.

PJMICC also supports Board independence as a foundational element of PJM governance. PJM’s proposal for open Board sessions—with public statements and recorded votes—generally aligns with PJMICC’s transparency goals, while warranting careful implementation to preserve the Board’s independence. Extended Board terms, if paired with meaningful accountability measures, could promote institutional stability, although a nine-year term may be longer than necessary and could discourage interest in Board service.  Board members for the other RTOs and ISOs across the country have three-year terms.  Board terms of five or six years are still longer than those of other RTOs/ISOs.

III. Areas of Divergence
While acknowledging this common ground, PJMICC also identifies areas of divergence that warrant attention in developing a durable solution. Of particular importance is the relationship between PJM’s proposed Section 205 filing rights and the stakeholder checks that currently govern those rights.

PJM’s mediation term sheet proposes removing PJM Members’ Section 205 filing authority over the energy and ancillary services markets, as well as transmission planning. The term sheet also proposes making the Members Committee advisory on RTO functions, extending Board terms to nine years, and providing that PJM-sponsored packages advance “on equal footing” with stakeholder alternatives. PJM currently holds Section 205 rights regarding resource adequacy and the capacity construct.  The areas of concern with the PJM capacity construct are well documented in proceedings before this Commission.

PJM’s proposals, taken together, raise significant concerns. As explained in PJMICC’s Pre- filed Statement at this docket, the distinction between Section 205 and Section 206 review is not merely procedural—it reflects a deliberate allocation of regulatory burdens that incentivizes collaborative development of market rules.5 The question presented is not whether PJM can act when Members do not agree—it can, through Section 206—but rather which review standard should apply, and what safeguards should attend the expansion of unilateral filing authority. As outlined in PJMICC’s Pre-filed Statement, the D.C. Circuit has recognized that Sections 205 and 206, while both requiring just and reasonable rates, “enforce that mandate differently” and impose distinct burdens on proponents of rate changes.6 The lighter Section 205 burden is appropriately reserved for proposals that command broad stakeholder support and provide evidence of balanced consideration of competing interests. Where that support is absent, Section 206 supplies the appropriate review framework—not as a bar on PJM action, but as a check commensurate with the significance of unilateral change that could directly affect the regional economy.

PJM’s proposal to make the Members Committee advisory on RTO functions would represent a significant shift from the current framework.  PJMICC views the Members Committee's approval as an important check on PJM management and Board actions. If stakeholders were limited to a consultative role while PJM’s unilateral authority expanded, the reform package would reduce a meaningful source of accountability. For example, there can be an inherent tension in RTO governance when staff or management advance positions that may diverge from the broader public interest the Board is charged with serving. That tension is compounded by the specialized operational and technical expertise of RTO staff, which independent Board members—despite their experience and best efforts—may not fully share. PJMICC respectfully suggests that maintaining a meaningful role for the Members Committee would preserve a diversity of sector perspectives at a point when the Board may benefit most from them.  These areas of divergence underscore the need to examine the accountability framework that would accompany any expansion of PJM’s authority.

IV. Accountability Considerations
A central concern animating these comments is what may be viewed as an “accountability gap” in PJM’s reform proposals. PJM has explained that expanded Section 205 filing rights would help it act decisively when the stakeholder process does not produce timely outcomes.  At the same time, the term sheet does not include comparable mechanisms for evaluating the consequences of PJM’s decisions, such as customer cost-impact assessments, structured after-the-fact reviews, or sunset provisions. Unlike a conventional utility, PJM generally does not internalize the financial consequences of its market-design decisions; PJM Members, states, and customers bear the effects. While PJMICC does not support PJM securing additional Section 205 rights, PJMICC does see value in implementing additional accountability safeguards that make performance and customer

impacts visible and provide a basis for corrective action. The governance literature identifies this risk allocation as presenting a structural asymmetry.7

These observations underscore additional authority, to the extent directed by the Commission or otherwise, should be accompanied by transparent projections, defined success metrics, and a structured opportunity to adjust course when results differ from expectations.  PJM’s own proposed reforms—longer Board terms, an advisory role for the Members Committee, and expanded filing authority—would increase the Board’s independence while reducing certain traditional stakeholder checks. That combination warrants careful design: the governance literature identifies structural incentive considerations that may affect independent Boards.8 The need for a constructive accountability framework is particularly important given the PJM region’s unprecedented challenges—large load growth, manufacturing reshoring, data center development, and electrification mandates. Decisions made now will shape the cost landscape for decades. PJMICC therefore supports objective, transparent accountability measures that complement—not compromise—Board independence, including Board evaluations tied to customer outcomes, affordability, and the cost consequences of PJM’s decisions.

V. Proposed Accountability Safeguards
To address the considerations identified above, PJMICC respectfully proposes the following measures to complement any governance reform package.  They are designed to operate consistently with Board independence while providing transparent, objective methods for evaluating the effects of any expanded authority on retail customers and the public interest.

Board Composition. The Coalition recommends that PJM’s Board include members with direct experience managing large energy budgets or overseeing industrial and commercial energy consumers, ensuring that demand-side perspectives inform Board deliberations. This perspective would supplement, not displace, Board independence by bringing relevant customer experience into informed deliberations.

Mandatory Ex Post Reviews. Any significant market or transmission rule change should be subject to mandatory after-the-fact review to assess whether it delivered its intended results, including actual cost impacts on customers. Where a rule change fails to deliver its intended benefits or produces cost impacts that materially exceed projections, the reform package should include defined corrective measures, which may include a sunset or reversion. Corporate Transparency. PJM should disclose greater information regarding corporate goals and executive compensation structures, including public performance metrics tied to customer outcomes and cost management. Transparency into institutional incentives would enable stakeholders and the Commission to evaluate whether those incentives appropriately balance reliability, customer cost, competition, and responsible cost management.  This level of transparency is especially necessary to the extent that PJM were to be afforded Section 205 rights. Customer Cost-Impact Assessments. Customer cost-impact statements, including robust stress testing, should be required for any significant Section 205 (and Section 206) filing by PJM. Analogous to regulatory impact analyses, these assessments would identify expected effects on end-use customers and the assumptions underlying the proposal. Making that information available would help the Commission and affected customers evaluate the proposal’s likely consequences and support informed review.

Together, these safeguards—Demand-Side Board expertise, ex post reviews, corporate transparency, and cost-impact assessments—would help to ensure PJM’s expanded authority is exercised accountably and in the public interest.

VI. Conclusion
PJMICC supports meaningful governance reform and appreciates the Commission’s leadership and the constructive engagement of PJM, the PJM states, and PJM Members. Particularly at this inflection point, the need for clear-eyed focus on the reforms necessary to support cost-effective investment in the PJM grid, PJMICC’s principal recommendation is to preserve the existing distinction between Section 205 and Section 206 and the associated regulatory rigor, with additional transparent, objective accountability safeguards. PJMICC respectfully urges the Commission to consider the safeguards described above— including transparent cost assessments, defined success metrics, ex post review with appropriate corrective measures, demand-side Board expertise, and corporate transparency—into any final governance package. These measures would support durable reform, protect meaningful stakeholder participation, and help ensure that PJM’s governance serves the public interest while respecting Board independence.
