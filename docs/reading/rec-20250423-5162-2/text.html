Introduction
In the data center world, action revolves around energy – 
power is critical to operation and key to efforts to improve 
the sector’s environmental footprint. As a sector, data centers 
are engaged in a just transition from fossil fuels to the clean 
energy economy. Meanwhile, rocketing growth from AI, cloud 
computing, and digitization is driving unprecedented demand 
for power. The IEA 2024 Electricity Forecast predicts that 
global electricity consumption from data centers will double, 
from 460 TWh in 2022, to 1,000 TWh in 2026 – roughly 
equivalent to the power consumption of Japan.1
Similarly, McKinsey’s forecast in the US shows more than 15 
GW getting added over the next decade, a near doubling, just 
due to data centers.2
Compounding this challenge, electric grids have experienced 
relatively stagnant growth for nearly two decades. The 2024 
Grid Strategies report forecasts a 38 gigawatt increase in peak 
electricity demand in the U.S.by 2028 and concludes that “The 
U.S. electric grid is not prepared for significant load growth.”
Figure 1: Data center power consumption forecast from

In some regions, data center availability is low while demand 
for capacity is still high. Figure 2 shows that current available 
data center capacity in two of the largest U.S. data center 
markets, Silicon Valley/Bay Area and Northern Virginia, are just 
.5% and .2%, respectively. Power constraints add significant 
risk to growth in these regions.

There is keen awareness of a lack of power availability in key 
regions, pressure to meet timing of market requirements, and 
recognition of the need for access to a renewable energy supply 
to power data centers throughout the lifecycle and in the 
supply chain.
BYOP: What it means and how it works
Power capacity drives business growth. For data center 
developers, flexibility and control of power systems has 
become essential. Deploying highly flexible, onsite power, also 
known as “Bring Your Own Power” (BYOP), can significantly 
speed expansion, control costs, open new siting options, and 
provide the agility needed to adapt to energy system changes. 
It also can enable a faster and more predictable path to 
decarbonization and allow data centers to partner with utilities 
on solutions. Figure 3 shows a basic BYOP configuration where 
onsite power generation can fill in for both utility and backup 
generation and then remain onsite after the utility is connected 
to provide additional value discussed below.

Time to Market: Accelerating time to commission 
with faster deployment

A 2023 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory analysis states 
that the average utility interconnection timeline for new grid 
generation resources is 35 months in many parts of North 
America.5 New load interconnection also faces significant 
delays. Some data center customers report that they’re 
being given 3-5 year estimates for grid delivery of data center 
power. For a 100 MW data center, assuming US$150,000/MW 
of revenue per month (~US$150/month/kW), three years of 
delay translates to over US$500M in lost revenue.6 BYOP can 
accelerate data center commissioning to less than one year, 
which can be paired with the overall data center development 
and construction. This directly reduces time to customer 
delivery and accelerates revenue and growth. 
Flexible onsite power also supports additional benefits such 
as incremental campus/site expansion, adaptivity to market 
fluctuations, backup needs and the ability to replace diesel 
generators, and partnering with local utilities by participating 
in wholesale markets during periods of high demand. Data 
center assets can sometimes grow in a modular way. BYOP 
can enable that growth, where power is added modularly with 
the expanding site, regardless of grid availability. This modular 
approach provides data center developers better use of capital.
Besides scalability, modular power can also achieve high 
availability targets with redundancy and can even eliminate 
the need for generators solely used for backup power onsite, 
replacing diesel generators with cleaner options that pay for 
themselves by improving time to commission. 
Lastly, BYOP with low-emissions power gives developers 
greater flexibility over where they site their data centers, not 
just at sites that have existing utility power. In many cases, 
the locations that need the capacity most are those where 
grid availability is already significantly constrained or delayed.7 
Greater location flexibility opens up new possibilities for site 
selection – as long as the onsite power is low emissions 
(and thus easily permitted) and scalable in size to match 
data center needs. 
Adaptivity: Flexible dispatch enables additional revenue 
in a changing environment

In a time of transformational change, the flexibility to be more 
“future-proof” to the evolving environment becomes critical. 
Key factors like grid pricing, server load profile, the cost of 
different fuels, rack power density, requirements in carbon 
management, and total build size all can change over the 
lifetime of a data center. BYOP gives developers and owners a 
tool to manage and respond to these changes. 
Over the last three years, grid pricing has increased 5% in 
the U.S. after being flat for the prior two years.8 Given the 
extensive need for new capacity and new transmission and 
distribution, these growth rates are not expected to drop. 
These increased grid costs not only result in increases in the 
base cost of energy, but can also lead to higher demand 
charges or pricing based on usage during peak loads. Even if 
the grid is ultimately connected to the data center, flexible 
onsite power generation allows the operator to dispatch when 
needed, increasing power generation when rates are high and 
dropping power production when rates are low. Depending on 
the location, commercial grid power customers can save 10-
30% of their electric bill by just reducing their peak loads.9 
This serves to reduce both costs for the data center operator 
and strain on the local power grid, supporting the local 
community and reducing the need for the grid operator 
to overbuild capacity.
Data centers will transition over time from being energy 
customers to being contributors to the local grid. When data 
center and utility grid operators both contribute to clean 
energy generation, a green grid is possible.

Advanced power generation technologies, enabled by 
advances in power electronics and software, have made 
BYOP a viable alternative for data center developers. 
One such technology is the linear generator, recently 
featured in IEEE Spectrum.10 

Distinct from an engine, microturbine, or fuel cell, a linear 
generator directly converts motion along a straight line 
into electricity using chemical energy. The outcome is high 
efficiency, near-zero NOx emissions, full dispatchability 
(0-100% load acceptance/rejection) for firming renewables, 
high availability via modular buildout, and switching between 
fuels – including zero carbon fuels like green hydrogen 
and ammonia.11

The unique combination of linear generator capabilities 
addresses data center power challenges by providing local, 
dispatchable, and fuel flexible prime power with low total cost 
of ownership, at any scale. 
For developers, this can offer a fast track to bringing facilities 
online to address grid constraints. Later, when grid power 
eventually becomes available, the technology can transition to 
serve as clean, resilient backup power generation.

Decarbonization: Getting to zero carbon power and 
zero carbon backup 
Building sustainably powered data centers requires a transition 
away from the use of fossil fuels. Data centers invest heavily in 
renewables, but achieving zero carbon 100% of the time cannot 
be done with renewables and batteries alone. Zero carbon 
fuels such as green ammonia and green hydrogen have a role 
to play. Onsite power that is fuel-flexible – meaning that it can 
switch between fuel types without retrofit – gives developers 
and operators greater optionality and control on the path to 
decarbonization. Fuel-flexible power enables them to build 
both prime and backup capacity today with solutions that 
are equivalent or cleaner than today’s grid, and transition to 
increasingly lower carbon fuel use over time.

Summary and recommendations
Many promising developments of data center new builds 
are facing the headwinds of grid power constraints across 
global energy markets. There is a new imperative for data 
center cloud and AI developers to consider onsite energy 
generation, to build more robust and resilient power 
systems, and to establish energy policy with regulatory 
policymakers to embrace the future of data center builds 
with clean energy digital infrastructure. Developers also must 
quantify the energy mix in a just clean energy transition and 
pivot their roles from solely energy consumers to energy 
contributors, collaborating with grid providers to green the 
grid. The authors recommend that developers capitalize on 
the advantages of BYOP on the path to a just, green energy 
transition such that:
•	 Data center colocation providers can accelerate the 
deployment of new power capacity and create competitive 
advantage.
•	 Energy procurement can move beyond conventional power 
purchase agreements, as data center developers take 
control of their energy needs, participate in energy markets, 
and contribute to the green grid.
•	 Energy management systems can be optimized by shifting 
to a model that provides both primary power and backup 
power that are grid-interactive ready and sustainable.
Developers no longer need to choose between adding power 
and meeting sustainability goals. It is time to think differently 
about how data centers design for and use power.

Sustainability in The AI Data Center Era

Since the introduction of ChatGPT in November 2022, AI or/
and Generative AI have become part of our daily language. 
Some are enamored with the potential for artificial intelligence, 
while others are determining its implications for our present 
and future operations. For the Data Center ICT sector, AI has 
shown great potential. 
Until the introduction of large language models (LLMs), 
training applications relied on analyzed data and made 
predictions based on learnings from data sets. They often 
required a large volume of data sets to be useful for 
applications, such as quality system control, chatbots, natural 
language processing risk analysis, security surveillance, or 
autonomous vehicle maneuvering navigation systems. LLMs 
are a new breed of AI Machine Learning with the ability to 
recognize, summarize, predict, and generate large volumes of 
datasets containing text, audio, images, video, and metaverse 
content beyond human and traditional cloud computing. 
Furthermore, the initial push for Generative AI is in content 
creation. In text form, this can be as an email or new articles, 
blog post, or poem; in audio format as a voice response, a 
series of sounds, or a complete song; in image format as a 
picture or a series of images; and in video format as a movie, 
game, or one or more metaverse scenes. These AI assets 
are stored in data centers and retrieved by the requests 
of IIoT devices for entertainment, education, and work 
purposes. The potential of AI content creation is endless. 
The consumerization of this new content will grow at a fast 
pace as AI digital assistants become more personalized with 
behavioral learning and the proliferation of AI devices coming 
to market. According to Nvidia, AI bucks the conventional 
belief that humans must become programmers who study 
computer science in order to compete in the information 
age. AI shows promise that human language is optimal for 
interacting with AI systems via text, audio, and touch. The 
interactions of the human interface are adapted by AI systems 
which generate programming language for AI systems to 
complete tasks for humans. 
In 2024, we are seeing the advent of Generative AI applications 
adoption in the form of AI digital assistants. This deployment 
trend will have a significant societal impact by quickly driving 
the growth of AI to the edge; new edge data centers at urban 
communities will serve new content generation, providing 
contextual awareness of these digital assistants with location 
proximity. AI digital assistants will demonstrate productivity 
improvements in AI devices for entertainment, education, home, 
and work. The proliferation of autonomous vehicles aims to 
improve efficiency and human productivity. 
Developers are using Generative AI in the creation of new code, 
therefore eliminating millions of programmer hours, ensuring 
consistency in code quality, and improving overall development 
performance, given that code is increasing in complexity and 
size beyond human comprehension. Moreover, generative AI 
can be versatile in different languages, unlike humans who are 
limited to several languages and in their accuracy. 
 
Generative AI is making a definitive impact in research, and 
as the use of generative AI continues, we can expect future 
scientific breakthroughs. 
Sustainability Impact with AI on Efficiency and 
Performance have Unlimited Possibilities 
As with any revolutionary technology, Generative AI models will 
be refined and optimized with regards to improving their impact 
on sustainability. The sustainability capabilities of resource usage 
effectiveness can be improved and measured by using multiple 
modalities of machine learning, so that multiple tasks are trained 
simultaneously to improve accuracy and efficiency. The total 
resources will be thus reduced. Additionally, the personalization 
of AI digital assistants in ever increasing and personalized 
versions of multimodal modes on various platforms will help to 
eliminate waste and redundancy. The use of AI in AIDC shows 
the promise of new realms of sustainability, with regards to

BYOP: Net Zero Availability Zone Future Proof 
Resilience Grid Power System 
Currently, it is a conventional design that each data center has 
a primary power system and a backup power system unit to 
maintain uninterrupted operation. Many power assets are 
designed for primary function, and the backup power assets take 
instantaneous action to provide power to the data center for a 
duration of time such as 4-8 hours or up to 24 hours – when the 
fault is being identified of the primary power systems until it can 
assume its normal operation. Otherwise, the backup systems 
are not used. 
We need to examine if the concept of primary and backup 
systems is sustainable, in comparison to a resilient system that can 
operate as both to maintain uninterrupted operation for the data 
center. Net Zero Utility Availability Zones provide flexible, resilient 
systems at Grid scale, consequently eliminating the need for 
conventional backup systems for data centers. 
BYOP: Empowerment as Energy Contributor 
to promote Sustainability 
The impact of a carbon free future is paramount for our 
generation. We must take action to pave the way for future 
generations. The planet itself is resilient; meanwhile, humans 
are depleting our natural ecosystems at a fast pace in a linear 
economy. 
The design of the Future of Data Centers is to be connected 
with the grid; rather, it becomes grid interactive to ensure cloud 
and AI services are resilient, sustainable and affordable, and in so 
doing, futureproof their growth potential for primary and backup 
power. The result is a triple bottom line of much lower Capex 
for redundant assets, much higher utilization through multiple 
uses of common assets (e.g., energy infrastructure), maximized 
investment efficiency for renewable power investments, clean 
energy generation, and the elimination of need for backup assets. 
Data centers transition from an energy customer to a contributor 
to the local grid. When data centers and utility grid operators 
contribute to clean energy generation, a green grid is made 
possible. The design concept of availability zones, which originated 
in the data center sector, may be applied to the utility grid. The 
electric grid assets operate to maximize power availability for 
all stakeholders in order to reach the full potential of power 
availability and concurrently deliver cost efficiency at utility scale 
at Utility Net Zero Availability Zones by regions. 
AI Data Centers are designed to be a sustainable contributor to 
the clean energy economy. We need to take a novel approach, 
utilizing net zero availability zones, linear generators with clean 
fuel for clean energy generation in place of on-premise prime 
power systems and a separate backup power system exemplifies 
the Sustainable Development Goals and impactful results.
