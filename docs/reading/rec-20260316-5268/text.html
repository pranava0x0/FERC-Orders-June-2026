Subject: Co-Located Load Related Revisions to Open Access Transmission Tariff 
Introduction 
Energy Artisans ("EA") is an established membership organization formed in 2017 that is comprised of industry 
experts to serve various client needs.  The group is anchored by individuals who have decades of energy market 
experience in general purchasing, wholesale and behind-the-meter power plant development and operation, as well 
as bulk and retail energy delivery, fuel purchasing and delivery, efficiency measures, and direct participation at the 
membership and stakeholder level in PJM. 
EA respectfully submits these formal comments in protest of the Compliance Filing submitted by PJM 
Interconnection, L.L.C. ("PJM") on February 23, 2026. While PJM ostensibly seeks to address reliability and 
resource adequacy risks pursuant to the Commission’s December 18, 2025, Order to Show Cause, the proposed 
revisions to the Open Access Transmission Tariff ("OATT") represent a fundamental misalignment of cost and 
benefit.  
PJM’s filing attempts to solve reliability concerns by imposing an untenable economic environment upon 
commercial and industrial (C&I) users through restrictive materiality thresholds and discriminatory study 
requirements. As currently structured, PJM’s proposal is unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory under 
Section 206 of the Federal Power Act. Consequently, EA urges the Commission to reject these revisions to ensure 
that the region’s C&I backbone does not suffer detrimental and unintended consequences for administrative 
convenience. 
1. Evaluation of Negative Economic Impact on Commercial and Industrial Users 
The proposed revisions to the Behind the Meter Generation ("BTMG") rules threaten the foundational economic 
stability of the PJM region. For over twenty years, C&I facilities have operated under BTMG netting rules to 
optimize energy efficiency and manage operational costs. PJM’s proposal to implement a 50 MW "Materiality 
Threshold" for Retail BTMG is an arbitrary cap that ignores the long-term, capital-intensive investment cycles of 
large-scale industrial facilities. By capping the ability to net at 50 MW, PJM effectively strangles the growth of 
high-efficiency industrial sites that were financed on the premise of netting on-site generation against total load. 
The financial implication of this 50 MW cap for two C&I clients we have managed would be between $7MM and 
$36MM/annually.  These are representative of impacts on various other clients, and are not inconsequential to their 
overall costs.  
The financial burden is further exacerbated by the "Necessary Study Process" described in this filing. PJM seeks to 
force "Project Developers" (industrial users) to fund exhaustive thermal, short circuit, and stability studies. Beyond 
these administrative costs, the filing mandates that users bear the full expense of: Network Upgrades, identified 
during the study process; Complex Control Technologies and Protection Systems, allegedly required to prevent 
"unauthorized grid withdrawals"; and Redundant Metering Equipment necessitated by PJM’s refusal to adopt a 
more flexible regulatory approach.  All this for systems on end users’ property supplying their electric usage 
requirements.  This is a breach of fundamental privacy, and such rules are not applicable to other alternative fueled 
systems that provide onsite work which is backed up by potential electric use should those systems fail.

Strategic Implications of the Economic Burden: 
• 
De Facto Penalty on Efficiency: These costs function as a punitive tax on cogeneration and Qualifying 
Facilities ("QFs") protected under the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act ("PURPA"). By making it 
administratively and financially impossible for large QFs to operate under the new OATT, PJM undermines 
the economic and energy efficiency benefits inherent in waste heat recovery. 
• 
Forced-Exit Strategy: PJM’s rejection of a hybrid approach—which would allow facilities to net up to a 
threshold—on the grounds that it is cost-prohibitive is a tacit admission. PJM is essentially acknowledging 
that its all-or-nothing requirement is a deliberate barrier to entry designed to force large loads off of BTMG 
status and onto the grid. 
• 
Stranded Assets: Forcing decades-old facilities to undergo modern "Necessary Studies" and install 
previously unnecessary metering will cause many to cease operations, leading to higher Network Loads 
and exacerbating the resource adequacy issues PJM claims to mitigate. 
2. Argument Against Discriminatory Treatment 
The filing represents a clear violation of the Federal Power Act’s requirement for just and reasonable rates due to 
its discriminatory application. PJM establishes a 50 MW cumulative nameplate cap for "Retail BTMG" (C&I users) 
while maintaining a massive 3,000 MW regional cap for "Non-Retail BTMG" (municipal and cooperative systems).. 
Furthermore, PJM’s manipulation of the legal boundary of the site is a transparent attempt to restrict BTMG 
eligibility. In its filing, PJM aggressively defends the use of the Point of Change in Ownership (“PCO”) rather than 
the Point of Interconnection (“POI”). This technical shift is strategically designed to shrink the legal definition of 
the facility boundary, thereby capturing more load for grid-service billing and excluding it from the BTMG actual 
use category. 
PJM’s filing admits that many of these industrial configurations have "been in existence without noted impact 
for decades". This admission renders the sudden imposition of the 50 MW cap and restrictive PCO definitions 
arbitrary. If no impact has been noted for twenty years, the reliability risk is clearly a manufactured pretext for cost-
shifting.  We have had direct experience with customers whose BTMG was as large as 150 MW behind loads in 
excess of 200 MW with no adverse impacts. 
3. Proposed Alternative: 200 MW Threshold and Interruptible Transmission Service 
A constructive alternative exists that balances grid integrity with the industrial sector's operational reality. The PJM 
ICC advocates for an Interim/Alternative Materiality Threshold of 200 MW, anchored in the Actual Use principle. 
Prior to data centers there have been many large industrial loads with correspondingly large BTMG.  As established 
in the PJM ICC Request for Rehearing/Clarification in Docket EL25-49, filed on January 20, 2026, and supported 
by the C&I clients we represent and our direct experience, the 200 MW threshold is a far more practical solution 
for existing industrial and institutional loads that have long relied on their on-site investments to manage electric 
and other commodity costs efficiently.  For sites exceeding this 200 MW threshold, the Commission should direct 
PJM to utilize the proposed "Interim Network Integration Transmission Service" (Interim NITS) under the new Part 
XI of the Tariff. This service is non-firm and interruptible, providing PJM with the operational safety switch it 
needs to manage reliability during peak emergencies without forcing cost-prohibitive upgrades or complete 
disconnection on industrial users.  There is a corollary in natural gas: firm and interruptible service.  It is critical 
that interruptible gas customer cease using gas when instructed by the applicable pipeline or utility system.  Failure 
to do so results in extreme financial penalties and the offending customer may even be shutoff if system integrity is

Appeal for Commission Action 
PJM has fundamentally failed to prove that a 50 MW threshold is the least cost or most equitable way to manage 
co-located load, especially while exempting municipal entities from similar constraints. 
We implore the Commission to summarily reject the February 23 Compliance Filing. At a minimum, the 
Commission should stay its implementation and direct a formal paper hearing to: 
1. Evaluate the 200 MW threshold based on the "Actual Use" principle; 
2. Mandate the inclusion of an interruptible tier using Interim NITS for all large loads; 
3. Reject the restrictive "Point of Change in Ownership" definition as an arbitrary shrinking of the site 
boundary; and 
4. Enforce PURPA protections for cogeneration facilities that PJM seeks to penalize. 
The Commission cannot allow PJM to undermine the industrial backbone of the region through arbitrary caps and 
protectionist barriers.
