III. ANSWER
A. Protection Systems and Other Reliability Remediation Requirements are
not Optional.
Two reply briefs raise issues concerning PJM’s proposal for protection systems and
other reliability remediation requirements.  They generally argue that reliability protections and remediation should be tailored to actual risk rather than imposed as default requirements.5 These arguments suggest that protection systems and remediation requirements are somehow elective.  For instance, Constellation claims that protection systems are not appropriate for every situation and should not be used to mitigate contractual issues such as unauthorized withdrawals.6  These arguments are inconsistent with the Order as well as fundamental reliability principles.

In the Order, the Commission made clear that one of the rationales for implementing the three new transmission services specific to Co-Located Load was Co-Located Loads “are willing and able to control their withdrawals from the transmission system” through the use of system protection facilities.7  The Commission was unambiguous on this point, stating that “in these instances where the Co-Location Arrangement includes special protection schemes that limit its energy withdrawals, we find that it is a just and reasonable replacement rate to allow an Eligible Customer taking transmission service on behalf of such load to take and be charged for new types of transmission service that are alternatives, and not equivalent, to [Network Integration Transmission Service].”8  In other words, in order for an Eligible Customer to take one of the new Co-Located Load-specific transmission services it is necessary and thus just and reasonable to require an Eligible Customer to include appropriate protection systems. Therefore, contrary to Constellation’s claim, it is not only appropriate but necessary for PJM to require that any Eligible Customer that wishes to take one of the new transmission services to include protection systems to ensure that those Eligible Customers do not exceed their contractual limitations.

Moreover, the obligations of PJM and Transmission Owners in PJM to safeguard and maintain system reliability are paramount, as the Commission has established on numerous occasions.  Accordingly, the new transmission services required under the Order must be subject to appropriate terms and conditions to ensure that the implementation of these new services does not compromise system reliability.  Co-Located Loads, as well as any on-site generators used to serve those loads, are not islanded.  Rather, they are fully connected and

B. Transmission Owner Load Integration Studies Should not be Subject to
FERC Filing.

integration studies conducted by Transmission Owners and direct those load integration studies to be filed with the Commission.17  This suggestion is meritless.  As PJM correctly pointed out in its April 24 reply brief, the Transmission Owners’ processes for studying the integration of new retail loads, and identifying associated upgrades, do not inherently implicate the Commission’s jurisdiction.18  To the contrary, retail load studies have always been subject to state jurisdiction, and the fact that such studies will be used as one of the inputs into PJM’s process for studying applications for one of the new transmission services does not provide a sufficient basis for the Commission to assert jurisdiction over those studies. C. Objections to the Proposed Grid Reliance Charge are Meritless.
A few reply briefs take issue with the PJM Transmission Owners’ Grid Reliance Charge proposal.  In general, these briefs claim that the proposed Grid Reliance Charge is unprecedented and inconsistent with principles of cost causation.22  These objections are misplaced for a number of reasons.

The PJM Transmission Owners developed the Grid Reliance Charge partially in response to Commissioner Chang’s guidance that a just and reasonable replacement rate for the new transmission services should reflect “some form of minimum transmission service charge, which would protect other customers against cost shifts resulting from Co-Located Loads that rely upon the transmission system but do not directly or meaningfully contribute to its costs via transmission service payments.”23  The PJM Transmission Owners’ proposed Grid Reliance Charge achieves this goal.  As the PJM Transmission Owners explained in their April 24 Reply Brief, the proposed Grid Reliance Charge is designed to reflect the costs of using the transmission system to deliver black start and regulation services, as well as the costs of providing the inherent benefits that all customers, including Contract Demand customers, receive as a result of their synchronization to and reliance on the grid.24

In its May 11, 2026 Answer, Constellation presents a handful of criticisms of the PJM Transmission Owners’ Grid Reliance Charge proposal.25  For example, Constellation’s witness, Zachary Ming, asserts that the PJM Transmission Owners “fail to address the fundamental shortcoming of their proposal – a transmission system built only to deliver ancillary services would be nowhere near as large or expensive as a transmission system built to serve peak demand.”26 Mr. Ming continues to miss the point.  The purpose of the Grid Reliance Charge is not merely to recover the costs of a fictional transmission system built only to deliver ancillary services, but rather to ensure that Co-Located Load customers pay their fair share of the transmission system as it exists and the associated benefits that it provides to all interconnected customers, including essential reliability services, such as frequency support, inertia, voltage stability, fault current capability, ride-through and protection coordination, and sufficient short-circuit capability.27  The PJM Transmission Owners’ proposed Grid Reliance Charge does so by calculating the portions of annual depreciation and operations and maintenance expenses that constitute a reasonable proxy for the costs of providing those benefits.  The Grid Reliance Charge is therefore just and reasonable.
