ANSWER OF EXELON CORPORATION TO THE MOTION FOR LEAVE AND
SUPPLEMENTAL PROTEST OF CONSTELLATION ENERGY GENERATION, LLC
Pursuant to Rule 2131 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or “Commission”), Exelon Corporation on behalf of its subsidiaries2 (collectively, “Exelon”) submits this Answer to the Motion for Leave and Supplemental Protest that was filed by Constellation Energy Generation, LLC (“Constellation”) on April 8, 2026 (“Constellation’s Motion”).

Constellation’s Motion adds nothing to the Commission’s analysis and should be rejected. The motion suggests that Constellation’s limited engagement with one PJM transmission owner about one proposed design implies a perverse policy of not only that one utility but of all PJM transmission owners.  The motion goes on to imply that Constellation’s one experience somehow undermines the justness and reasonableness of PJM’s proposal.  Exelon files this Answer to correct any implication that Exelon, the ultimate parent corporation of various PJM transmission owners, or those various transmission owners are attempting to prevent large loads, co-located or otherwise, and their load serving entities from receiving transmission services in the Exelon utilities’ service territories.

EXELON LARGE LOAD STUDY PROCESS
4. The Exelon Utilities, like others utilities around the nation, have received an unprecedented influx of requests to interconnect new large loads to serve data centers.  At ComEd, for instance, over the past two years, there have been many requests, with projected demands of tens of thousands of megawatts.  The projected demands exceed ComEd’s all-time system peak, which is to say that they would more than double ComEd’s overall demand.

5. The Exelon Utilities are committed to serving these customers—that is their business and obligation.  It is what we do.  But it has to be done in a way that ensures that service is safe and reliable for both existing customers and new customers alike, and thus each request is subject to study.

6. The details of the study process are evolving, but in general, we have found that it is better for all concerned to conduct studies in clusters, with projects submitted within particular time windows studied in synchrony.  To this end, the Exelon Utilities define study “windows” during which they collect requests from new large loads across their respective service territories.  Each window has a set deadline for submission, and a new window opens once the previous window closes. The utility then aggregates the demand schedules, or “ramp rates,” of all projects submitted during the same window and studies them collectively to determine the cumulative impact on the transmission system.  Through this analysis, the utility also identifies whether any transmission or high voltage system reinforcements or expansions are needed to ensure the safety, reliability, and efficiency of those systems, as well as the utility’s ability to serve all its customers.  A cluster study includes the determination of how customers are interconnected to the grid, a planning analysis to identify any necessary transmission upgrades to maintain reliability, and preliminary engineering, scope, cost, and schedule to connect customers and complete necessary network upgrades. Once a cluster study is complete, customers that execute a transmission security agreement and remain committed to interconnect, have their direct connection needs and any necessary network upgrades detail engineered and planned for actual construction.  That mode of analysis is well-established at Exelon where ComEd, for instance, is completing study of Cluster 2 and preparing to begin Cluster 3.

7. The cluster studies were implemented because one-by-one studies delayed the utility’s ability to provide customers with critical information about potential in-service dates and sometimes required repeated re-studies as new projects entered and exited the queue.  The cluster study process resolves these issues by allowing the utility to evaluate projects together, improving

responsiveness to customers and capturing economies of scale in study and planning processes.  This approach also provides a more comprehensive and transparent view of the collective impacts of large demand growth.  When multiple projects are proposed in similar timeframes, studying them as clusters enables the utility to better assess overall system reliability and the interrelationship of nearby loads—insights that cannot be achieved through isolated, piecemeal evaluations.  As a result, cluster studies produce more accurate, efficient, and forward-looking outcomes for both applicants and the broader customer base.

8. Because these are studies of service requests for new end users, aspects of their conduct and their application to those retail service applications are subject to regulation by state utility commissions.  For example, the ComEd process was the subject of a proceeding at the Illinois Commerce Commission evaluating proposed tariff amendments to address service to such loads.   That ICC proceeding recently adopted the process proposed by ComEd.  See, https://www.icc.illinois.gov/docket/P2025-0677/documents/377910/files/662669.pdf.

9. It is typical that prior to the study being conducted, there will be one or more informational meetings with the customer to clarify the details of the project to be studied.  These meetings are an early opportunity for engagement, well before the actual studies occur.  The customer may seek clarification on the parameters of the upcoming study, and the utility may obtain information to refine its understanding of the proposal.  Which is to say that the meetings are a two-way street, each side developing a better understanding of what should be studied. These meetings do not include policy or legal teams, and are thus not a place where decisions get resolved.  Where a policy or legal question may arise, those at the meeting will follow an established escalation path to ensure appropriate review by those with decision-making authority.
