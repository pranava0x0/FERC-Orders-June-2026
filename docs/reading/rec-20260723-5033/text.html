Pursuant to the Commission's Notice of Technical Conference and Second Supplemental Notice in the above-
captioned proceeding, Energy Artisans, LLC respectfully submits these comments regarding PJM Interconnection, 
L.L.C.'s governance and stakeholder processes. The Commission has requested comments addressing potential 
reforms to improve PJM's ability to respond to operational and market needs in a timely and effective manner.  
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Power markets, like all markets, exist for one purpose: to serve customers. They do not exist to serve market 
administrators, incumbent market participants, or even regulators. Institutions succeed only insofar as they promote 
reliable service, efficient investment, prudent risk management, meaningful competition, and customer choice. 
PJM has been one of the world's most successful organized wholesale electricity markets. The Commission's inquiry 
should therefore not be viewed as criticism of that success, but as an opportunity to ensure that PJM's governance 
evolves alongside the electric system it serves. 
Energy Artisans respectfully offers some guiding principles: 
1. Governance exists to serve customers rather than institutions. 
2. Governance should be evaluated by measurable outcomes rather than procedural activity. 
3. Customers, who pay for everything need a stronger voice. 
4. Competitive markets should reward preparation, prudent investment, and responsible risk management. 
5. Rules should solve demonstrated problems using the least restrictive means reasonably available. 
6. Accountability should apply equally to market participants and institutions. 
7. Price signals are ineffective behind process bottlenecks. 
 
I. MARKETS EXIST TO SERVE CUSTOMERS 
The Commission's Notice asks how PJM's governance can become more responsive, transparent, accountable, and 
capable of addressing operational and market needs in a timely manner. Those questions are best answered by first 
identifying the purpose of organized wholesale markets. 
Power markets exist to serve customers.  They do not exist to preserve existing market structures, protect incumbent 
participants, maximize procedural activity, or perpetuate institutional processes. 
PJM exists because customers benefit when competitive wholesale markets encourage efficient investment, reliable 
service, prudent risk management, innovation, and economically efficient prices.  Governance therefore should 
always be evaluated according to whether it advances those objectives.

II. GOVERNANCE SHOULD BE MEASURED BY RESULTS 
Governance is not an end in itself.  Its purpose is to produce timely, transparent, accountable decisions that improve 
customer outcomes.  The electric industry now faces unprecedented changes driven by electrification, artificial 
intelligence, data centers, distributed energy resources, storage technologies, and evolving resource adequacy needs. 
Institutions designed during a period of relative stability must now demonstrate that they remain capable of 
responding at the pace required by today's markets.  Governance therefore should always be evaluated according to 
whether it advances those objectives.  Lengthy deliberative processes undoubtedly encourage broad participation. 
However, when process itself becomes the primary objective, governance can inadvertently delay the very market 
reforms it was designed to facilitate. 
Governance should therefore be evaluated using measurable outcomes, including: 
• 
timeliness of major decisions;  
• 
implementation of approved reforms;  
• 
transparency of deliberations;  
• 
stakeholder confidence; and  
• 
customer outcomes.  
 
III. CUSTOMERS SHOULD HAVE A STRONGER VOICE 
One aspect of PJM governance deserves particular attention. 
Organizations with substantial commercial interests in existing market structures understandably devote 
considerable resources to stakeholder participation. Utilities, transmission owners, generation companies, 
marketers, and trade associations all provide valuable expertise. 
However, commercial and industrial customers, municipalities, project developers, and private investors often 
participate with comparatively fewer resources despite bearing many of the costs associated with governance 
decisions. 
Customers ultimately finance generation investments, transmission facilities, market administration, and 
compliance with PJM requirements. Governance reforms should therefore seek to ensure that customer perspectives 
receive meaningful consideration throughout the stakeholder process. 
Improving customer participation does not require diminishing the contributions of other sectors. Rather, it requires 
maintaining a governance structure that appropriately reflects the interests of those who ultimately bear the 
economic consequences of market decisions. 
 
IV. COMPETITION REWARDS PREPARATION 
Competitive markets succeed because they reward preparation rather than prediction.  Markets, like Aesop's 
seasons, reward participants who anticipate uncertainty, maintain liquidity, hedge prudently, and invest before

V. RULES SHOULD SOLVE DEMONSTRATED PROBLEMS 
Every institution accumulates rules over time.  Most begin as reasonable responses to legitimate concerns.  
Collectively, however, they may eventually produce unintended complexity, delay, administrative burden, and 
barriers to investment.  The Commission therefore has a recurring opportunity—and responsibility—to determine 
whether accumulated governance provisions continue to serve the competitive purposes for which they were 
originally adopted. 
Rules should solve demonstrated problems using the least restrictive means reasonably available.  That principle 
preserves both competition and customer welfare. 
 
VI. GOVERNANCE HAS BECOME A CONSTRAINT ON INVESTMENT 
Energy Artisans' own experience demonstrates why governance matters.  As a practical example, we have a project 
that entered PJM's interconnection queue in February 2021 and is in the current Transition Cycle 2 with no network 
impacts.  Under the present schedule, commercial operation is not expected until approximately March 2029—
roughly eight years to interconnect a 10 MW facility connected to an existing 69-kV transmission line producing 
approximately 20,000 MWh / year. 
During this period, Energy Artisans has been required to post a $250,000 Readiness Deposit for a system that does 
not appear ready to take our project, paid interconnection study costs exceeding $70,000.  By comparison, a similar 
project completed before PJM's recent interconnection reforms progressed from study through complete 
interconnection agreement in approximately six months with study costs of approximately $9,000 and entered 
commercial operations 18 months later.  This experience indicates a broader governance issue. 
PJM required Energy Artisans to demonstrate its readiness through substantial financial commitments.  Neither 
PJM nor the transmission owner were subject to a comparable obligation to demonstrate their readiness to complete 
the interconnection process within a commercially reasonable period.  Accountability should apply equally to all 
institutions and market participants.

VII. WHEN PROCESS BECOMES THE BOTTLENECK, PRICE SIGNALS LOSE THEIR 
EFFECTIVENESS 
Recent market results reinforce this conclusion.  The 2028–2029 PJM Base Residual Auction cleared at the market 
price cap while reserve shortfalls increased and only approximately 525 MW of new resources entered the market. 
Analysts concluded that the present framework is not successfully bringing sufficient new supply online.  Classical 
economics predicts that higher prices encourage additional supply.  That relationship breaks down when 
governance, interconnection, and infrastructure delivery—not economics—become the limiting factors. 
Even if capacity prices had cleared at $1,000, $5,000, or $10,000 per MW-day, Energy Artisans’ project—and many 
other projects already caught in lengthy interconnection processes—still could not have contributed capacity for the 
2028–2029 delivery year. The limiting factor was not the strength of the price signal, but the time required to obtain 
studies, agreements, upgrades, and authorization to interconnect. 
VIII. RECOMMENDED REFORMS 
Energy Artisans respectfully recommends four practical reforms. 
A. Establish Governance Performance Metrics 
PJM should annually report objective measures including average decision timelines, implementation schedules, 
stakeholder participation, and completion of major initiatives, and new generation in service net of retirements. 
Governance should be evaluated by measurable performance rather than procedural activity. 
B. Create Expedited Procedures 
Certain issues—including reliability, resource adequacy, cybersecurity, and significant market integrity 
concerns—should be eligible for accelerated stakeholder review with clearly defined timelines. 
Expedited procedures should supplement, rather than replace, the existing stakeholder process. 
C. Improve Transparency 
Stakeholders would benefit from publicly available tracking of significant initiatives, including expected 
decision milestones, implementation schedules, and reasons for material delays. 
Transparency increases confidence even where participants disagree with particular outcomes. 
D. Conduct Periodic Governance Reviews 
Just as utilities periodically reassess planning assumptions, PJM governance should undergo regular review to 
ensure that governance structures continue to support evolving market conditions. 
 
IX. RESPONSES TO THE COMMISSION'S QUESTIONS 
The Commission's questions generally focus upon Board authority, stakeholder representation, transparency, 
responsiveness, governing documents, and opportunities for reform.

Energy Artisans respectfully offers the following observations: 
Board Accountability: The Board should continue serving as an independent steward of PJM while periodically 
reporting objective governance performance metrics that demonstrate responsiveness and implementation 
effectiveness. 
State Participation: States provide important policy perspectives. Their participation should continue through 
established channels while preserving PJM's operational independence and regional mission.  PJM should establish 
a practical pathway for bona fide state consumer representatives and other customer representatives to obtain 
meaningful voting participation in the End-Use Customer Sector, subject to safeguards against duplicative 
representation.. 
Stakeholder Process: The existing stakeholder structure has produced substantial benefits over many years. 
Nevertheless, the process would benefit from greater transparency, defined timelines, and expedited treatment for 
high-priority issues. 
Governing Documents: Operating Agreements, Manuals, and related governance documents should be periodically 
reviewed to eliminate unnecessary complexity and administrative burden. 
Customer Representation: Governance reforms should seek meaningful participation by commercial, industrial, 
institutional, and municipal customers whose investment decisions and operating costs are directly affected by PJM 
policies. 
 
X. CONCLUSION 
Energy Artisans, LLC develops, owns, finances, operates, and advises commercial, industrial, institutional, and 
utility energy projects throughout the PJM region. Our experience includes wholesale and retail electricity markets, 
behind-the-meter generation, distributed energy resources, utility operations, industrial energy management, project 
finance, and direct participation in PJM stakeholder activities. 
PJM has earned an international reputation for operating one of the world's most successful competitive wholesale 
electricity markets. The next stage of that success should focus upon maintaining the strengths of the stakeholder 
model while improving accountability, transparency, customer participation, and timely decision-making. 
Periodic governance review is therefore not a criticism of institutional success. It is an essential characteristic of 
responsible stewardship.  FERC's responsibility is not simply to protect markets from risk.  That observation applies 
equally to regulators, regional transmission organizations, utilities, generators, developers, and customers.   
It is to ensure that wholesale markets remain just, reasonable, competitive, and responsive to the customers who 
ultimately pay every transmission bill, every capacity payment, every energy settlement, and every rate-based 
investment.  The best markets reward performance, innovation, prudent risk management, and timely execution—
not organizational size or the ability to navigate unnecessary procedural barriers. 
Markets do not progress because someone predicts the future correctly. They progress because thousands of 
participants, each risking their own capital, continuously discover a better future.
