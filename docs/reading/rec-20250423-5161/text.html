COMMENTS OF THE SOLAR ENERGY INDUSTRIES ASSOCIATION
Maintaining U.S. leadership in Artificial Intelligence (AI) is essential for our national security and economic growth.1 To maintain leadership in the industry though, the U.S. needs to provide for the rapid development of the data center infrastructure necessary to support AI growth. Hyper scalers, utilities, Regional Transmission Organizations, developers, state regulators, and the Commission each play a critical role in supporting that infrastructure and its significant power demands. And each one of these stakeholders must work expeditiously to remove the barriers to that development and provide regulatory certainty.2

SEIA submits these comments in response to the Commission’s February 20, 2025 Order Instituting Proceeding under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act and Consolidating with Other Proceedings and PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s March 24, 2025 Answer to that Show Cause

Order.3 In the Show Cause Order, the Commission seeks comment on whether the PJM Tariff addresses, with sufficient clarity or consistency, the rates, terms, and conditions of service that apply to co-location arrangements between large loads and generating facilities. Co-located load arrangements are increasingly common in PJM,4 because they help address the large power demands of AI infrastructure and new manufacturing.5 However, the PJM Tariff lacks the clarity necessary to allow for these kinds of arrangements. In its response to the Show Cause Order, PJM presents eight options, three of which are currently in its Tariff. PJM’s answer provides the framework for the Commission to direct Tariff changes that will clearly provide for the rates, terms, and conditions of co-location agreements.

SEIA urges the Commission to direct PJM to develop Tariff language implementing the five new options for co-location arrangement. As PJM already notes, its options are not mutually exclusive.6 Nor should they be. PJM, and the Commission, should provide clear rules for co- location and provide flexibility and regulatory certainty to accommodate different arrangements. Additionally, with respect to PJM’s Option 6, the Bring-your-own Generation option, the Commission should direct PJM to create a process by which it would expedite the interconnection of generation being used exclusively to serve large load customers through a co- location arrangement.

I. COMMENTS
A. The Commission must base costs on grid use and should accommodate both
front of the meter and behind the meter load arrangements when setting
those costs.
The transmission and ancillary service costs must reflect the benefits the co-located load arrangement receives from the grid.7 The type of benefits received will depend on the specific co-location arrangement.8 As SEIA noted in our post-technical conference comments, some arrangements can be completely islanded from the grid and bear the risk of going offline.9 Others cannot take such a risk. Some large loads are flexible and can respond to utility price signals and demand response programs. Each arrangement “is going to be a snowflake . . . they are going to have their own design, their own concerns, their own issues, their own configurations that they would like to meet.”10

Despite the variety of potential arrangements, PJM’s Tariff lacks any mechanism to
determine which transmission service classification—and corresponding cost allocation—applies to each co-location arrangement. Although PJM prefers large loads to be considered network load, in front of the meter,11 two of the options would allow for load to be considered behind the meter.12

SEIA does not take a position on whether the large load should be considered front of the meter or behind the meter and urges the Commission to refrain from mandating one network configuration over the other. As the Commission itself recognized, “[t]he record demonstrates that different co-location arrangements are likely to use or benefit from the transmission system in different ways depending on how they are configured and, moreover, that those different configurations will themselves likely have different impacts on the transmission system.”13 There are trade-offs in each model and no single model can address the full spectrum of technical considerations across the variety of co-location proposals. The Commission should maintain both pathways so that each project can select the configuration best suited to its commercial structure and reliability needs. As the Commission observes, “there is no clear record before us that Network Integration Transmission Service is the appropriate transmission service for all co- location arrangements or that such service would be consistent with the cost causation principle … for such arrangements.”14

The Commission should refrain from preferencing a particular Network configuration. Beyond technical and market considerations, the importance of data-center growth to U.S. economic competitiveness and national security reinforces the need for regulatory neutrality. As then-Chairman Phillips observed, “data centers…are national resources, with generational significance, and vast national security and national economic consequences. They belong in the United States, and…this agency should be doing [its] very best to nurture and foster their development.”15 Preferential treatment of one network configuration would constrict, rather than

B. The Commission should direct PJM to create processes in its Tariff to
effectuate Options 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8.
1. The PJM Tariff lacks provisions to address the co-location of large loads
and generation.
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission found that the PJM Tariff  “appears to be unjust and unreasonable or unduly discriminatory or preferential because it does not contain provisions addressing with sufficient clarity or consistency the rates, terms, and conditions of service that apply to co-location arrangements.”16 Despite statements from PJM,17 and the PJM Indicated Transmission Owners,18 the PJM Tariff does not address with “clarity or consistency the rates, terms, and conditions of service that apply to co-location arrangements.”19 While PJM presents three options that are currently in its Tariff, only one of those options explicitly provides for co-location. Option 1, Load with a separate Point of Interconnection that is Network Load, explicitly provides that the load and generation are not co-located.20 Though presented as a large load co-location option for the Commission to consider,21 it does not meet PJM’s definition of co-located load because the load and generation are behind separate Points of Interconnection.22 Similarly, Option 2, Co-located load that is at the same Point of

2. The new options provided in PJM’s Answer would result in just and
reasonable rates, terms, and conditions for co-location of large load and
generation.
In its Answer, PJM presented five “potential options that have been proposed by others or developed by PJM in response to issues raised in the various predecessor co-located load proceedings.”29 The Commission should direct PJM to develop and file Tariff revisions to implement each of these five options.

The November Technical Conference demonstrated that large loads come in different
shapes and sizes.30 In the Show Cause Order, the Commission recognized that “The record demonstrates that different co-location arrangements are likely to use or benefit from the transmission system in different ways depending on how they are configured and, moreover, that those different configurations will themselves likely have different impacts on the transmission system.”31 As the Indicated Transmission Owners recognized, there is an “evolving co-location configuration.”32

This proceeding has only just scratched the surface of the technical capabilities of data centers when it comes to energy needs, load flexibility, and demand reduction. This is, in part, because the market does not yet “know yet what types of load flexibility we're going to fully see from data centers.”33 The technology is constantly evolving and may result in significant power savings for both new and existing data centers.34 Researchers from the Nicholas Institute at Duke

C. The Commission should direct PJM to fast-track new generation that would
come online via co-location agreements.
While making progress, PJM is still in the process of transitioning its backlogged serial queue to the new cluster study process. It has been well established throughout this proceeding, and several other proceedings, that the addition of large loads to the PJM system is outpacing the addition of the new resources needed to serve that new load.45 Several comments in this proceeding have noted that when new large loads sought to co-locate behind the meter of existing generation, there could be resource adequacy issues.46 The Commission echoed these potential concerns in the Show Cause Order.47

 One way to address these concerns is to “figure out ways to bring new gen[eration] online.”48 PJM’s Option 6 provides the basis to do exactly that.49 Option 6 “offers a potentially faster interconnection path for generation resources supporting co-located loads.”50 The Commission should direct PJM to modify this option to implement an expedited co-located interconnection process, separate from the queue, to determine whether the interconnection request for the co-located generation would require new Network Upgrades.

Under this model, PJM would study the generator, whether utility owned or owned by an independent power producer, similar to how it would study surplus service requests, in order to determine whether any new Network Upgrades would be needed to facilitate the interconnection request.51 One assumption of this co-location interconnection study would be that the resources using this interconnection option would supply their entire output to the co-located load, and would not be exporting to the grid and therefore these arrangements should be exempt from traditional Network Upgrade triggers or full deliverability requirements.

service.52 In Order No. 845, the Commission stated that “surplus interconnection service is created because generating facilities may not operate at full capacity at all times.”53 Under a co- located interconnection process, the generating facility may be operating at full capacity, however, that capacity would never exceed the requirements of the co-located load, and therefore

The purpose of co-located interconnection service is twofold. First, this service would help address the “rapid tightening of supply and demand” that PJM has highlighted in several filings.56 Bringing new supply online faster will alleviate some of the demand pressures on the PJM system. Second, fast tracking new generation may also help prevent transmission system overbuild. Utilities include planned data centers in their load projections and transmission planning. The utility may begin incurring transmission costs to accommodate that load and those costs may end up stranded if the data center project is cancelled and the load does not show up.57 By allowing co-located generation to come online along with the large loads, the grid impact of the large load would likely be reduced, which would reduce the need to plan for Network Upgrades. The Commission should direct PJM to create a fast-track co-location interconnection process to ensure that new generation is timely, reliable, and avoids unnecessary transmission overbuild.
