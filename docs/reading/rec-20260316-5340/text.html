1. 
PJM’s proposed materiality threshold does not comply with the 
Commission’s Co-Location Order. 
 
The Commission must reject PJM’s proposed BTMG materiality threshold because it 
does not comply with the Commission’s directive in the Co-Location Order. PJM failed to file 
revisions that impose a “MW threshold for the amount of load that Network Customers may net 
by using BTMG” as directed in ordering paragraph (F) of the Order.7 Rather than limit what load 
a Network Customer may net, PJM proposes a limit on the amount of nameplate generation that 
may qualify as BTMG.8 Generation is not load. A threshold based on generation capacity is 
plainly not a limit based on the load that is being netted.

load that a customer may net.9 The Commission found PJM’s existing BTMG provision are not 
just and reasonable because they “do not limit the amount of qualifying load that a Network 
Customer may net by using BTMG and do not provide any protections against this potential 
transmission and resource adequacy planning problem or against the costs of maintaining 
sufficient resources to serve these loads being shifted onto other customers.”10 The Commission      
was motivated by the potential for large amounts of netting to occur through BTMG

3. 
PJM’s proposal based on nameplate generation is not just and 
reasonable because it limits commercial opportunities for BTMG without 
providing additional commensurate system benefits. 
 
PJM’s materiality threshold based on nameplate generation unnecessarily constrains 
access to BTMG arrangements without providing commensurate system benefits and is 
therefore unjust and unreasonable. By focusing on nameplate generation, PJM’s proposal cuts 
off the potential for the routine, relatively small-scale upsizing of generation above the amount 
of load that is netted, taking off the table a number of valuable BTMG configurations without 
providing additional system benefits in terms of reducing the risk of resource adequacy or cost-
shifting concerns.  
The Commission recognized the value of BTMG netting in the Co-Location Order, citing 
prior precedent finding that netting “encourages load response during periods when generation 
of transmission are in short supply and prices are rising” and may contribute to charges for use 
of PJM’s transmission system that are consistent with the principle of cost causation.14 The 
Commission sought to address risks, however, associated with BTMG netting at a very large 
scale made possible by the proliferation of large loads such as data centers in PJM.15 As 
reflected in the Commission’s directive to PJM to implement a materiality threshold that is 
based on reductions in load, the resource adequacy and cost-shifting risks of BTMG depend 
upon the scale of the netting that is allowed. As the Commission explained, “not all loads using 
BTMG are large loads that create the reliability and resource adequacy risks described above.”16

The Commission thus found that the just and reasonable replacement rate should strike a 
balance by limiting large scale BTMG netting while enabling smaller scale netting that does not 
contribute to reliability and resource adequacy risks in the same way. 
PJM’s proposed materiality threshold upsets that balance by limiting the availability of 
BTMG arrangements for loads even at the smaller scale. As seasoned developers of BTMG in 
PJM, BTMG Parties are aware of several common BTMG arrangements that would net no more 
than 50 MW of load, and therefore not raise the kinds of reliability, resource adequacy, or cost-
shifting risks of concern, but that would rely on more than 50 MW of nameplate generation. For 
example, it is not uncommon for BTMG configurations to rely on combinations of resources 
that have different availability profiles, such as solar and storage, to firm up the generation. In 
such arrangements, the combined nameplate generation is greater than the load reduction sought 
to be achieved in order to ensure that reduction can be achieved during the required hours of 
load operation. It is also common for nameplate BTMG to be somewhat oversized for loads that 
have the most stringent commercial expectations of reliable operation (the so-called “five nines” 
of reliable operation), as is the case for certain data centers. Such oversizing of the BTMG may 
be commercially valuable because it ensures the load can continue to be served by BTMG 
during partial outages, like when a unit may need to be taken offline to perform planned or 
unplanned maintenance. Or, in another routine case, customers may choose to upsize the scale 
of a battery storage system to address battery degradation that occurs over time, and to ensure 
that there is no disruption in operation of the load while replacement of that capacity occurs.        
Under each of these scenarios, the load netted would remain below the 50 MW threshold 
and therefore fall within the scope of the small scale BTMG netting that Commission found 
should be permitted under the Co-Location Order. Yet PJM’s proposal would prevent such

4. 
PJM’s proposal is also unduly discriminatory because it treats 
similarly situated load configurations differently. 
 
Using a nameplate-based materiality threshold rather than one based on the amount of 
load that can be netted treats behind the meter generators with similar impacts on resource 
adequacy and cost shifting differently and is therefore unduly discriminatory. As discussed 
above, the relevant characteristic of customers relying on BTMG is the scale of the reduction in 
load that they seek to achieve, because large scale reductions may result in resource adequacy 
and cost-shifting concerns. But PJM’s proposal treats BTMG configurations that achieve the 
same netting of load differently. For example, 30 MW of BTMG combined heat and power 
generation at one site and a hybrid 30 MW gas turbine plus 30 MW battery storage system at a 
different site, serving a different retail customer, can each be designed and operated to have the 
same impact on the grid, where each achieves no greater than a 30 MW reduction in the 
respective retail customer’s load, but the customer with the 30 MW BTMG diesel generation 
would be eligible for netting under PJM’s rules while the customer with a 60 MW combined 
nameplate hybrid system would not. This is particularly objectionable given that PJM’s Effective 
Load Carrying Capability (ELCC) recognizes the different resource adequacy profiles that 
different resources have and requires real-world addition to meet resource adequacy needs,17 
while PJM’s proposed BTMG rules would bind loads to nameplate metrics that do not reflect 
how these resources perform in the real world.

Additionally, PJM’s proposal would needlessly expose retail loads served by BTMG just

threshold based on net load would protect against the reliability and cost shift impacts that the 
Commission sought to address while providing for more continuous treatment to BTMGs on 
either side of the threshold line.
