I. INTRODUCTION
Community Organizations1 and Clean Energy Advocates2 and their respective members reside throughout the Midcontinent Independent System Operator Inc.’s (“MISO”) footprint.  They rely on MISO to provide affordable and reliable wholesale service to power many aspects of their lives, including their residences, businesses, hospitals, places of worship, and schools.  Their members also rely on MISO’s adherence to non-discriminatory practices that enable a diverse set of energy generation technologies, namely distributed energy resources, to provide low-cost energy options that will help lower their energy bills without increasing pollution in their communities. Additionally, one of Clean Energy Advocates is an organization including energy project developers that regularly use MISO’s interconnection services.

2 Clean Energy Advocates consist of Sierra Club and Natural Resources Defense Council, which are nonprofit environmental organizations with members throughout MISO territory; and Clean Grid Alliance, a nonprofit corporation chartered under the laws of Minnesota.

On June 18, 2026, the Commission issued an order to show cause (“OSC” or “Show Cause Order”) to MISO and its transmission owners (“TOs”) directing them to either provide more options to fast track the grid interconnection of large load customers, which at the moment are primarily data centers, or explain why MISO’s tariff is sufficient.  MISO submitted its Zero Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement (“ZGIA”) proposal (the “Proposal”) to respond to the co-location requirements in the Commission’s OSC.3   Our organizations do not oppose the intended design of MISO’s ZGIA Proposal—a co-location arrangement that results in no injections or withdrawals of electrons to MISO’s transmission system and, therefore, does not require network upgrades nor cause negative impacts to the Definitive Planning Phase (“DPP”) process. Rather, we oppose the lack of processes and tariff provisions to make those design elements a reality.

Without proper tariff provisions, this process could have the opposite of MISO’s intended effect by instead operationalizing ongoing cost shifts that will be difficult, if not impossible, for states to untangle, triggering otherwise preventable reliability shortalls, and allowing generators with ZGIA service (“ZG Resource”) to perpetually gain an unfair advantage over projects in DPP, frustrating the implementation of Order 2023’s queue reforms to the detriment of market participants and consumers, like our members.  As discussed below, we urge the Commission to direct MISO to address these tariff and procedural defects through either a deficiency letter or direct further compliance via the show cause proceeding.

3. Length of the Transient Tolerance Window Should be Set According to
Clear Standards.
A third infirmity in ensuring that zero means zero is how MISO proposes to set the “Transient Tolerance Window,” which is “the period of time after a reduction or loss of load by the ZG Associated Load by the end of which window the [ZG] Generating Facility must have” ceased injections to the grid.42  In other words, this time span, defined as no more than 20 seconds,43 is the time for a ZG Generating Facility to ramp down production as needed when its co-located load drops.  This time window is a fundamental engineering parameter of ZGIA service, but MISO has not justified why a maximum period of 20 seconds is either operationally attainable by a generator (or if a greater or lesser time window would be more appropriate), or operationally acceptable for the transmission grid to remain stably within reliability criteria while receiving unexpected injections over that time interval.

MISO states that it “determined that a 20-second limit provides the best system-wide balance between what is technically feasible and commercially practical on the one hand, and minimizing even transient injections on the other hand”;44 and MISO’s witness Keyser opines that 20 seconds was chosen “in order to align with the MTEP modeling interval of 20 seconds for transient stability analysis.”45  The physical or even customary basis of 20 seconds remains obscure, as this figure is mentioned neither in the existing Attachment FF (Transmission Expansion Planning Protocol) nor in Business Practices Manual 20 (Transmission Planning).  MISO should better justify the specific time interval chosen with reference to NERC standards, local planning criteria, or some other grounding in electrical engineering realities.

C. The ZGIA Program’s Scope Should Have a Defined Limitation.
The Commission’s two orders last year on the ERAS fast-track interconnection program emphasized the Commission’s longstanding policy on open access to the transmission system and requirement to limit the scope of departures therefrom.  The first ERAS order denied MISO’s initial proposal as not just and reasonable, in part because it lacked a program size cap (in either project counts or project megawatts).57  The Commission also found that the proposed program end date of December 2028, allowing for three and a half years’ worth of project selections, would “exacerbate[] the potential for a volume of ERAS interconnection requests untethered to reliability or resource adequacy needs” and would “make[] it difficult to determine […] whether the solution is narrowly tailored to fix the problem.”58  MISO altered the time limit on its ERAS proposal when it re-filed, setting August 2027 (two years after program commencement) as the sunset date, and capping the size of the program at 68 total projects.  The Commission credited these limitations in ultimately approving the second ERAS proposal, finding that “the limited, one-time design of the [proposed ERAS] process weighed significantly on our decision here.”59

D. The Proposal Fails to Adequately Mitigate the Risk that Network Upgrades Will Be Paid by Other than the Cost Causers.

MISO’s ZGIA proposal includes two primary mechanisms that together are designed to prevent cost-shifting from ZGIA projects to other interconnection customers or load. These mechanisms involve: 1) a requirement that, in order to qualify for ZG Interconnection Service to begin with, the ZG Generating Facility will not require additional large-scale network upgrades, and 2) that MISO can credit the ZG Generating Facility as a “Non-Transmission Alternative” as a result of signing five-year commitments with ZGIA load.  However, as further described below, those protective measures do not sufficiently insulate Network Upgrade costs from instances when some or all the ZG Associated Load is not placed in service.

Specifically, as discussed in section II.D.2 below, there are at least three principal flaws with the Proposal that can result in cost-shifting: 1) the five-year commitment is insufficiently concrete to provide the requisite certainty that the load integration study and its assumptions regarding needed upgrades are accurate; 2) the structure of the Proposal creates a fast-track ZGIA to GIA process whereby ZG Generating Facility could later convert to ERIS or NRIS service and thereby

2. MISO’s Safeguards are Inadequate to Prevent Cost-Shifting of Network
Upgrades to Interconnection Customers and Load.
The safeguards described above were, in several different ways, inadequately designed to prevent cost shifts of Network Upgrades being socialized to non-ZGIA load.  MISO’s proposal is unjust and unreasonable until such time that it closes the gaping holes in its proposal that are particularly vulnerable to gaming and undue cost shifting.

i. The proposed five-year commitment requirement does not provide
certainty that the load integration study and its assumptions regarding
needed upgrades are accurate

First, for new ZG Associated Load, the Proposal does not require adequate financial security, cost-recovery agreements, or true-ups for the cost of deferred transmission upgrades if the ZG Generating Facility fails to materialize, retires early, or becomes permanently unavailable.  For example, if the ZG Generating Facility or its contractual arrangement terminates, and a deferred upgrade is identified and becomes necessary through a later MTEP cycle, MISO’s proposal would not specifically have those costs assigned to either the ZG Generating Facility or to the associated LSE.  Therefore, the upgrade[s] would likely be allocated under ordinary MTEP rules and spread to other transmission customers. III. CONCLUSION
To effectuate MISO’s zero injection concept without raising the risk of adverse reliability events and cost shifts, Community Organizations and Clean Energy Advocates respectfully ask the Commission to use its authority to help MISO course correct.  Given the demonstrated reliability concerns interconnecting and serving large load customers, no countervailing reasons justify failing to establish operational penalties and transparent baseline requirements for system protection mechanisms.   Similarly, as the Commission noted in the OSC, cost transparency is paramount given the scale and pace of forecasted load growth.  Without publishing study information and reducing instances for mismatching cost responsibility between the multiple generator interconnection queue processes, MISO’s ZGIA process could unduly shift cost burdens to other ratepayers without giving states and other advocates visibility to identify or resolve the issues.
