I. 
INTRODUCTION 
Community Organizations1 and Clean Energy Advocates2 and their respective members reside 
throughout the Midcontinent Independent System Operator Inc.’s (“MISO”) footprint.  They rely 
on MISO to provide affordable and reliable wholesale service to power many aspects of their lives, 
including their residences, businesses, hospitals, places of worship, and schools.  Their members 
also rely on MISO’s adherence to non-discriminatory practices that enable a diverse set of energy 
generation technologies, namely distributed energy resources, to provide low-cost energy options 
that will help lower their energy bills without increasing pollution in their communities.  
Additionally, one of Clean Energy Advocates is an organization including energy project 
developers that regularly use MISO’s interconnection services.

On June 18, 2026, the Commission issued an order to show cause (“OSC” or “Show Cause 
Order”) to MISO and its transmission owners (“TOs”) directing them to either provide more 
options to fast track the grid interconnection of large load customers, which at the moment are 
primarily data centers, or explain why MISO’s tariff is sufficient.  MISO submitted its Zero 
Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement (“ZGIA”) proposal (the “Proposal”) to respond to 
the co-location requirements in the Commission’s OSC.3   Our organizations do not oppose the 
intended design of MISO’s ZGIA Proposal—a co-location arrangement that results in no injections 
or withdrawals of electrons to MISO’s transmission system and, therefore, does not require 
network upgrades nor cause negative impacts to the Definitive Planning Phase (“DPP”) process.  
Rather, we oppose the lack of processes and tariff provisions to make those design elements a 
reality.  
Without proper tariff provisions, this process could have the opposite of MISO’s intended 
effect by instead operationalizing ongoing cost shifts that will be difficult, if not impossible, for 
states to untangle, triggering otherwise preventable reliability shortalls, and allowing generators 
with ZGIA service (“ZG Resource”) to perpetually gain an unfair advantage over projects in DPP, 
frustrating the implementation of Order 2023’s queue reforms to the detriment of market 
participants and consumers, like our members.  As discussed below, we urge the Commission to 
direct MISO to address these tariff and procedural defects through either a deficiency letter or 
direct further compliance via the show cause proceeding.

3. 
Length of the Transient Tolerance Window Should be Set According to 
Clear Standards. 
A third infirmity in ensuring that zero means zero is how MISO proposes to set the “Transient 
Tolerance Window,” which is “the period of time after a reduction or loss of load by the ZG 
Associated Load by the end of which window the [ZG] Generating Facility must have” ceased 
injections to the grid.42  In other words, this time span, defined as no more than 20 seconds,43 is 
the time for a ZG Generating Facility to ramp down production as needed when its co-located load 
drops.  This time window is a fundamental engineering parameter of ZGIA service, but MISO has 
not justified why a maximum period of 20 seconds is either operationally attainable by a generator 
(or if a greater or lesser time window would be more appropriate), or operationally acceptable for 
the transmission grid to remain stably within reliability criteria while receiving unexpected 
injections over that time interval. 
MISO states that it “determined that a 20-second limit provides the best system-wide balance 
between what is technically feasible and commercially practical on the one hand, and minimizing 
even transient injections on the other hand”;44 and MISO’s witness Keyser opines that 20 seconds 
was chosen “in order to align with the MTEP modeling interval of 20 seconds for transient stability 
analysis.”45  The physical or even customary basis of 20 seconds remains obscure, as this figure is 
mentioned neither in the existing Attachment FF (Transmission Expansion Planning Protocol) nor 
in Business Practices Manual 20 (Transmission Planning).  MISO should better justify the specific 
time interval chosen with reference to NERC standards, local planning criteria, or some other 
grounding in electrical engineering realities.

C. The ZGIA Program’s Scope Should Have a Defined Limitation. 
The Commission’s two orders last year on the ERAS fast-track interconnection program 
emphasized the Commission’s longstanding policy on open access to the transmission system and 
requirement to limit the scope of departures therefrom.  The first ERAS order denied MISO’s 
initial proposal as not just and reasonable, in part because it lacked a program size cap (in either 
project counts or project megawatts).57  The Commission also found that the proposed program 
end date of December 2028, allowing for three and a half years’ worth of project selections, would 
“exacerbate[] the potential for a volume of ERAS interconnection requests untethered to reliability 
or resource adequacy needs” and would “make[] it difficult to determine […] whether the solution 
is narrowly tailored to fix the problem.”58  MISO altered the time limit on its ERAS proposal when 
it re-filed, setting August 2027 (two years after program commencement) as the sunset date, and 
capping the size of the program at 68 total projects.  The Commission credited these limitations in

D. The Proposal Fails to Adequately Mitigate the Risk that Network Upgrades Will 
Be Paid by Other than the Cost Causers. 
MISO’s ZGIA proposal includes two primary mechanisms that together are designed to prevent 
cost-shifting from ZGIA projects to other interconnection customers or load. These mechanisms 
involve: 1) a requirement that, in order to qualify for ZG Interconnection Service to begin with, 
the ZG Generating Facility will not require additional large-scale network upgrades, and 2) that 
MISO can credit the ZG Generating Facility as a “Non-Transmission Alternative” as a result of 
signing five-year commitments with ZGIA load.  However, as further described below, those 
protective measures do not sufficiently insulate Network Upgrade costs from instances when some 
or all the ZG Associated Load is not placed in service.  
Specifically, as discussed in section II.D.2 below, there are at least three principal flaws with 
the Proposal that can result in cost-shifting: 1) the five-year commitment is insufficiently concrete 
to provide the requisite certainty that the load integration study and its assumptions regarding 
needed upgrades are accurate; 2) the structure of the Proposal creates a fast-track ZGIA to GIA 
process whereby ZG Generating Facility could later convert to ERIS or NRIS service and thereby

MISO’s Safeguards are Inadequate to Prevent Cost-Shifting of Network

The safeguards described above were, in several different ways, inadequately designed to

particularly vulnerable to gaming and undue cost shifting.

CONCLUSION 
To effectuate MISO’s zero injection concept without raising the risk of adverse reliability 
events and cost shifts, Community Organizations and Clean Energy Advocates respectfully ask the 
Commission to use its authority to help MISO course correct.  Given the demonstrated reliability 
concerns interconnecting and serving large load customers, no countervailing reasons justify 
failing to establish operational penalties and transparent baseline requirements for system 
protection mechanisms.   Similarly, as the Commission noted in the OSC, cost transparency is 
paramount given the scale and pace of forecasted load growth.  Without publishing study 
information and reducing instances for mismatching cost responsibility between the multiple 
generator interconnection queue processes, MISO’s ZGIA process could unduly shift cost burdens 
to other ratepayers without giving states and other advocates visibility to identify or resolve the 
issues.
