PROTEST OF PJM POWER PROVIDERS GROUP
PJM is at a critical moment in its history. Facing the challenges presented by the emergence of potentially significant new large demand and finding the market-based means of building the capacity to support the new growth will demand economically rational and structurally sound market design.  Now is precisely the time that PJM and the Commission need to dedicate themselves to the proposition that a properly designed and well-functioning market will ultimately serve the consumers of the PJM region by providing reliability at the least cost. Unfortunately, the interim resource adequacy service (“IRAS”)1 and large load registry proposal (the “IRAS Proposal”) PJM has submitted represents an ill- conceived and unnecessary step away from PJM’s core principles and, perhaps more troubling, has long term implications for PJM’s capacity construct that may permanently scar it.  PJM’s IRAS Proposal reflects an untested and unsupported, unjust, unreasonable and unduly discriminatory experiment with the nation’s largest power grid that has historically provided the consumers of the region with reliable service at competitive capacity prices. A resource that cannot expect to cover its going-forward costs may deactivate or leave the Region, and that decision responds to the same signal as a decision to build. Price is the mechanism through which a reduced procurement quantity reaches those decisions. New entry and retention are not separate market-design questions. Every new resource becomes an existing resource, and PJM must provide a sufficient and durable investment signal throughout the asset’s operating life. The Reliability Pricing Model was designed to deliver, on average over that life, the net cost of new entry: the revenue a resource requires beyond what the energy and ancillary services markets provide. That shortfall is what the capacity market exists to supply. A capacity market that lowers the revenue a resource can expect affects both the decision to build and the decision to keep operating, and resource adequacy depends on both.

22. The mechanism works through the auction’s demand curve. PJM decides in advance how much capacity it will buy at each price, and that schedule is the Variable Resource Requirement Curve. The filing would subtract the Excluded Amount from the quantity on which that Curve is built, so PJM would buy less capacity at every price, while continuing to count the same load when it calculates how much capacity the Region needs to serve its load reliably. Separately, capacity procured through the Reliability Backstop Procurement is required to participate in the auction as a price taker at $0/MW-day, which adds supply that clears regardless of price. Buying less and adding supply that always clears both push the clearing price down. Neither does so while the auction clears on the flat portion of the Curve; once it clears on a sloped portion, both do.

Consumers may pay lower Reliability Pricing Model charges in the near term, but that reduction results from buying less capacity, not from a reduction in the resources needed to serve the load or in the cost of providing them. It told the Commission that recognizing value in capacity above the reserve margin dampens volatility and allows “investors to make reasonable predictions of revenue streams,” which “reduces reserve and capacity price volatility and consequently the return required by investors resulting in savings in consumer costs.” PJM Interconnection, L.L.C., Docket Nos. ER05-1410-000 and EL05-148-000, Transmittal at 24 (Aug. 31, 2005). PJM added that “additional reserves due to the VRR curve result in lower capacity and energy costs to consumers,” and that “[e]xcess reserves also further reduce the probability of loss of load and discourage withholding by suppliers to increase profits.” Id. at 24-25. It supported that with a table showing capacity cost falling as the reserve level cleared higher, and explained that the capacity savings “will be augmented by energy cost savings” because, as both Mr. Ott and Professor Hobbs explained, “the commitment of capacity at a higher reserve level will tend to decrease energy market prices.” Id. at 74 (citing Ott Affidavit at 23-26). Reducing the reserves procured, as this filing proposes, should be expected to have the opposite result across each of those measures: a lower capacity charge, and higher energy costs, higher scarcity costs, and a higher probability of loss of load. The filing does not analyze that trade.

APPENDIX B — ADDITIONAL IMPLEMENTATION ISSUE:
ATTACHMENT DD-4, SECTION IV(b)
B1. Attachment DD-4, section IV(b) governs the amount of Eligible New Large Load that must be available to provide Interim Resource Adequacy Service, determined after the Third Incremental Auction for each Delivery Year. It does not determine the amount removed from the auction procurement quantity. That reduction is made by the Excluded Amounts definitions and applied in Attachment DD, section 5.10(a).

B2. The provision does not express its calculation unambiguously. Two features leave the formula unclear.

B3. The placement of the divisor. In subsections (i) and (ii) the bracket encloses a comparison — capacity “is less than” the requirement — the division by the Pool-wide Accredited UCAP Factor follows the bracket, and a clause then measures the result against the Eligible New Large Load Amounts. In subsection (iii) the bracket encloses “equals or exceeds,” the division again follows a completed comparison, and nothing follows it. A comparison yields no quantity that can be divided. At least three readings are available: the divisor applies to the difference between the two quantities, to the requirement alone, or to nothing at all in subsection (iii).

B4. The units on each side, and the factor used. Section V(b) converts Bring Your Own New Capacity and Allocated RBP UCAP MW to load by dividing by the Forecast Pool Requirement, and both Excluded Amounts definitions convert load to capacity “by using the Forecast Pool Requirement.” Section IV(b) instead applies the Pool-wide Accredited UCAP Factor, and measures the result against the Eligible New Large Load Amounts, which section V(b) defines as registered peak load less an offset — a quantity denominated in load. The Commission has described the Forecast Pool Requirement as reflecting “PJM’s installed capacity requirement in Unforced Capacity (UCAP) terms,” and as applied to a Demand Resource’s Nominated Value to yield unforced capacity. PJM Interconnection, L.L.C., 186 FERC ¶ 61,080 at PP 2, 9 (2024). The Forecast Pool Requirement converts load to capacity; the Pool-wide Accredited UCAP Factor converts installed capacity to unforced capacity.

B5. The Reliability Requirement is also unqualified. Section IV(b) refers to “the PJM Region Reliability Requirement determined prior to the Third Incremental Auction” without stating whether that quantity is gross or net of the Excluded Amounts, and without stating whether the comparison is made on a regional basis or net of the obligations of FRR Entities. Attachment DD, section 5.4(c)(2)(i) states a comparison of the same shape — the updated Reliability Requirement against total capacity committed — and there qualifies the requirement as “inclusive of any adjustments associated with Excluded RTO New Large Load Amounts or Excluded LDA New Large Load Amounts.”

B6. PJM should state the calculation and its units expressly on the tariff sheets: what quantity the Pool-wide Accredited UCAP Factor operates on; the units on each side of the comparison; why that factor rather than the Forecast Pool Requirement; whether the
