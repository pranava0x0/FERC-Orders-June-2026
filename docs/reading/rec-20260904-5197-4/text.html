Under what might be termed a “tough love” approach, PJM could have confined itself to the responsibilities that are clearly at the federal wholesale level —assessing whether the aggregate new large load reported by PJM-jurisdictional entities is matched by aggregate new capacity, procuring against any gap through the RBP, and directing zonal load reductions when the gap persists during grid stress. This would leave the states, exercising their jurisdiction over the retail service, to define, coordinate, and enforce customer-level procurement and curtailment rules.

Under an approach that truly maximized “cooperative federalism,” PJM could instead have convened with state authorities to align definitions and implementation mechanisms before filing. While this would be challenging, it would have given load and capacity providers a more coherent foundation on which to contract-- with greater certainty, lower cost and lower risk. Instead, the IRAS Filing has waded into matters that are at best gray areas for federal jurisdiction and at worst squarely within state jurisdiction. Through the Large Load and New Large Load definitions and the Large Load Registry, PJM has taken on the task of deciding whether a retail load is new, whether it is one facility or several, and whether—and how much— new capacity it has procured, rather than simply ensuring that the aggregate load and aggregate new capacity is reported to PJM properly and accurately. PJM’s discussion makes clear that it intends load added through a facility’s ramp after June 1, 2027, to be treated as “New Large Load.” The Large Load Registry would include the in-service date for each increment of load, as well as the facility’s peak load and ramp schedule, and PJM describes the in-service date of each increment as critical to determining IRAS applicability. The proposed tariff language, however, does not clearly implement that intent.

Clause (b) of the New Large Load definition applies to incremental load that enters service after June 1, 2027, and increases the load at a site that, before that increment, did not meet the Large Load definition. Read literally, that language appears to capture the increment that causes a previously smaller site to cross the 50 MW threshold, but not subsequent increments at a site that already meets the Large Load definition. It also does not clearly state whether the New Large Load consists only of the qualifying increment or the entire registered peak load of the site.

That ambiguity has direct consequences for Equinix. A facility energized in 2023, 2024, or 2025 may continue to ramp after June 1, 2027, under a retail service agreement that was negotiated, studied, and executed years earlier. PJM’s discussion indicates that it intends to treat the post-June 1, 2027, portion of that ramp as new, even though the utility may already have committed to serve the facility’s full ramp. Yet the proposed tariff does not clearly identify which increments would be treated as New Large Load or how the corresponding MW obligation would be calculated. Equinix therefore cannot determine with confidence the amount of BYONC it would need to procure. The Commission should require PJM to clarify the definition and, more importantly, exclude ramp increments covered by binding retail service agreements executed before the IRAS Filing.

Even the “clear” cases of new load raise open questions: must a New Large Load procure capacity equal to its ultimate peak load contribution, or the design capacity it originally requested from its Electric Distributor? Must it procure that full amount from the first day of service, or may it ramp its procurement alongside its ramping demand? PJM’s proposal to collect load and BYONC ramp schedules, and to permit BYONC in excess of a load’s current Eligible New Large Load Amount, suggests that procurement may track expected growth. But the proposed tariff does not say how PJM will translate those schedules into the registered peak load used to calculate the facility’s IRAS obligation. Customers served by a vertically integrated Electric Distributor are left with this question: has that utility’s procurement of qualifying new capacity through its own supply plan already satisfied some or all of the customer’s BYONC obligation?

PJM has relied on the Large Load Adjustment to assess the magnitude and timing of large load growth that it contends requires the measures proposed here. Yet neither Equinix nor its Electric Distributor can determine how individual facilities are reflected in that adjustment. Equinix knows what information it provides to its Electric Distributor, but not what the Electric Distributor ultimately submit to PJM. PJM, in turn, has made adjustments to the Electric Distributor submissions before the Large Load Adjustments are finalized in a forecast. PJM explained in the stakeholder process that the Large Load Adjustments cannot identify how an individual load site is reflected or whether or not it is included in the final load forecast. That opacity now has direct consequences because customers cannot determine which facilities and ramp increments PJM will treat as in service by June 1, 2027, and therefore would be outside the definition of New large Load, and which PJM will subject to IRAS. B.  PJM SHOULD NOT BE ALLOWED TO VIOLATE TERMS AND
CONDITIONS OF EXISTING RETAIL SERVICE CONTRACTS
PJM is a world-leading wholesale market operator, but data centers (and other retail load) are not wholesale customers- just as all fasteners are not nails, notwithstanding the perspective of the proverbial carpenter. The further PJM strays from its wholesale market swim lane, the more numerous and severe the problems it inadvertently creates.

PJM’s proposed Large Load and New Large Load definitions would attach new resource- adequacy and curtailment consequences to retail load arrangements negotiated under existing state-jurisdictional service frameworks. Yet PJM did not vet the details of those definitions through a stakeholder process or meaningfully address their effect on already-energized facilities and contractual ramp schedules that utilities have studied and agreed to serve. PJM has taken its rules for Qualified Facilities and proposed to apply them to retail large loads for purposes of siting and sizing, apparently presuming that the same considerations that apply to power supply must apply equally to retail load, and that doing so was necessary to prevent gaming of the requirements PJM would like to impose on those retail loads. In doing so, PJM has strayed into a thicket without a map, threatening to infringe upon existing retail contracts and the financial expectations built on them—including retail service contracts for data centers that have operated for years or, in many cases, for decades.

1. A NEW ADDITION DOESN’T MAKE THE WHOLE HOUSE
NEW: INCREMENTAL LOAD THAT WOULDN’T ITSELF
COMPRISE A NEW LARGE LOAD SHOULD NOT CAUSE
EXISTING LOAD TO BE RECATEGORIZED
Adding a small increment of new load to an existing facility should not recategorize that existing load—or the increment itself, if it is below the 50 MW threshold—as a new large load. This is especially true where existing contracts already studied and addressed the impacts of the

fully ramped-up project, but it is also true for existing facilities that are not changing their base operations or characteristics and are merely adding capacity that would not, on its own, meet the definition of “Large Load.”

That incremental capacity must of course be studied and addressed in the appropriate retail service process and contracts, but adding it should not transform a facility that may have operated for decades into a “Large Load” itself—potentially disrupting its existing contracts and financial expectations, and unnecessarily pulling a small increment of new capacity into the IRAS framework when that increment would not, on its own, constitute a new large load.

2. THE WRONG TOOL FOR THE WRONG JOB:  DATA CENTERS
CANNOT FIT A QF-BASED PROCRUSTEAN BED
PJM has settled on a one-mile radius to aggregate facilities as a single load entity under its definition of Large Load, proposing this rule without regard to existing data center clustering practices or the reasons—wholly unrelated to energy—for those practices. Data centers cluster for legitimate commercial reasons: locating them near one another creates economic value by improving latency, the reliability of data interconnection, and easy access to other customers’ or business partners’ computational services, data, networks, and equipment.13

The one-mile rule can convert incremental growth at a longstanding, separately developed group of legacy facilities into “New Large Load” solely because PJM retroactively aggregates those facilities into a newly defined single electrical site. In Ashburn’s "Data Center Alley," for example, well over a hundred datacenters are located in close proximity. Any one- mile radius drawn in this area will capture dozens of facilities, many operated by the same

III.   CONCLUSION
For the reasons discussed herein, the Commission should reject the IRAS Filing or, at minimum, require PJM to: (1) grandfather load and contractual ramps under final retail service agreements before August 13, 2026; (2) eliminate or make rebuttable the one-mile aggregation rule as applied to independently developed legacy facilities; (3) provide Large Loads notice and an opportunity to correct Large Load Registry data before PJM relies on it; and (4) credit the PJM-determined value of qualifying, customer-sponsored Peak Shaving Adjustments without double counting.
