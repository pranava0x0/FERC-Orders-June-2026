II. 
COMMENTS 
A. 
Board Makeup, Selection, and Independence  
 
Many commentators across the spectrum support changes to the PJM Board makeup and 
selection process. One relatively straightforward change is extending the term of individual 
Board members. This was proposed or supported by Mr. Finkel,14 Mr. Mills,15 and Ms. Philips,16 
among others. PPL Electric agrees with extending the Board term.17 Relatedly, there was some 
discussion of a preference for Board members who live in the region.18 This, however, must be 
tied to loosening of rules restricting potential Board members from serving because of business 
connections to PJM Members. In other words, knowledgeable, qualified people dedicated to the 
success of PJM and the PJM markets are exactly the people who are excluded by the current con-
flicts rules.19 One option for resolving this would be to move from a two-year stay out to a one-
year stay out.20 PPL Electric supports this commonsense change, and would add a slight prefer-
ence for Board candidates with some geographic connection to the region, so long as it does not 
impact candidate quality.21

 More contentious is the question of who selects the Board. Some state representatives 
have proposed  a significant voting role for state representatives on the PJM Nominating Com-
mittee, including a proposal for three voting seats.22  Mr. Finkel defended that proposal at the 
technical conference as  designed to achieve “rough parity” with other interests represented on 
the Committee.23 Others supported some form of state representation in the nomination process, 
although not necessarily the same structure, number of seats, or level of voting authority.24 Left 
unsaid at the technical conference is the fact that roughly half of the existing PJM Members al-
ready represent customers or customer-aligned interests (including state actors like consumer ad-
vocates voting in the End Use Customer sector and municipal entities voting in the Electric Dis-
tribution Sector) rather than “private interests.” This means that companies investing billions of 
dollars in PJM would be left with at most three, and more likely two (Generation Owners and 
Transmission Owners), of ten seats—a materially reduced role in contested votes. This is not a 
recipe for an independent Board, nor one likely to establish market rules that invite the signifi-
cant investment in the transmission and generation systems necessary to solve the resource ade-
quacy crisis.

PPL Electric offers a few additional considerations in this discussion:  
• 
The states, acting through OPSI, a governor’s collaborative, or any other group, 
already exercise significant influence at FERC. They are already able to comment 
on any PJM filing (or any other filing) and make clear their own interests and 
opinions. If they are persuasive, they can stop a PJM proposal they object to from 
moving forward. The states also have section 206 authority, including to propose 
an alternative to PJM’s proposal.  
• 
“Jump ball” filings are legally uncertain, change the nature of section 205 filings, 
and at best empower the Commission and its staff rather than their holders. The 
law on “jump ball” filings is, at best, undeveloped. While they have worked in 
other regions in extremely limited circumstances, as envisioned here, they are a 
recipe for litigation. PJM’s filings, especially in key areas like the capacity mar-
ket, move tens of billions of dollars. If the Commission is awarded the ability to 
“select” which section 205 filing it (the Commission) prefers, the proponents of 
the losing proposal have every incentive to object, forcing the region to wait for 
months or years as the process flows through the courts. This is not a recipe for 
market stability or investment. It also turns the Commission from a regulatory 
body deciding section 205 filings pursuant to law, to the decider of which

D. 
Public Interest 
 
Finally, PPL Electric offers comment on proposals to incorporate a “public interest” 
standard into PJM’s governing documents or mission statement.49 Although that concept may ap-
pear straightforward at a high level, it raises significant questions unless carefully tied to PJM’s 
existing statutory obligations, governing documents, and Commission-approved responsibili-
ties.50 
 
Standing alone, a “public interest” mandate is vague and potentially difficult to adminis-
ter. It could mean anything that a particular advocate believes to be of value to its interests or its 
own view of the public’s needs. This inherently subjective standard risks unnecessarily

confusing PJM’s mission. PJM is a public utility—by some measures the largest and most im-
portant in the world—it is not a government actor. PJM is charged by existing statutory language 
and a member charter to operate the bulk electric system reliably and to administer efficient 
competitive wholesale energy markets at just and reasonable rates. It is also regulated by the 
Commission, which has its own legal standards to apply, and which is the proper democratically 
accountable actor. No matter how potentially beneficial, FERC cannot simply instruct PJM to 
adopt a different mandate not found in statute or the governing documents.51  
 
Second, because PJM is a stakeholder-driven organization, and many of those stakehold-
ers are themselves government organizations (PJM governors, OPSI and public utility commis-
sions, legislators, consumer advocates, municipalities, environmental protection organizations, 
etc.) with their own public interest mandates, adding “public interest” to PJM’s mandate intro-
duces the potential for deep conflict. If the PJM Board is supposed to act in the public interest, 
but in its independent judgment decides that standard calls for a different action than a gover-
nor’s representative or consumer advocate urges (when they are also acting in the public inter-
est), how should it resolve the matter? What if there is a conflict between the “public interest” 
and reliability? Or with just and reasonable rates? Introducing new legal concepts to complex le-
gal entities like PJM, even seemingly benign ones, is problematic and should be avoided.  
 
Third, “public interest” has a specific meaning in the context of the FPA incompatible 
with imposing a public interest mandate on PJM. It is part and parcel of the Mobile-Sierra doc-
trine by which FPA contracts may not be disturbed by FERC unless those contracts are found 
contrary to the public interest. This has been interpreted by the court to mean not anti-

17)
