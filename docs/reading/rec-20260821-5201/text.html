II. COMMENTS
A. Board Makeup, Selection, and Independence

Many commentators across the spectrum support changes to the PJM Board makeup and selection process. One relatively straightforward change is extending the term of individual Board members. This was proposed or supported by Mr. Finkel,14 Mr. Mills,15 and Ms. Philips,16 among others. PPL Electric agrees with extending the Board term.17 Relatedly, there was some discussion of a preference for Board members who live in the region.18 This, however, must be tied to loosening of rules restricting potential Board members from serving because of business connections to PJM Members. In other words, knowledgeable, qualified people dedicated to the success of PJM and the PJM markets are exactly the people who are excluded by the current con- flicts rules.19 One option for resolving this would be to move from a two-year stay out to a one- year stay out.20 PPL Electric supports this commonsense change, and would add a slight prefer- ence for Board candidates with some geographic connection to the region, so long as it does not impact candidate quality.21

 More contentious is the question of who selects the Board. Some state representatives have proposed  a significant voting role for state representatives on the PJM Nominating Com- mittee, including a proposal for three voting seats.22  Mr. Finkel defended that proposal at the technical conference as  designed to achieve “rough parity” with other interests represented on the Committee.23 Others supported some form of state representation in the nomination process, although not necessarily the same structure, number of seats, or level of voting authority.24 Left unsaid at the technical conference is the fact that roughly half of the existing PJM Members al- ready represent customers or customer-aligned interests (including state actors like consumer ad- vocates voting in the End Use Customer sector and municipal entities voting in the Electric Dis- tribution Sector) rather than “private interests.” This means that companies investing billions of dollars in PJM would be left with at most three, and more likely two (Generation Owners and Transmission Owners), of ten seats—a materially reduced role in contested votes. This is not a recipe for an independent Board, nor one likely to establish market rules that invite the signifi- cant investment in the transmission and generation systems necessary to solve the resource ade- quacy crisis.

D. Public Interest

Finally, PPL Electric offers comment on proposals to incorporate a “public interest” standard into PJM’s governing documents or mission statement.49 Although that concept may ap- pear straightforward at a high level, it raises significant questions unless carefully tied to PJM’s existing statutory obligations, governing documents, and Commission-approved responsibili- ties.50

Standing alone, a “public interest” mandate is vague and potentially difficult to adminis- ter. It could mean anything that a particular advocate believes to be of value to its interests or its own view of the public’s needs. This inherently subjective standard risks unnecessarily

PJM is charged by existing statutory language and a member charter to operate the bulk electric system reliably and to administer efficient competitive wholesale energy markets at just and reasonable rates. It is also regulated by the Commission, which has its own legal standards to apply, and which is the proper democratically accountable actor. No matter how potentially beneficial, FERC cannot simply instruct PJM to adopt a different mandate not found in statute or the governing documents.51

Second, because PJM is a stakeholder-driven organization, and many of those stakehold- ers are themselves government organizations (PJM governors, OPSI and public utility commis- sions, legislators, consumer advocates, municipalities, environmental protection organizations, etc.) with their own public interest mandates, adding “public interest” to PJM’s mandate intro- duces the potential for deep conflict. If the PJM Board is supposed to act in the public interest, but in its independent judgment decides that standard calls for a different action than a gover- nor’s representative or consumer advocate urges (when they are also acting in the public inter- est), how should it resolve the matter? What if there is a conflict between the “public interest” and reliability? Or with just and reasonable rates? Introducing new legal concepts to complex le- gal entities like PJM, even seemingly benign ones, is problematic and should be avoided.

Third, “public interest” has a specific meaning in the context of the FPA incompatible with imposing a public interest mandate on PJM. It is part and parcel of the Mobile-Sierra doc- trine by which FPA contracts may not be disturbed by FERC unless those contracts are found contrary to the public interest. Comments: The states are not PJM Members and should not have a voting role in the stakeholder process (unless they become Members, similar to their role in SPP). That said, PPL Electric has consistently called for more state coordination with PJM—as sovereigns, regulators, generation planners, etc.58 Rather than engage in fights over vot- ing power, the states should focus on these areas within their jurisdiction.

20) Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a section 205 filing related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation?

X

Comments: PJM already regularly consults with OPSI, and PPL Electric supports con- tinued coordination with the states.  However, converting consultation into a mandatory prerequisite to a section 205 filing could slow PJM’s ability to act, create procedural disputes unrelated to the merits of the filing, and constrain PJM’s ability to exercise its independent judgment.  In addition, to the extent PJM does not have the relevant section 205 filing rights for certain transmission cost allocation matters, a mandatory OPSI con- sultation requirement would not provide a meaningful legal mechanism for influencing those filings.
