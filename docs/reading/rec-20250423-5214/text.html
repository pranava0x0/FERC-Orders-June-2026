COMMENT OF CONSUMERS’ RESEARCH
Pursuant to the Commission’s Order entered on February 20, 2025, at Docket Nos. EL25- 49-000 et al., Intervenor Consumers’ Research submits the following comment responding to the filings in this docket and addressing four key principles that should guide the Commission’s decision making when considering how to protect consumers and ensure reliable and affordable electricity in the context of large loads co-located at generating facilities. Consumers’ Research is an independent educational 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization whose mission is to increase the knowledge and understanding of issues, policies, products, and services of concern to consumers and to promote the freedom to act on that knowledge and understanding.1  The cost and reliability of energy is a critical issue for the organization because it affects not only household utility bills but also the cost of energy that is factored into every good and service that consumers purchase.

First, retail customers should not subsidize the costs of co-location of large loads. Consumers’ Research agrees with PJM that “participants involved in co-location arrangements

should pay the costs of any grid services they consume and the arrangements must be reliable and operationally manageable.” See PJM Answer at 4. And it is undisputed that “many co-located load configurations utilize grid support and consume ancillary services (including, for example, frequency regulation, black start service, and reactive service).” Id.

To avoid improper subsidization, the Commission and regional transmission organizations should consider both direct and indirect costs, including the following: infrastructure that is built to power the co-located load itself; existing and new or upgraded infrastructure, including generators and transmission, that is ostensibly for the benefit of regular consumers but that is made necessary by the allocation of existing base-load power to the co-located load; developments to ensure reliability and resource adequacy, including ancillary developments like backups and black start, that are necessary because of the co-located load’s impact on reliability; grid support that enables the co-located load to receive reliable power in case of the connected generator’s failure or shortfall; and potential rate increases resulting from decreased supply caused by co-location. In consideration of this, a co-located load’s access to, dependence on, and use of grid-supplied energy must be thoroughly accounted for when forming the co-located load’s rate structure. Second, retail customers should not bear the risks of co-location. The risks of co-location should not be distributed to or socialized among regular consumers. Co-location may require large upfront costs for infrastructure, and there is a risk that those costs will not be recovered (e.g., if the company that obtained the co-location fails). Another risk of co-location is that the co-located load could obtain the first right of access to reliable, dispatchable power, while regular consumers are left with less reliable resources like wind and solar.

To protect regular consumers from the risks of co-location, including the risk of stranded assets, co-located loads could be required to do the following as a precondition to the risks: make

long-term financial commitments that will insure against risks; agree to a take-or-pay clause that applies over a defined period of time; and paying for the development of additional reliable power generation to ensure that regular consumers maintain the same level of generation from reliable sources as they did before the co-location.

Third, co-location should not be allowed to undermine dispatchable power—including by serving as an alternative to using existing dispatchable, generating sources that will otherwise retire. According to the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (“NERC”), many states are now at risk of suffering critical shortfalls in electric power. The amount of money at issue to address this shortfall is enormous: construction costs alone are estimated to be in the trillions,2 and that does not even account for the indirect costs to consumers and the economy from bad policy choices. Early retirement and non-repair of dispatchable generating assets is shortening their useful lifespans, and there is a general failure to build new dispatchable generating assets. As Chair Christie stated, “I have repeatedly warned that America is facing a reliability crisis driven by the dangerous pace of retirements of dispatchable generation units and failure to build sufficient new generation.”3 It is important that co-location of certain resources not occur in a way that exacerbates (or fails to ameliorate) the reliability crisis facing many states. FERC should therefore consider several possible policies. For example, parties engaged in co-location arrangements could be required to maintain a specified reserve capacity of dispatchable power for use by ratepayers that are not part of the co-location. It is also critical that co-location not be allowed unless the parties involved, including the generator and the company seeking the co-location, have no targets or commitments for net zero or any related low-carbon goals. Co-located loads often demand 24/7 dispatchable power, so they should not be allowed to misleadingly claim that they are lowering their emissions by co-locating to a “clean” facility while simultaneously creating risks to existing dispatchable generation that is supplied to the grid. Net-zero commitments and related targets harm consumers by artificially weakening the market for dispatchable power.

In contrast, if a co-located load plans to connect a new generator that provides dispatchable power (e.g., a new fossil-fuel-powered generator) and will also increase grid reliability overall (rather than decrease it), then FERC should consider policies that allow that new generation to move ahead in the interconnection queue, in front of projects for non-dispatchable resources. Time-savings incentives may be important in this context, as one of the reasons that companies seek co-location is to avoid delays and start operating profitably as quickly as possible. Allowing dispatchable generation (that benefits consumers generally) to move forward in the queue is consistent with solving a problem identified by Interior Secretary Burgum, who identified the problem of the 2,600 GW of wind, solar and storage sitting in the interconnection queues as a major energy imbalance that could lead to brownouts or blackouts.4  And the Commission has adopted a rule allowing dispatchable generation to get priority in the queue.5

Fourth, co-location arrangements should be transparent to the public. Companies seeking co-location may try to benefit from special contracts that are not open to the public. Transparency will also help protect consumers from the risk that their power infrastructure will be built up, at the consumers’ long-term expense, for a co-location that eventually falls through when a company decides to withdraw its interconnection request and instead follow through on a duplicative request that it made somewhere else. Companies seeking co-location should also be required to disclose information about the extent and nature of any on-site backup generating facilities. Thank you for considering the comments of Consumers’ Research on this important issue. Ultimately, the key consideration must be promoting affordable and reliable electricity to consumers. The foregoing principles will help achieve that goal.
