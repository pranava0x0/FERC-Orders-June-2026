PROTEST AND COMMENTS OF CONSTELLATION ENERGY GENERATION, LLC
Pursuant to the Commission’s August 13, 2026 notice in the above-captioned proceeding, Constellation Energy Generation, LLC (“Constellation”) submits this protest and these comments addressing issues raised by the August 12, 2026 Proposal of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. to Establish Interim Resource Adequacy Service and A Large Load Registry to Address System Reliability (the “PJM Proposal,” “IRAS Proposal,” or “Proposal”).

The Commission should reject the PJM Proposal for multiple independent reasons. I. PJM’s Proposal Is Beyond The Commission’s Jurisdictional Reach
A. The Commission’s Jurisdiction Is Subject To Strict Statutory Limits
Section 201(b) of the FPA limits the Commission’s jurisdiction to “the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce and to the sale of electric energy at wholesale in interstate commerce.”37  The Commission cannot regulate “any other sale of electric energy.”38 In keeping with those formal limits, Section 201(a) declares that “Federal regulation … extend[s] only to those matters which are not subject to regulation by the States.”39  It also declares that federal regulation “is necessary in the public interest” only as to “that part of [the electric energy] business which consists of the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce and the sale of such energy at wholesale in interstate commerce.”40  Those declarations “cannot nullify a clear and specific grant of jurisdiction” elsewhere in the statute,41 but they are relevant as a guiding expression of congressional purpose where there is no clear and specific grant of jurisdiction and the Commission is asked to take a novel action.42

In FERC v. EPSA, the Supreme Court interpreted Section 201(b) and established a test for assessing whether the Commission has acted within the scope of its statutory jurisdiction.43  The Commission’s jurisdiction extends only “to rules or practices that directly affect the [wholesale] rate.” 44  And even if a Commission action has that necessary direct effect, the Commission nevertheless cannot intrude on a sphere that is “reserv[ed]” for the states—namely, “retail electricity sales.”45  Because the “wholesale and retail markets in electricity … are not hermetically sealed from each other,” the fact that a Commission action merely affects the retail sphere is not enough to create a jurisdictional problem.46  Conversely, mere effects on wholesale rates are not enough to make a Commission action jurisdictionally permissible:  “FERC cannot take an action transgressing th[e] limit” on intruding on the retail sphere “no matter how direct[] or dramatic” that action’s “impact on wholesale rates” may be.47  Thus, the Commission cannot “specif[y] terms of sale at retail,” which “is a job for the States alone,” even though such a specification could undoubtedly have a significant effect on wholesale rates.48

1. PJM’s Proposal Impermissibly Intrudes On The States’ Regulatory

The Proposal impermissibly takes a federal reliability function—telling distribution utilities how much load must be shed during an emergency—and transforms it into a federal regulatory regime governing which categories of retail customers must procure generation, which retail customers must accept curtailment if they do not, and how those curtailed customers are to be compensated.

50. The endpoint for PJM is a market that is continually in a spiraling crisis.  Ongoing interventions drive resource exit and eliminate the RPM as a viable pathway for new resource entry, which drives new resource shortfalls which beget further intervention, further eroding confidence in the market.  This outcome will erode reliability over the long run.  It will erode reliability because ad hoc interventions are a poor solution to resolving resource adequacy shortfalls.  They are slow to implement, frequently involve substituting inferior alternatives (such as RMR resources or IRAS curtailment) for real capacity, carry the risk of unforeseen or unintended consequences compared to the tried-and-true design of the market, and are often subject to ongoing legal risk.  Repeated reliance on these measures raises the risk that something will go wrong – IRAS loads do not curtail as expected, non-Capacity Performance compliant RMR resources fail to perform as hoped, RBP resources are delayed, either or both is thrown out on legal review – with ongoing elevated physical resource adequacy risk for all customers being the ultimate consequence.

A core feature of this type of world is ongoing market exit of relatively low-cost existing resources that are nonetheless needed for maintaining resource adequacy. 52. The forces moving PJM in the direction of the Credibility Trap have been operating for several years now and some of the interventions driving it have already occurred.  And PJM’s credibility as a steward of a stable, non-discriminatory, investable market for capacity has already been badly damaged.  Put simply, PJM is already deeply mired in the Credibility Trap.  Without modification, the IRAS proposal will drive substantial further market erosion for the reasons discussed above which will further undermine resource adequacy and increase costs in PJM.  This is particularly the case because the IRAS proposal will become a permanent part of the tariff and even if its implementation is suspended due to changing market conditions it can be repeatedly re- activated later if market conditions again become tight.  Thus, the IRAS proposal is a permanent “heads I win, tails you lose” policy from the perspective of existing resources.

53. But strong, prompt intervention by the Commission in the proceeding could put PJM on a path to ultimately escaping the Credibility Trap.  The modifications I discuss in other sections of this affidavit – namely moving IRAS curtailments back in the curtailment order and reflecting them in energy prices and reflecting all new large load in demand – are necessary to this but not sufficient in my view.  As discussed above, the BYONC mechanism is ultimately highly corrosive to markets and is a major contributor to the confluence of factors driving PJM towards the spiral of crisis.  Ideally the Commission should direct PJM to remove the BYONC mechanism from its proposal.  If the Commission declines to recommend a full removal of the BYONC mechanism from PJM’s proposal, it should consider mechanisms that limit its impact on the broader market, such as requiring resources entering the market under BYONC arrangements to comply with the Minimum Offer Price Rule which would preserve a new entry price signal for the broader RPM market.  The issues discussed in the PJM White Paper are broad and not limited only to the IRAS proposal, but strong action by the Commission to preserve markets in this proceeding will send a strong signal to investors that markets remain viable over the long term.
