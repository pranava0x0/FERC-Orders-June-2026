UNITED STATES OF AMERICA 
BEFORE THE
FEDERAL ENERGY REGULATORY COMMISSION


PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms				Docket No. AD26-7-000


POST-CONFERENCE COMMENTS OF THE R STREET INSTITUTE

Introduction

On July 23, 2026, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC or Commission) convened a technical conference on PJM governance.[fn:1] On July 30, 2026, the Commission issued a notice inviting post-technical conference comments and requesting responses to twenty specific questions.[fn:2] Kent Chandler participated in the technical conference and pre-filed a statement for the R Street Institute (RSI).[fn:3] We appreciate the opportunity to further contribute to this discussion.

PJM’s coordinated operation of the regional grid has produced substantial benefits for customers in the fourteen jurisdictions served, maintaining reliability and enabling more efficient use of generation and transmission resources. The Commission’s inquiry in this docket concerns the processes by which PJM and its Members reach decisions. Reform of those processes should proceed with the benefits in view. These comments identify principles against which alternative governance arrangements can be judged and apply them to the questions the Commission has posed.

The Governance Debate So Far

The pre-conference filings split over whether the Members Committee should retain its approval right over PJM’s section 205 filings. Constellation, in the Generation Owner sector, would leave approval rights alone. PJM already holds section 205 rights over the most contested matters, it argued, and where PJM has not acted approval rights were not the obstacle.[fn:4] PPL, a transmission owner, said the founding allocation of substantive responsibilities to the Members Committee was a mistake and urged that stakeholder processes be made advisory. The Pennsylvania PUC, not a PJM member, argued that the Board should be clear of a Members Committee check to ensure independence, while states should be provided a path to advance consensus proposals. Three transmission owners submitted pre-conference filings and all three asked that the approval right be removed; three generation owners filed and all three wanted to retain the right as is. Ten filings came from state officeholders and state agencies, nine of them holding no vote at the Members Committee. Of the seven that addressed the approval right, none asked that it be kept as is.

A generating plant, once built, cannot easily be moved, and its owner is exposed to the rules governing the grid and market where it sits.[fn:5] Merchant generators hold no section 205 filing rights over PJM rules, nor do they have captive customers to recover investments from. Their protection within PJM comes primarily through the Members Committee sector-weighted vote, where two sectors unified against a proposal can defeat it. Investor confidence in long-lived assets depends on how easily the rules can be revised, and the approval right is what makes such revision costly. All market participants lacking regulated rate recovery, not just merchant generators, will consider rule stability before making irreversible commitments.

The exposure argument does not distinguish among types of revenues. Rule stability protects both a return earned by operating well under the rules and a return produced by a flaw in them. Which of the two a proposed rule change would threaten is often what stakeholders debate over. What a governance rule can do is register how widely a proposed change is supported.

A section 205 filing must show only that what it proposes is just and reasonable; a section 206 proceeding must also demonstrate that existing arrangements are not. The difference matters because a section 205 filing can displace an existing rule without showing that the rule is deficient. Reserving the lighter route for proposals with broad support leaves the heavier route for proposals lacking broad support.[fn:6] Research on sector-weighted voting has not found that any one constituency benefits from the process, with both supply side and demand side coalitions sometimes blocking proposals.[fn:7] Without Members Committee support, PJM can still bring a rule change to the Commission under section 206. 

Transmission assets, once built, cannot be moved either, but transmission owners nonetheless asked that the approval right be removed. Transmission owners hold section 205 filing rights under the Consolidated Transmission Owners Agreement, independent of the Members Committee. Their returns come from rates the Commission sets rather than from market outcomes, and investor confidence in transmission expansion rests on that rate treatment rather than on the stability of PJM’s market rules. Those filing rights were retained when the owners transferred operational control of their facilities to PJM.[fn:8]

State entities have constituencies that pay retail rates affected by PJM operations. Fifteen consumer advocate offices hold ex officio membership in the End-Use Customer sector and fourteen have a vote in the Members Committee, but they do not represent state energy offices or utility regulators. These other state officials may participate in stakeholder processes (such as the Independent State Agencies Committee), but they are not PJM members and hold no vote within the RTO. Lacking a direct role at PJM, states act where they hold the power. Kent Chandler in his pre-filed statement described states responding to unfavorable PJM outcomes by using siting and permitting authority, an eye-for-an-eye dynamic that leaves both sides worse off.[fn:9] The demand for a voice is a demand for a mechanism that resolves conflicts inside the process rather than outside.

The three sets of filings surveyed above rest on different grounds, and no single remedy addresses all three. Merchant generators defend rules they operate under and want changes to those rules to require broad support. Transmission owners primarily wish to defend the terms on which they transferred operational control of their facilities to PJM. State entities want a clearer role in a regional process directly affecting retail rates, in effect seeking to reclaim influence lost to the regional grid operator. 

Not every filing falls into one of the three groups. American Municipal Power, a public power agency in the Electric Distributor sector, says the current framework works as is. PJM’s market monitor and the Industrial Customer Coalition both argue in favor of keeping the Members Committee approval right, and the market monitor would go further, withdrawing PJM's existing section 205 rights over the capacity market. Delaware’s Public Advocate does not explicitly address the approval right, but proposes instead a sixth voting sector for state entities and a state committee to reach the Board when no proposal clears the Members Committee threshold. 

The alignment of positions carries information about the questions themselves. Where a rule serves an objective that was fixed outside the stakeholder process, disagreement within the process becomes focused on means, on what works, and positions often cross sector lines. Where the objective itself is being chosen in the process, disagreement focuses on ends and positions follow the interests at stake. Security-constrained economic dispatch is the clearest case of the first kind. Reliability standards and approved tariff provisions, backed by hard-earned experience and well-established theoretical insights, set the standard for what dispatch must accomplish.[fn:10] PJM executes against that standard continuously and the results are measured. Disputes over dispatch are primarily arguments about how best to meet a requirement, not about which requirement to meet. Resource adequacy policies, including capacity market design, are the clearest case of the second. While fixed objectives such as a LOLE criterion of 1 day in 10 years can be articulated, there are multiple defensible approaches to achieving that standard, and they differ substantially in who bears the costs or receives payments.[fn:11] Meeting the criterion constrains the choice without making it. A vote among those interests records how large a coalition is. It does not establish that the allocation the coalition prefers is the right one.

The Commission’s questions divide along the same line. The appendix in the request for comments asks the filing-rights question twice, once for the energy and ancillary services markets and once for the regional planning process, and asks separately whether states should vote on “certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional transmission cost allocation.” No question contemplates a comparable state role in dispatch, congestion management, or reserve procurement. No pre-conference filing suggested such a state role. PJM’s own tables sort the same way.[fn:12] Of the nine failed votes PJM lists in its Table 2, at least six concern the capacity market or the interconnection process, where PJM's Table 5 shows its section 205 authority is not conditioned on Member approval. Pamela Quinlan, formerly chief of staff at the Commission, states that the Board already holds broad authority over the capacity market and can proceed under section 206 otherwise, and that the limiting factor has been willingness rather than authority. Constellation, opposing any expansion of PJM’s rights, identified two proceedings in which PJM went forward under section 206 without Member endorsement.[fn:13] Only one falls squarely within the Members Committee's conditioned domain, the reserve requirement revision of July 2024, and that is the example PJM builds its timing case on.

The two functions the Commission names do not fail in the same way. Resource adequacy sits with PJM under the Tariff and the Reliability Assurance Agreement, and the difficulty there is not member voting.[fn:14] PJM must decide how much to procure, at what cost, and borne by whom, while the state policymakers answerable to consumers for the costs have no vote in the process. Regional transmission cost allocation works differently. PJM’s table of section 205 filing rights by core function does not list it, and the Order No. 1920 compliance proceeding shows why: the PJM Transmission Owners are the transmission provider responsible for filing the region’s ex ante cost allocation method.[fn:15] The two routes converge on a defined state role in the two functions the Commission has named. Neither reaches dispatch. Dispatch executes against objectives fixed in reliability standards and approved tariff provisions, it does so repeatedly, with results anyone can measure. The two named functions have none of those three features. Part III applies that distinction to the Commission's twenty questions.

Responses to the Appendix Questions

R Street Institute is not a PJM member. Part II of these comments sets out the analysis several answers apply: where a rule serves an objective fixed outside the stakeholder process, disagreement runs to means and the process performs well; where the objective is chosen inside the process, the question allocates value among the participants, and their vote registers the size of a coalition rather than the soundness of the result.

PJM Board Independence


Questions/Comments
Yes
No
1)
Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years?
Yes


Comments: A longer term reduces how often a sitting member stands before the body that elects him. We do not think the prospect of reappointment seriously compromises independence, and we do not present this as a remedy for anything at issue in this docket. Under a fifteen-year ceiling, five-year terms would leave a member facing two reappointment decisions rather than four, which is a modest improvement at little cost. One drafting point matters more than the answer. The limit is written as a number of terms, not a number of years, so extending terms without amending it would raise maximum service to twenty-five years. If terms are extended, the ceiling should be restated in years.[fn:16]
2)
Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may serve (e.g., 10 years)?
Yes


Comments: A cap limits how long reappointment can matter to a Board member, which is the main independence concern in a re-electable seat. To be clear, a PJM Board member is already capped at either 15 years or 75 years of age, whichever comes first. It is possible that reducing the maximum term of a board member would yield some benefits. 
3)
Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a preference for some Board members that reside in the PJM region?

No

Comments: Residency is a proxy for exposure, not for knowledge. The Board's work is to resolve disputes among regional interests. Board members can acquire regional knowledge through staff, advisors, and the communication measures in questions 5 through 7. A residency preference, however, would build regional ties into the selection process itself.
4)
Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off period for Board members from two years to one year?

No

Comments: The two-year period addresses a real conflict-of-interest risk. A departing member's value to a future employer active in PJM markets is highest in the member's final year of service. If the prospect of that employment shades judgment, the cost falls on the parties to the member's last decisions. Shortening the period would ease Board recruitment, but recruitment can be addressed through compensation. Reducing the cooling-off period would move Board independence in the wrong direction.
5)
Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable more communication between the Board and stakeholders?
Yes


Comments: Existing restrictions can also keep useful information from reaching the Board. Relaxing those restrictions is worthwhile if communication is open to all sectors on comparable terms and participation is disclosed. The concern is unequal access, especially private access, not communication itself. Relaxation paired with disclosure meets that concern.
6)
In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the Board be required to explain its reasoning? 
Yes


Comments: An appointed Board should explain important decisions. Several proposals in this docket would move decisions toward the Board, which raises the value of knowing what the Board decided and why. Published votes and stated reasons would let members, states, and the Commission evaluate the Board's work and would improve the record supporting later section 205 filings. Among the Board measures in this appendix, this one does the most work.
7)
In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or available to the public?
Yes


Comments:  Some Board business can be public; some cannot. Sessions on process, planning assumptions, and stakeholder matters can be open at little cost. Sessions touching market-sensitive information, personnel, or litigation cannot be. The default should be open sessions with stated criteria for closure rather than closed sessions with occasional openings.
8)
Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest?

No

Comments: The “public interest” does not name a clear objective the PJM Board could apply. It would rather assign PJM the job of choosing among objectives that states, consumers, generators, and transmission owners actively dispute. These are issues PJM is least equipped to settle and least accountable for settling. In practice a public interest clause would be invoked by every side of every dispute. The likely effect would be to increase the political legitimacy concerns that motivate the Commission’s inquiry rather than empower it to better address those concerns.[fn:17]
9)
Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the Nominating Committee?

No

Comments: The proposal does not identify who a state representative would be, and the candidates are not interchangeable. A governor's designee would speak for one state among thirteen and the District of Columbia. An OPSI designee would hold a single seat on behalf of commissions whose interests diverge on the matters the Board later decides. Neither arrangement supplies a rule for converting divergent state positions into the single position the seat would express.

The committee's procedure adds a further difficulty. Candidates are vetted in confidence and are not disclosed to the Members Committee until thirty days before the annual meeting, so a state member would vote on a slate without having been able to consult the commissions on whose behalf the seat exists.[fn:18] State influence is better placed where a decision rule can carry it, as in questions 14 and 20.
10)
Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management compensation to their achievement?
Yes


Comments: Performance metrics make sense for functions with clear objectives. Dispatch performance, interconnection queue processing, planning cycle duration, and implementation deadlines can all be measured against external specifications, and compensation tied to them aligns management effort with the operator's core assignment. Compensation should not be tied to outcomes in contested domains, such as auction price levels or cost allocation shares, where a financial stake would give management an interest in distributive results.

Section 205 Filing Rights

Questions/Comments
Yes
No
11)
Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes only inform the Board?

No

Comments: Participants commit capital against rules they expect to persist. Where the Members Committee vote still gates PJM's filings, that vote is much of what makes an Operating Agreement term a premise a generation or storage owner can build against. Advisory models work where participants hold something else. Under the NEPOOL Participants Agreement, an ISO-NE market filing can be met by a stakeholder alternative placed before the Commission on equal terms, and NEPOOL sectors hold seats in board selection.[fn:19] Advisory status transplanted alone is not the same reform.
The question also runs across domains the record separates. Of the nine failed votes in PJM's Table 2, at least six were capacity market or interconnection matters where PJM holds filing rights under its own Table 5, and in the resource adequacy and large load proceedings it filed. Only one arose where the Members Committee vote conditions PJM's section 205 authority. Questions 12 through 15 ask this at a resolution the record can answer.
12)
Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over the energy market and ancillary services unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee?

No

Comments: The condition does least harm where it remains. Energy and ancillary services rules operate closest to objectives fixed outside the stakeholder process—reliable operation at least cost on the system as configured. Disagreement there tends to run to means rather than ends, and positions cross sector lines. The record matches: across seventy-five senior committee votes from 2023 through mid-2026, one failed vote fell in this domain. PJM's reserve market example shows a slow process, and slowness has costs; it does not show a blocked filing, and PJM retained section 206 throughout. PJM observes that other RTOs file these matters without member approval. Other regions also carry checks PJM does not—under the NEPOOL Participants Agreement, ISO-NE's market filings can be met by a stakeholder alternative placed before the Commission on equal terms—so the comparison cannot rest on filing authority alone. Removing this condition would trade a check that rarely binds for speed the record does not show to be missing.
	13)
	Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over Regional Transmission Expansion Planning (RTEP) unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee?

No

Comments: Planning is where the operator's choices come closest to choosing ends. RTEP rules shape which needs are recognized, which projects are selected, who builds, and who pays. The distributive stakes exceed those in the energy market, and the positions filed in this docket sort accordingly, as Part II shows. A two-thirds vote of the claimants is a poor settler of those rules. But giving PJM unilateral filing authority does not by itself solve that legitimacy problem, because PJM answers to no residual claimant for the costs its plan allocates. What makes planning tractable is external specification: the Commission's planning rules supply the objectives, and the state instruments in questions 14 and 20 supply the warrant for what remains contested. We answer no because the proposal operates on the wrong variable, not because the current condition performs well. 
If the Commission nonetheless moves filing authority to PJM, the reason-giving in question 6 and the instruments in questions 14 and 20 become necessary complements.
14)
Should the Organization of PJM States Inc (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights over resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a supermajority of OPSI agrees on an alternative proposal?
Yes


Comments: Resource adequacy and regional cost allocation are choices among ends, and states hold the warrant to make them. A jump-ball right adds authority without adding a veto because it carries its own decision rule: the Commission chooses. The Commission may also order something outside both filings, so neither filer's preferred bargain is safe, which presses both toward proposals that can stand on their own; the comparable NEPOOL right has been exercised sparingly for the same reason.
One drafting point. Section 205 rights over the Tariff's regional cost allocation provisions sit with the PJM transmission owners rather than with PJM.[fn:20] For that subject the right should be drafted against the relevant filings, whoever makes them.
	15)
	Should the Members Committee have “jump ball” filing rights over the portions of the tariff where PJM has 205 filing rights when a supermajority of stakeholders approves an alternative proposal?

No

Comments: The difference between this proposal and question 14 is who stands behind the alternative filing. OPSI represents state officials accountable to the customers who bear the region's costs; the Members Committee represents market participants with stakes in the rules being contested (both buyers and sellers). In the domains where PJM holds the filing right—the capacity market above all—allocation among those participants is much of what the rules decide, and Part II sets out why breadth of support in that body registers the size of a coalition rather than the soundness of a proposal. The concession is worth making: a jump ball is not a veto, the Commission decides, and an additional option before the Commission has some value. But the right would re-encumber, through the filing process, the domains the current assignment left with PJM. Members already place alternatives before the Commission through protests, section 206 complaints, and the stakeholder record itself.

Stakeholder Process

Questions/Comments
Yes
No
16)
Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered from two-thirds sector-weighted voting?

No

Comments: A voting threshold trades one cost against another. A higher threshold means members are less often bound by decisions they opposed. A lower threshold means the group reaches decisions at less cost to itself. Lowering the threshold buys the second and gives up the first.[fn:21] Which way the balance moves depends on how far members' interests diverge, and PJM's membership has grown large and heterogeneous—five sectors, 525 eligible voting members, and positions in this docket sorting by sector on the central questions. Rising divergence raises the cost of decisions imposed over objection, which argues for holding the threshold rather than lowering it. The main effect of lowering the threshold would be to let a smaller cross-sector coalition ratify distributive changes.
17)
Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing committees, also apply to lower-level committees?
Yes


Comments: Vote integrity should not depend on committee tier. Much of what reaches a senior committee arrives shaped by lower-level votes, and a bar that applies only at the top invites affiliate voting to move down. The rule should apply to any committee whose vote gates what advances. Purely deliberative task forces can be left alone.

State Participation

Questions/Comments
Yes
No
18)
In addition to OPSI, should there be a second state representative group consisting of governors’ representatives and/or state legislators’ representatives?

No

Comments: A second state organization would divide the state channel without adding authority. The state instruments worth having—the jump ball in question 14, the consultation in question 20—all require knowing who speaks for the states. A parallel body of governors' and legislators' representatives would hand PJM and the Commission two state voices to weigh whenever they disagree, with no rule for choosing between them. Governors and legislatures already hold their own instruments: appointment, confirmation, legislation, and the state proceedings PJM's rules eventually reach. If a governor's office wants a seat at the regional table, OPSI's own membership arrangements can accommodate it.
19)
Should state representatives have a formal voting role in the stakeholder process (limited to certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional transmission cost allocation) in addition to the consumer advocate votes?  

No

Comments: A vote inside the Members Committee gives states responsibility without power, and fundamentally misrepresents the roles states should play. The states’ efforts are best applied to addressing trade-offs among values rather choosing among implementation techniques. State commissions also adjudicate the retail consequences of the same rules; casting stakeholder votes on those rules entangles the adjudicator in the record it will later review. Questions 14, and 20 give states authority attached to a decision rule without placing them inside the body whose disputes are at issue. A limited state vote therefore creates more problems than it solves.
20)
Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a section 205 filing related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation?
Yes


Comments: A consultation requirement is workable if it is bounded. Requiring PJM to consult OPSI before resource adequacy filings, on a stated schedule, improves the information in the filing at little cost to speed. The Order 1920 engagement period shows what happens without bounds: the negotiation over regional cost allocation between the transmission owners and the state entities has run through repeated extensions and, as of this summer, a request for mediation, because its design contains no rule for non-agreement. The consultation requirement should therefore have a firm deadline and should not delay the filer's ultimate decision. The same drafting point from the response to Q14 applies. Because the section 205 rights over regional cost allocation sit with the transmission owners, a consultation duty aimed at those filings must be addressed to the owners rather than to PJM.




Conclusion

RSI respectfully requests that the Commission consider the comments herein.
