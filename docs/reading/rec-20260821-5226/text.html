AMERICAN MUNICIPAL POWER, INC. 
In accordance with the July 30, 2026 Notice Requesting Post-Conference 
Comment issued by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission” or 
“FERC”),1 American Municipal Power, Inc. (“AMP”) submits its comments regarding the 
issues addressed at the July 23, 2026 technical conference in the above-captioned 
docket. AMP’s Vice President of Transmission & Regulatory Affairs, Steven Lieberman, 
was a conference panelist, and AMP appreciates this opportunity to further address these 
important issues. AMP’s comments focus on key topics from the conference and respond 
to the questions included in the appendix to the Notice.  
I. 
COMMENTS 
A. 
It is essential to identify the problems we are trying to solve. 
Governance reforms are essential for PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) as the 
region faces daunting resource adequacy and affordability challenges attributable in large 
measure to data center-driven load growth. Accomplishing the Commission’s goal of 
improving PJM’s ability “to address [its] operational and market needs in a timely and 
efficient manner,”2 however, first requires clearly identifying the aspects of PJM governance 
that have contributed to a lack of efficiency and effectiveness – and those that have not.

B. 
PJM could meaningfully improve the stakeholder process through 
better issue prioritization and other straightforward reforms. 
The technical conference evidenced significant agreement among the participants 
regarding key deficiencies in the stakeholder process, particularly a lack of strategic 
planning and issue prioritization by PJM.6 The concern includes PJM’s failure to schedule 
meetings on important issues in a way that promotes prompt resolution.7 It is within PJM’s 
power to address these problems without changes to the PJM governing documents, and 
PJM’s acknowledgement of these deficiencies and its expressed willingness to address 
them are encouraging. 
There also appeared to be general alignment over revisiting the existing voting 
structure of lower-level stakeholder committees. Providing corporate affiliates separate 
votes in these committees gives entities with multiple affiliates undue influence over the 
proposals that are endorsed for consideration by the higher-level committees, and, in turn, 
allows them to veto proposals that are more likely to garner consensus stakeholder 
support. A simple fix would be to apply the same sector-weighted voting approach used 
in the standing committees to lower-level committee voting. While this change would 
require the stakeholders to approve changes to Manual 34, the agreement expressed by 
panelists on this issue suggests that this may be achievable.

C. 
Enhanced state input is desirable. 
AMP supports enhancing state input into the PJM stakeholder process. While there 
are various proposed paths toward achieving this goal, increased participation by the 
Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”) under the current arrangements would be one 
simple, highly effective, and seemingly uncontroversial way to improve the ability of the 
states to make their voices heard. Resource constraints should not limit OPSI’s ability to 
provide its valuable perspective in PJM stakeholder debates, as OPSI determines its own 
budget, which is funded by PJM customers.  
Another effective way to bring an enhanced state perspective to PJM governance 
would be to revise the PJM Board qualification criteria by adding a requirement that at 
least one Board member have experience working for state government or a state 
regulatory commission. Focusing on Board qualification criteria seems more effective than 
assigning state representatives one or more seats on the PJM Nominating Committee. 
AMP looks forward to further engagement with the states’ representatives on approaches 
to ensuring that state perspectives are adequately considered in PJM governance. 
D. 
Concerns about PJM Board independence and transparency should 
be addressed. 
Although PJM’s existing governance framework strikes an appropriate balance 
between Board independence and RTO responsiveness to stakeholder views, concerns 
were expressed at the technical conference that the current framework does not ensure 
adequate Board independence. Those concerns should be addressed, with due

consideration given to the various potential responses. Revising PJM’s governing 
documents, for example, to state that the Board owes a duty to the public interest should

considered as part of a more holistic discussion. In considering any such changes,

independence is balanced against the need for responsiveness to stakeholder concerns,

E. 
FPA section 205 filing rights are not the problem. 
The technical conference included significant discussion of whether the PJM 
governing documents should be changed to eliminate the requirement for two-thirds 
sector-weighted approval by the Members Committee for certain PJM section 205 filings. 
Such revisions, however, are not necessary to improve PJM’s ability to address efficiently 
its operational and market needs because PJM’s authority with respect to making FPA 
section 205 filings is simply not the problem. 
A number of technical conference panelists – most prominently, those representing 
PJM itself – argued that giving PJM unconditioned FPA section 205 filing rights over the 
regional transmission planning protocol and the rules governing the energy and ancillary 
services (“EAS”) markets would improve PJM’s ability to address current challenges. But 
neither the PJM representatives nor any other panelist, persuasively explained how 
providing PJM with unconditioned FPA section 205 filing rights over transmission planning

The technical conference participants considered potential changes to the existing

Questions/Comments

No
1) 
Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years? 

Comments: The current three-year Board term does not impose a significant 
constraint on Board independence or on effective PJM governance. A three-year 
term, subject to re-election, gives Board members time to develop perspective on 
the issues facing the RTO, while providing stakeholders with an appropriate 
mechanism to promote RTO responsiveness. PJM’s Board term of three years, 
moreover, is consistent with the other RTOs, including MISO, ISO-NE, SPP, 
ERCOT, and Cal-ISO. NYISO has four-year terms.
2) 
Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member 
may serve (e.g., 10 years)? 

Comments: The Board has already adopted limits on the duration of a Board 
member’s service. As explained in PJM’s Board fact sheet, “[s]ervice is limited to 
five terms, and only candidates younger than age 75 are eligible.” Thus, terms 
are effectively limited to fifteen years. There is no need to change this structure. 
Arbitrary adoption of shorter term limits could deprive PJM and its stakeholders 
of qualified, experienced, and trusted Board members, with little or no RTO 
governance benefit.
3) 
Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a 
preference for some Board members that reside in the PJM region?

Comments: There should be no formal geographical preference for Board 
members to reside in the PJM region. The emphasis should be on selecting the 
best available candidates regardless of where they reside. To the extent that a 
prospective Board member has valuable experience with PJM or the PJM region, 
the Nominating Committee would presumably give appropriate weight to that 
experience in selecting Board candidates. 
4) 
Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling 
off period for Board members from two years to one year? 

Comments: It has not been demonstrated that the two-year cooling off period is a 
significant constraint on the ability to identify qualified Board candidates. Even if 
the two-year rule does have some such impact, this cooling off period remains an 
appropriate conflict of interest mitigation measure. If there is even an appearance 
of a conflict of interest, confidence in Board member independence could be 
significantly compromised. To the extent there is a valid concern that two years is 
too long, consideration should be given to a shorter one-year cooling off period, 
consistent with MISO’s approach, but the cooling off period should not be 
eliminated.
5) 
Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable 
more communication between the Board and stakeholders? 

Comments: There is no reason to modify any current rules pertaining to 
communication between the Board and stakeholders, as provided for in PJM’s 
Manual 34. Ex parte rules exist for good reason: to ensure that no person or 
stakeholder has an undue advantage or ability to influence a decision maker, in 
this case PJM’s Board of Managers. The PJM Board needs more transparency, 
not less.  
Relaxing the PJM Board’s ex parte rules may allow incumbent PJM transmission 
owners (individually or collectively) to exercise greater influence over PJM Board 
action than they can today. As the Independent Market Monitor for PJM has 
observed, transmission owners possess “a unique ability to affect the decision 
making of the PJM Board,”14 and allowing PJM transmission owners to meet 
behind closed doors with the PJM Board would only exacerbate such RTO 
independence concerns.
6) 
In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made 
public or the Board be required to explain its reasoning? 

Comments: Yes, transparency on all levels is severely lacking in PJM. Contrast 
that with the manner in which the boards operate at SPP and MISO, as described 
in Mr. Lieberman’s written statement. Board votes should be made public, 
meeting minutes published, and a requirement for the Board to explain its 
reasoning, including whether it reviewed alternative solutions beyond those

7) 
In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be 
open or available to the public? 

Comments: Other than meetings or sessions specifically convened to discuss 
confidential information, as would occur in executive session, Board meetings 
should be open to and available for the public. PJM should consider the MISO 
approach to board meetings, which allows the public to attend and listen to board 
and board committee meetings, followed by an opportunity for the public to make 
comments to the board or committee at the end of the meeting. Similarly, SPP has 
board meetings that are open to the public. 
8) 
Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that 
the responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest? 

Comments: As PJM stated at the July 23 technical conference, repeated in its pre-
conference statement, and includes on its website, “PJM operates a competitive 
wholesale electricity market and manages the high-voltage electricity grid to 
ensure reliability for more than 67 million people.” Given this mission, the interests 
of PJM as an organization already are generally aligned with the public interest, 
and a provision in PJM’s governing documents making this explicit could be 
appropriate. 
9) 
Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the 
Nominating Committee? 

Comments: Changes to improve state confidence in PJM’s decision-making could 
be useful. But the technical conference record and Mr. Lieberman’s own 
experience serving on the PJM Nominating Committee show that the absence of 
explicit state representation on that committee does not undermine effective PJM 
governance. Nominating Committee members do not appear to have shown a 
pattern of “voting their interests” in lieu of nominating the most qualified 
candidates, nor would providing state representatives with one or more votes on 
the Committee necessarily result in more Board candidates focused on state 
interests.   
A more effective way to help ensure that state interest perspectives are 
represented on the PJM Board could be to revise the Board qualifications. For 
example, the Operating Agreement could be revised to add a requirement that at 
least one Board member has experience working for state government or a state 
regulatory commission. While PJM maintains a list of required skills for Board 
members based on section 7.2 of the PJM Operating Agreement, there is currently 
no requirement for this specific experience on the PJM Board. This approach also 
avoids any potential complications associated with having non-Member state 
representatives on the Nominating Committee.

10) 
Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management 
compensation to their achievement? 

Comments: Presently, there is little or no insight into or accountability for PJM 
management’s performance. Tying performance evaluation and compensation to  
publicly available metrics would likely increase confidence in PJM’s decision 
making. But any such performance assessment framework must be carefully 
designed to avoid misaligned incentives. For example, metrics that simply promote 
speed in decision-making might encourage PJM management to act quickly on a 
given issue, at the expense of pursuing a better solution that takes more time to 
develop.

11) 
Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes 
only inform the Board? 

Comments: Currently the stakeholder process is advisory on matters solely 
contained within the Open Access Transmission Tariff, meaning stakeholder votes 
only inform the Board but do not bind the Board’s decision about whether to make 
an FPA section 205 filing and what to include in the filing.15 While PJM FPA

conference record, there is no reason to modify this requirement (or the sector-

effectiveness of PJM decision-making.

timely and efficient manner.”16 The need for Members Committee approval for

achieve consensus should not delay action by PJM. In such a situation, a filing 
under FPA section 206 is a reasonable way to put the issue before the 
Commission. Absent such exigent circumstances, a collaborative stakeholder 
process is likely to result in more well-informed and durable proposals to the 
Commission, and the current Members Committee approval requirement 
promotes such a process. 
The Members Committee approval requirement has the salutary benefit of helping 
ensure that the PJM decision-making process is independent of control by any 
market participant or class of participants. Eliminating the collective say that the 
PJM sectors currently have regarding proposed Operating Agreement 
modifications could provide a particular sector or sectors with the ability to exert 
undue influence on PJM.  
As with other issues, however, AMP is willing to consider changes to PJM’s FPA 
section 205 filings rights framework as part of a broader discussion of PJM 
governance.

Stakeholder Process 
Questions/Comments 
Yes
No
16) 
Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered 
from two-thirds sector-weighted voting? 

Comments: The two-thirds majority was established to drive a consensus building 
stakeholder process intended to result in well-informed, durable proposals for 
changes to the Operating Agreement. Lowering the sector-weighted voting 
approval threshold would diminish the effectiveness of this framework, which has 
worked well historically. Moreover, the Members Committee approves the vast 
majority of issues that come before it. While a lower approval threshold might 
result in easier passage of proposals on more contentious issues, there is no 
reason to think that it would improve the quality and durability of those proposals. 
Well-informed, consensus proposals are most helpful on contentious issues.
17) 
Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior 
standing committees, also apply to lower-level committees? 

Comments: As explained in Mr. Lieberman’s pre-conference statement,21 this is a 
relatively simple reform that could meaningfully improve the effectiveness of the 
PJM stakeholder process. The current practice of allowing affiliates to vote at the 
lower level committees gives disproportionate influence to entities with multiple 
affiliates by allowing them effectively to determine the proposals that are endorsed 
for consideration at higher-level committees. This practice interferes with the 
development of consensus proposals, can result in the endorsement of proposals 
that are unlikely to pass at senior-level committees,22 and raises RTO 
independence concerns to the extent that the RTO’s decision-making process is 
ultimately unduly influenced by a particular class of market participants.

record indicates that state regulators originally did not want a formal vote because 
they did not want to be in a position of having voted on PJM rules that then 
resulted in costs coming before them to allocate at the retail level. This concern – 
to the extent it is still operative – would presumably apply under any approach that 
provides states with formal voting rights. Providing state representatives with 
voting rights through PJM membership would have the benefit of avoiding major 
changes to the existing PJM stakeholder framework, while giving state 
representatives a more direct voice in PJM governance.
20) 
Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a 
section 205 filing related to resource adequacy and transmission 
cost allocation? 

Comments: A formal requirement for PJM to consult with OPSI is unwarranted, 
and, in any event, would not necessarily improve the likelihood of achieving 
durable resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation reforms. OPSI 
already participates in the stakeholder process, and funding is available for OPSI 
to allow it to participate even more effectively than it already does. Additionally, 
OPSI meets with the PJM Board several times a year. Finally, it is worth noting 
that the PJM transmission owners currently have FPA section 205 filing rights over 
regional transmission cost allocation, not PJM.
