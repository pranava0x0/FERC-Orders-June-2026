POST TECHNICAL CONFERENCE COMMENTS 
 
 
Pursuant to the Commission’s invitation extended on July 23, 2026, to provide post-
technical conference comments in this docket, and the Notice Requesting Post-Conference 
Comment issued July 30, 2026, the Illinois Commerce Commission (“ICC”) does hereby provide 
these post-technical conference comments for consideration.  The ICC is the public utility 
commission in the State of Illinois.  Illinois has utilities in both PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. 
(“PJM”) and the Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc. (“MISO”).  The ICC 
submitted a notice of intervention in this docket on August 5, 2026, so it is an interested party. 
The ICC appreciates the Commission convening the technical conference on July 23, 
2026 (“Technical Conference”) and provides these comments in support of the needed reforms to 
improve PJM’s Board of Directors (“PJM Board” or “Board”) independence, more effective PJM 
leadership in the stakeholder process, increased state input into the process, and clear direction 
that PJM should be working in the public interest.

I. 
COMMENTS 
 
A. The Commission Should Direct PJM to More Effectively Lead the RTO and its 
Stakeholders to Address High Priority Issues in a Timely Manner.  
In Order No. 2000, the Commission set forth the minimum functions and characteristics 
for the formation of regional transmission organizations (“RTOs”).1  Notably, the Commission 
did not impose any restrictions on state agencies participation and encouraged the states to 
participate in the formation of the RTOs.2  The Commission took a flexible approach.  It made 
the point that “states will play a key role in RTO formation and development”3 but it declined to 
“specify generically a state's role in governance.”4  The Commission said, “State involvement is 
important for several reasons, especially where RTOs are a critical element of the retail choice 
programs of many states.  State commissions are in a unique position to assess whether a 
particular RTO design will help or hinder their efforts to promote retail competition.”5 
In response, states formed a regional state committee (“RSC”), the Organization of PJM 
States, Inc. (“OPSI”).  The ICC is a member of OPSI and the Organization of MISO States, Inc. 
(“OMS”), the RSC in the MISO region.  OPSI and its member states have actively collaborated 
and participated in the formation and development of the PJM processes.  Recent events, 
however, such as the generation fleet transition, tremendous load growth, and public policy 
changes have transformed the energy landscape such that an increased state role and public 
interest focus has become crucial. These changes have increased costs while threatening 
reliability. The PJM existing stakeholder process has struggled to meet this moment. Given the 
stakes, it is reasonable to revisit the PJM process to ensure PJM provides economically efficient

The ICC cares about the viability of PJM and sees the need to improve the PJM 
stakeholder process.  As a restructured state, Illinois relies significantly on our participation in 
competitive RTO markets to ensure resource adequacy and reliability for our ratepayers at an 
affordable price. As discussed at the Technical Conference, many key reliability and economic 
issues get lost in the sometimes seemingly obtuse stakeholder process in which related issues are 
often split between various committees, subcommittees, task forces and work groups.   
To combat this problem, PJM should develop a clear process of both (1) better 
collaboration with state entities (detailed further in section B below) and (2) setting an annual 
work plan, requiring each committee and subcommittee to do the same, including a plan to 
prioritize each work plan action item. If PJM is unwilling to set a clear work plan, set timelines, 
and ultimately follow through, the Commisssion should direct PJM to achieve this.  An example 
of how to efficiently prioritize work plan action items is to determine key priorities then give 
each issue/work plan item a prioritization ranking according to high, medium or low risk.  Each 
committee, subcommittee and task force follows through with a focus first on those items 
considered highest priority.  Such a system in which PJM has developed a clear work plan and 
action items with a prioritization on high-priority items, should help PJM and stakeholders stay 
focused.  If a time sensitive issue appears throughout a year, there should be clear plans from 
PJM on how to address time-sensitive issues to reduce reliance on Critical Issue Fast-Paths, 
something that has become a normalized process in the PJM stakeholder process over the past 
few years.      
Further, while the ICC agrees that stakeholder discussions can be productive, these 
discussions should be led with the purpose of accomplishing goals in a more-timely manner.

Solutions exist today that can accomplish noticeable change.  Specifically, PJM stakeholder 
meetings should be streamlined and more rigorously moderated to make them more productive. 
Committee and subcommittee leadership should consider limiting participants to one or two 
questions before going back into the queue to allow more voices and diverse opinions to be 
heard, similar to MISO stakeholder processes.  Moreover, PJM should streamline obtuse 
committee structures that bifurcate related issues and force stakeholders to guess when and 
where their key issues will be discussed.  PJM agendas should be clear and respect time 
schedules to help stakeholders know how best to allocate what may be scarce resources.  A 
feedback process like MISO has may be a useful tool.  These suggestions could be implemented 
quickly and would require little work on PJM Staff to incorporate. 
B.  The Retail Jurisdictions Need A Larger Voice in the PJM Stakeholder Process 
To Better Represent Their 67 Million Residents. 
PJM’s current structure and culture largely relegate retail jurisdictions to the sidelines. 
States and the District of Columbia (“DC”), despite their key role in providing resource 
adequacy, lack a functional way to directly participate in PJM’s governance process.  PJM 
governance will be improved by allowing states and DC, all of whom represent ratepayers and 
raise necessary questions about issues like affordability, a greater voice in the stakeholder 
process. This enhanced voice for states and DC should come in two specific forms.  
First, while it is not a public utility under the Federal Power Act, OPSI needs an enhanced 
voice in the PJM section 205 rate filing process under the Federal Power Act.6 At a minimum, 
OPSI should be provided the opportunity for input and to provide alternative proposals when 
PJM makes a section 205 filing at the Commission. OPSI is the primary vehicle for states and the

DC to communicate preferences and concerns to the PJM Board, Staff, and stakeholders. Despite 
this crucial role, OPSI does not currently have sufficient opportunity for meaningful input to help 
shape PJM’s direction.  
States and the DC lack a formal voice at PJM, a significant problem. They are responsible 
for resource adequacy and are important representatives of their ratepayers’ concerns.  In 
addition, state regulators bring technical expertise and state policy background that would 
enhance PJM filings.  Given states regulators’ important function in the PJM region, it is sensible 
for the Commission to require PJM to make any necessary tariff changes to allow OPSI the 
opportunity to provide input as well as alternative proposals to the Commission when PJM 
makes section 205 filings.  Failure to do so directly results in an underdeveloped and incomplete 
stakeholder process.  After all, ratepayers are directly impacted by the cost and reliability issues 
that flow from such filings, as well as any resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation 
decisions made in the PJM stakeholder process.  
Given this, it is crucial for states and DC to have a greater voice in shaping any PJM 
section 205 filings, particularly with respect to resource adequacy and transmission cost 
allocation matters.  Specifically, states and DC should be provided alternative proposal “jump 
ball” rights that require PJM to file state and DC preferences alongside PJM Board filings. 
Giving PJM states and DC more input into PJM filings would bring PJM in alignment with other 
RTOs. RSCs in the Southwest Power Pool (“SPP”), ISO New England (“ISO-NE”) and the 
MISO regions have some version of this option over certain policy areas.  The ICC supports 
OPSI’s request to have at the very least the same level of valuable input into PJM’s section 205 
filings.  This simple change will improve PJM’s proposals by bringing more perspectives,

notably the currently largely overlooked state and DC voices, more centrally into the PJM 
process  
  Second, ICC also supports a meaningful voice in the process for the Governors’ 
Collaborative.  Importantly, this group should have formal recognition at PJM.  Bringing the 
region’s executives into the PJM’s stakeholder and governance processes in a more meaningful 
way will elevate important voices in conversations on the most pressing issues the region faces.  
C. The PJM Board Should Have Greater Independence To Enable the Board to 
Make Necessary, Difficult Decisions. 
The PJM Board requires greater independence to meet current and future challenges.  For 
PJM to accomplish its duty of providing reliable power at the lowest price possible to consumers, 
it is crucial that PJM – and the PJM Board – have sufficient independence to enact the policies 
they believe are right at any given moment while balancing its responsibilities to stakeholders 
and its 67 million residents.  RTOs, such as PJM, require careful interaction of numerous 
technical aspects to accomplish their mission.  RTOs exist at the intersection of prudent market 
design, thoughtful transmission planning, careful legal reasoning, and policy implementation.  
Each of these areas requires the PJM Board, at times, to make the right decision based on 
feedback from PJM’s technical staff, not simply to implement the most popular choice of the 
voting members.  
PJM’s current governance structure too often impedes the Board’s decision-making 
authority.  PJM Members’ ability to file tariff changes on their own with a supermajority vote 
and without Board support as well as their power to remove Board members after only three 
years, means that PJM’s Board is often restricted in its choices.  PJM’s Board needs sufficient 
protection to ensure non-discriminatory tariffs and important revisions to the PJM operating 
manuals are implemented.  Stakeholder input and expertise is undeniably central to PJM’s

process, particularly to developing nuanced, workable solutions for market and transmission 
problems. PJM Members, however, are also unavoidably (and reasonably) tied to their own 
interests and perspectives.  Too often PJM Members’ entrenched preferences have resulted in 
PJM’s stakeholder process breaking down and failing to converge on workable solutions.  PJM 
Board’s independence can come in many forms, such as longer terms for its members.  The 
Commission should consider ways to expand PJM Board’s independence without wholly 
undermining PJM Board’s responsiveness to its members and other stakeholder concerns.    
II. 
SURVEY RESPONSE 
In the July 30, 2026, Notice Requesting Post-Technical Conference Comments, the 
Commission directed commenters to include responses to the survey attached in Appendix A to 
the Notice.  As described below, many of the individual changes proposed by the ICC must 
accompany others and cannot stand alone.  Changes can make PJM better able to address the 
challenges that it faces today.  As Chairman Swett said at the Technical Conference, there are 
ways to “right the ship.”  What has been made clear in the last few years, PJM ratepayers are 
experiencing the highest costs from PJM while being the least protected from abuses and scarcity 
prices.  Empowering and incorporating states government, including the public service 
commissions tasked with balancing protection ratepayers, is critical to ensuring PJM works for 
the consumers who ultimately pay the bills, not just powerful market sector interests.    
The ICC provides the following responses to the survey:

PJM Board Independence 
 
 
Questions/Comments 
Yes No 
1) 
Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years? 
x 
 
 
Comments:  
PJM’s Board members’ term should be lengthened to five years.  It was made clear at 
the July 23 Technical Conference that PJM Board and Staff must take a greater 
leadership role in guiding PJM, its planning, its operation, and the markets it oversees.  
 
Much of this leadership role is utilizing the tools and authority PJM already has.  There 
are difficult choices to make, and term lengths greater than three years will help ensure 
stability in Board leadership to make tough choices.  Five-year terms, for example, 
would help ensure Board member positions are not a revolving door and there is 
stability when the Board needs to make important, sometimes unpopular, decisions, 
while also ensuring Board Members are responsible to the stakeholders of PJM.  
2) 
Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may 
serve (e.g., 10 years)? 
x 
 
 
Comments:  
 
There should be a maximum number of years any Board member may serve. This is not 
dependent on other changes that Commission or PJM may undertake to improve 
governance and PJM’s health.  Reasonable board turnover is good for the longevity of 
any organization.  While there should be a limit on the number of years any Board 
member may serve, the total should allow for Board members to stay on for a 
reasonable amount of time.  ICC recommends around 10 years (or two five-year terms) 
to strike a reasonable balance between encouraging institutional expertise on the Board 
while also ensuring new perspectives.   
3) 
Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a 
preference for some Board members that reside in the PJM region? 
 
 
 
Comments:  
While the ICC does not believe that residency in PJM is a necessary requirement to 
serve on PJM Board, residency in the PJM territory should not be a barrier to being 
considered and selected as a board member.

ultimately improve and speed up processes by addressing problems early on. Board 
members should meet with state officials on individual and more frequent basis in both 
formal and informal settings.
