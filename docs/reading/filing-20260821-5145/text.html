COMMENTS OF THE PUBLIC SERVICE COMMISSION OF WEST VIRGINIA  
 
 
The Public Service Commission of West Virginia (“PSCWV”) respectfully files these 
comments to address concerns regarding PJM’s July 31, 2026 filing proposing a series of steps to 
acquire a predetermined quantity of capacity in a process it calls Reliability Backstop Procurement 
(“RBP”).1  While the stated need for its unusual and hurry-up new capacity acquisition approach 
is “primarily driven by the unprecedented addition of large loads, most notably data centers, in the 
PJM Region”2 the target level for PJM’s RBP is not specific to new large loads and is not 
sufficiently flexible to modify the RBP target if alternative proposals, championed by PJM itself, 
are successful in acquiring capacity for the new large loads in a more direct way that better protects 
other electric customers from bearing cost increases for RBP capacity they do not  need, and which 
may not be needed even for new large load.    
The PSCWV does not support the filing in its present form and timetable because the 
proposal fails to provide adequate support that the target level of so-called backstop capacity is 
needed and the proposed tariff fails to assure that the RBP will acquire capacity for and will be 
paid for by New Large Loads (NLL) created by data centers that have not made arrangements to 
provide their own capacity (“unsupported NLL”).  This major defect in the PJM RBP is created 
because PJM is effectively preempting its own parallel path “bilateral matching process” which,

Indeed, throughout the underlying stakeholder process, PJM members have 
expressed a strong preference for bringing new capacity to the PJM system 
voluntarily through bilateral contracting rather than through the RBP. To streamline 
and support the efforts of PJM members to enter into bilateral arrangements for 
capacity, PJM, in partnership with PJM consultant Charles River Associates, has 
facilitated bilateral arrangements through a bilateral matching process with the aim 
of connecting supply of new capacity, i.e., new capacity resources, with new 
demand for that capacity, i.e., new load.3    
 
Instead of waiting to see if the bilateral matching process resolves the immediate need for 
capacity to serve NLL, PJM is proposing the RBP based on a perceived 6,831 MW shortfall from 
its capacity target set for its last capacity auction.4  Although PJM’s filing provides for offsets to 
reduce this artificial and potentially unnecessary 6,831 MW target, the offsets are subject to 
complex and potentially discouraging qualifications that will likely understate offsets and result in 
an excessive final RBP capacity target that will be committed for fifteen years at high prices that 
will likely exceed the clearing prices of the last two capacity auctions and could exceed an average 
of $1.4 billion per year.5  
The qualifications for the offset include:  
• Expected commercial operation date no later than June 1, 2032;

While these qualifications for an offset from the load serving entity allocated share of the 6,831 
target for the RBP can be individually defended, they are necessary only because the starting point 
for the RBP is a presumed shortfall, not directly tied to unsupported NLL, in PJM’s reliability 
target that has not been properly modified after the results of the bilateral matching of NLL to new 
capacity are known and the PJM capacity model has been rerun eliminating unsupported NLL with 
Bring Your Own Generation (“BYOG”) NLL.       
 FERC should not allow PJM to put the cart before the horse, or ineffectively beside the 
horse, by allowing a premature implantation of a flawed RBP while the superior bilateral matching 
process is underway and the results are unknown.  To the extent that new large loads procure new 
capacity directly the RBP procurement target should decrease. But even more importantly, from a 
cost allocation approach the bilateral contracting is superior because it reduces, or eliminates,  the 
risk of other customers bearing costs of capacity acquired for NLL.  Bilateral contracts allow the 
costs of new capacity to be assumed directly by the contracting new  large loads, creating the need 
for that capacity.  Therefore, there is a reduced need for a large PJM RBP capacity procurement 
target.  Lowering the RBP target reduces the capacity costs assigned to load serving entities that

could be potentially stranded if the new large loads cannot pick up the cost, and minimizes the risk 
of cost shifts to other, non-NLL data center customers.  
The bilateral matching process is superior to the one sided PJM capacity market which has 
no real demand price curve bid by willing buyers.  The PJM administratively determined demand 
curve may be necessary to acquire capacity under normal circumstances.  But “ the proposed RBP 
differs significantly from PJM’s standard market constructs.”7  Therefore, a market that receives 
price bids from willing buyers is superior and a reason to rely more on the bilateral contracting to 
acquire capacity to serve NLL rather than prematurely rushing into the RBP. 
Under the bilateral contracting model, willing buyers contract with willing sellers without 
resorting to centralized procurement by PJM and a complicated cost allocation or assignment 
approach that assigns costs to load serving entities and leaves it up to them to recover the costs 
from NLL.  Thus, a real supply price/demand price capacity acquisition approach is better at 
addressing the region’s capacity needs created by NLL while protecting existing customers.8 
CONCLUSION 
To protect customers, and to accomplish goal of assigning costs incurred to serve NLL  at 
a minimum, FERC should require PJM to determine a new target for the RBP based on the model 
from its last capacity auction run under two different scenarios.’  One run should exclude all NLL.

The second run should exclude NLL that is bringing its own generation pursuant to the bilateral 
matching process established by PJM or for any other reason.  The difference between the two 
results will provide a critical piece of information that is necessary to determines if the RBP is 
needed, and, if it is needed, what level of capacity is needed due to unsupported NLL.  Moreover, 
by comparing a capacity reliability target that is created by unsupported NLL and providing states 
with the location and amounts of the unsupported NLL that the RBP is acquiring capacity for, PJM 
can provide states with the information needed to identify unsupported NLL,  allocate the capacity 
costs to that NLL, and insulate other customers from paying for capacity acquired to serve NLL.  
In addition, before committing the hopefully much lower level of RBP capacity there should be 
some delay after providing the state level information to states and their load serving entities so 
that they can determine that there is a realistic opportunity to recover the costs from the 
unsupported NLL and how they will accomplish that recovery.
