LIMITED PROTEST OF GOOGLE LLC 
 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy 
Regulatory Commission (“Commission”),1 Google LLC (“Google”) submits this limited protest 
of Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc.’s (“MISO”) proposal to establish a Large 
Load Addition Resource Study (“LARS”) process.2  Google commends MISO for working 
proactively to address the concerns that the Commission identified in the Large Load Show Cause 
Order.3  The concept underlying LARS is a particularly important one that is worth prioritizing, 
and it is a concept that Google continues to strongly support.  Studying new large loads together 
with paired generation resources that will serve those loads will maximize the use of existing 
infrastructure and minimize strain on the grid,4 which will, in turn, accelerate interconnection

Nevertheless, Google is filing a limited protest because MISO has filed LARS without 
accompanying and necessary protections against cost shifting, which prevents the Commission

To be clear, Google supports both the concept underlying LARS, which will improve speed

identified cost-shifting concerns, while still enabling the benefits of a targeted, combined process 
for studying a large load and its contractually paired generation together.

A. The LARS Proposal Opens the Door to Shifting the Costs of Serving LARS Pairs 
onto Other Customers and Future MTEP Cycles 
Customers like Google could be prevented from using LARS without violating their 
Ratepayer Protection Pledge commitments.  That is because, under LARS, MISO will study the 
load and generation in a pair and “any common violations that show up in the generation and load 
studies [will be] assigned to [BRPs] and Other project types.”11  Only the violations specifically 
caused by the generator’s interconnection are assigned to the generator.  Under the MISO tariff, 
the costs of BRPs and “Other” projects are recovered through Attachment O by the Transmission 
Owner developing the project, for the portion of the project located in that Transmission Owner’s

In addition, LARS could shift costs onto residential customers and other existing 
commercial customers by relying on the MTEP process as a backstop to develop projects needed 
to resolve transmission issues caused by LARS pairs.  MISO explains that if it allocates headroom 
to a LARS generator that has also been allocated to a DPP project—a phenomenon that MISO 
calls “overallocation”17—then any resulting constraints caused by that overallocation “will be 
formalized in a future MTEP project.”18  Put differently, residential and other commercial 
customers could be left holding the bag for transmission projects needed to interconnect LARS 
generators any time MISO “overallocat[es]” available headroom.    
That too represents an unacceptable risk of cross-subsidization.  That is particularly so 
given that LARS’s design creates the very real risk of overallocation because ongoing DPP cycles 
do not include LARS requests and the LARS studies do not include DPP projects before they have 
signed Generator Interconnection Agreements.19  As a result, each process is based on an 
incomplete snapshot of the transmission system and how it will operate in practice, which increases 
the likelihood that the LARS and DPP processes will produce incompatible results.  MISO would 
not, however, reconcile the DPP and LARS study processes until after both are complete,20 and, if 
the cumulative impacts of the two separate studies create constraints not identified in either, the 
upgrades necessary to resolve those constraints will be deferred to the MTEP process.  
That result would be unjust and unreasonable and inconsistent with Google’s customer-
protection commitments, including the Ratepayer Protection Pledge.  The Commission should

C. LARS Opens the Door to Undue Discrimination  
Finally, Google is concerned that LARS opens the door to undue discrimination and could 
undermine core open access principles by making load-serving entities (“LSEs”) the gatekeepers 
of the LARS process.  Under MISO’s proposal, LARS applications must be submitted through a 
designated LARS Associated Transmission Customer, which MISO indicates is typically the LSE. 
This appears to give LSEs discretion to determine whether and under what circumstances to submit 
a LARS application because it gives the LSE the central role of submitting applications, with no 
guardrails should an LSE simply decline to submit an application on behalf of an independent 
generator.  This concern is particularly acute given that many of the states within MISO’s footprint 
are vertically integrated, and vertically integrated utilities have a structural incentive to favor their 
generators over those that are independently owned.25 While Google recognizes that LSEs have 
an obligation to serve load and are responsible for submitting load study requests, making LSEs 
gatekeepers for the LARS process will unnecessarily restrict supply available to serve new large 
loads, particularly where independent generators have direct offtake arrangements with loads. 
Google encourages FERC to provide targeted guidance to MISO to modify the proposal to include 
protections providing that all forms of generation, including independent generation, have 
nondiscriminatory, direct access to the expedited generation interconnection study process under 
LARS.  This could include requirements to file a LARS application that meets objective criteria, 
an ability for independent generators to secure Commission resolution of disputes with the relevant 
LSE, similar to how the Commission resolves unexecuted generator interconnection agreements, 
or an alternative mechanism that addresses these concerns.
