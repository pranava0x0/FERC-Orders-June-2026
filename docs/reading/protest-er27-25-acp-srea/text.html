I. BACKGROUND
A. MISO’s Initial and Revised ERAS Proposals
On May 16, 2025, the Commission rejected MISO’s initial ERAS proposal, finding that MISO had “not demonstrated that its ERAS proposal is a just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory or preferential approach for addressing MISO’s stated resource adequacy and reliability needs.”5 The Commission identified two defects. First, the proposed Tariff placed “no limit on the number of projects that could be entered in the ERAS process,” which could produce an ERAS queue with processing times “similar to the challenges with the current DPP queue” and “a volume of ERAS interconnection requests untethered to reliability or resource adequacy needs.”6 Second, MISO had not shown “how the ERAS process is sufficiently targeted to study only interconnection requests needed to meet the anticipated shortfall in generating capacity described by MISO.”7 Commissioners Rosner and See each concurred separately.8

MISO refiled a revised ERAS proposal that the Commission accepted on July 21, 2025. The accepted ERAS process is capped at 68 interconnection requests, with ten reserved for IPPs that have agreements with entities other than LSEs and eight reserved for requests serving retail- choice load; it requires verification by a Relevant Electric Retail Regulatory Authority (“RERRA”) of a specific resource adequacy need; and it sunsets on the earlier of August 31, 2027 or completion of the sixty-eighth study.9 The Commission explained that these changes “ensure the ERAS process is sufficiently limited in scope to swiftly address discrete, demonstrated resource adequacy

C. The LARS Proposal
MISO proposes LARS as a new, permanent study process under which a large load of at least 200 megawatts (“MW”) and one or more new Generating Facilities are studied in parallel, with the generator receiving a Large Load Generator Interconnection Agreement (“LLGIA”) for NRIS in approximately 120 days.17 MISO will run three LARS study periods per year, each with up to ten LARS Applications studied serially on a first-come, first-served basis.18 The LARS Application may be initiated and submitted only by the “LARS Associated Transmission Customer”—the entity that procures Transmission Service for the load, which MISO acknowledges “is often” an LSE.19 The LARS Associated Transmission Customer also pays the $250,000 non-refundable application deposit.20 A LARS Generating Facility need only be located in the same Local Resource Zone (“LRZ”) as the load, may request NRIS up to 150% of the load’s peak demand, and must remain paired with the load for fifteen years, after which its Interconnection Service continues regardless of whether the load remains.21 LARS studies will

II. PROTEST
Because MISO filed LARS under FPA section 205, MISO bears the burden of
demonstrating that its proposal is just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory or preferential.23 MISO has not carried that burden. LARS is unduly discriminatory because it makes the LSE the gatekeeper of a Commission-jurisdictional generator interconnection process and gives LSEs contracting options that IPPs do not have. It is also unjust and unreasonable on its own terms.  It is a permanent, uncapped priority queue untethered to any demonstrated need, it does not reflect the electrically proximate configuration the Show Cause Order described, and it shifts transmission capacity and costs away from DPP customers.  These are the same defects the Commission identified when it rejected MISO’s initial ERAS proposal. Finally, MISO has not shown that LARS satisfies the independent entity variation standard.

A. LARS Would Unduly Discriminate Against Independent Power Producers and
Create Opportunities for LSE Self-Dealing.
i. Open access requires that all interconnection customers have comparable
access to Commission-jurisdictional interconnection service.

B. LARS Is Not Just and Reasonable.
i. LARS is a permanent, uncapped expedited queue untethered to any identified need, unlike every expedited interconnection process the Commission has

accepted.

D. MISO Has Not Demonstrated That LARS Satisfies the Independent Entity
Variation Standard.
In Order No. 2003, the Commission permitted RTOs and ISOs to propose “independent entity variations” from the pro forma LGIP and LGIA, recognizing that an RTO or ISO “has different operating characteristics depending on its size and location and is less likely to act in an unduly discriminatory manner than a Transmission Provider that is a market participant.”112 Under that standard, an RTO/ISO must demonstrate that its proposed variations “are just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory or preferential and accomplish the purposes of Order No. 2003.”113 The flexibility the standard affords rests on the RTO/ISO’s independence. Although RTOs have far less incentive to discriminate, the same cannot be said of the LSEs and Transmission Owners to which LARS delegates control.

First, under the pro forma LGIP, an interconnection customer submits its interconnection request directly to the transmission provider, and the transmission provider—not a third party—administers access to interconnection service.114 LARS instead requires that the request be submitted by the LSE, and it requires a POI agreed to and attested by the Transmission Owner before the request may be filed.115 Second, the pro forma LGIP establishes a single queue in which requests are studied on

III. REQUEST FOR CLARIFICATION
If the Commission does not reject the Filing, it should direct MISO to clarify whether the LARS same-LRZ requirement applicable to a LARS Interconnection Customer is determined based on a Generating Facility’s physical location or on its POI to the MISO Transmission System. Proposed Attachment X, Section 3.12.2 appears to apply the same-LRZ requirement based on the Generating Facility’s POI for an individual LARS Interconnection Customer, providing that “[t]he Point of Interconnection specified in a LARS Application must be located in the same Local Resource Zone as the Point of Withdrawal of the LARS Associated Load.”121 However, where multiple Generating Facilities serve a single LARS Associated Load behind different POIs, the proposed Tariff states that the Generating Facilities must “[b]e located in the same LRZ as the LARS Associated Load,” creating ambiguity as to whether the requirement applies to the physical location of the Generating Facilities or to their respective POIs.122

The Clean Energy Trades request that the Commission direct MISO to clarify that, for purposes of the same-LRZ requirement, the relevant location is the applicable POI to the MISO Transmission System rather than the physical location of the Generating Facility, including where multiple Generating Facilities serve a single LARS Associated Load.
