PJM should not create the problem in the first place. However, having created the problem, PJM should follow its logic through to the end. PJM fails to do so. For example, of course it would provide a stronger incentive to IRAS load to be curtailed before demand resources. But that is not relevant to the “logic” of IRAS load created by PJM. IRAS load is data center load without matching generation that is nonetheless required to pay for capacity. It would also create a stronger incentive to curtail IRAS load prior to shortage pricing or whenever prices exceed $500/MWh. PJM’s argument about incentives proves too much. It does not stand the test of simple PJM capacity market logic. IRAS load pays for capacity. Demand resources supply capacity that IRAS load pays for.

PJM's principal rationale is that Eligible New Large loads have not brought new capacity and therefore should not receive the “capacity benefit” of Demand Resources.33 PJM ignores the salient fact that these IRAS loads are required to pay for capacity. PJM fails to explain what not bringing new generation had to do with being interrupted before demand resources that PJM has already procured, compensated, and obligated to provide reliability service. 7. Interconnecting Large Data Center Loads Without Generation
Decreases Reliability and Increases Cost to Other Consumers.
PJM makes the unsupported assertion that “calling for IRAS load reductions before pre-emergency load management” completely mitigates the resource adequacy risk of interconnecting new large loads without matching capacity and “protects native loads from the cost and reliability impacts of such new large loads.”41

PJM’s asserts that, from a reliability perspective, adding large data center load to the PJM system with curtailment provisions is the same as not adding large data center load or adding data center load with matching generation. PJM provides no analytical support for this claim. PJM’s operational history contradicts PJM’s claim. Higher levels of 8,760 hour load in PJM without matching generation, net of accounting for curtailments, increases the risk of unanticipated operational events. The higher the load, the larger the risk. Higher capacity factors lead to a need for higher maintenance outages and a higher risk of unforced outages and more units reaching emissions limits.

Large data center loads consume energy around the clock. Existing generating units are required to run more and therefore have higher capacity factors. Higher capacity factors mean an increased frequency of maintenance outages and a higher risk of forced outages.42

8. PJM’s Proposal Will Negatively Affect Reliability.
The essential requirement for scheduling planned outages is substantially lower load during off peak seasons. Lower load outside peak summer and winter periods, typically during the shoulder months, provides an opportunity to operate the grid reliably with less available generation. PJM schedules planned maintenance outages during this period. As the load without matching generation increases in PJM, PJM operations is and will be challenged to meet higher loads with lower generation capacity due to planned outages. PJM acknowledged this important concern. During the Critical Issue Fast Path Education, PJM stated that “if load continues to increase 8,760 hours/year, and the BRA clears the IRM, either: Future generation outages may be denied/deferred to the following season to avoid emergency procedures; Not a good option - Units may then be less reliable or become an Unplanned Outages if Planned/Maintenance Outages requests are denied.”45

In addition to the scheduling of outages, when units reach their emission limits they are no longer available to meet 8,760 hour load. There are multiple generating units in PJM today that are subject to emission restrictions.46 In some cases, the restrictions limit the total output within a rolling calendar year to only a few hours.
