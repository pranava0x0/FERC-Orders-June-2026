LIMITED PROTEST OF BLOOM ENERGY CORPORATION

Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Commission's Rules of Practice and Procedure, 18 C.F.R. § 385.211, Bloom Energy Corporation ("Bloom") submits this limited protest of the October 2, 2026 filing by the Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc. ("MISO") to establish the Large Load Addition Resource Study ("LARS").

Bloom supports LARS and urges the Commission to accept it by MISO's requested December 2, 2026 effective date. LARS solves a real problem: today, load and generation studies run on different timelines, so each must wait on the other. By studying a large load and its paired generation in parallel, LARS removes that delay. It does so in a technology-neutral way, tying generation to load through a 150% cap. And it protects the Definitive Planning Phase (“DPP”) queue from speculative entry through serial ten-pair cycles, deposits, milestone payment, and 15- year pairing requirement. Bloom protests one element only: the 200 MW minimum load size.

I. Limited Protest
MISO bears the burden under FPA section 205 to show that each element of its proposal is just and reasonable. Its only evidence of transmission system impact does not support a 200 MW threshold. MISO initially proposed 250 MW, relying on Expedited Project Review (“EPR”) results showing that loads of 250 MW and above created "meaningful grid impacts," with upgrades "increasingly likely between 300–500 MW."1 MISO then lowered the threshold to 200 MW, not on the basis of new analysis, but in response to stakeholder feedback that many projects

Section 205 prohibits any public utility from maintaining “any unreasonable difference in rates, charges, service, facilities, or in any other respect” between classes of service.7 A paired load and generation that interconnect to the Transmission System are similarly situated whether the load is 75 MW or 200 MW. Each takes transmission service, each faces the study timing mismatch LARS cures, and each presents the same coordination problem. MISO must show that paired loads and generation below 200 MW affect the Transmission System differently. It has not done so.

MISO responds that the 200 MW minimum preserves LARS’s “focus on large, impactful load additions.”8 But MISO has already determined that loads of 50 MW or more are impactful enough to warrant its large load reliability requirements.9 MISO cannot treat those loads as large and impactful for reliability purposes yet not for the study process designed to integrate them. Nor does the load size bear on the problem LARS solves – the timing mismatch between MISO’s load study and its generator interconnection queue arises for any paired load awaiting a generator’s GIA. And the ten-pair cap, not a size threshold, already preserves the focus of each study cycle.10

The proper screen for LARS is interconnection of Large Load to the Transmission System, not load size. MISO’s own Large Load definition draws that exact line: it applies to loads of 50 MW or more that connect to the Transmission System. That definition implements the Commission’s direction to tailor the large load definition to MISO’s system.15 LARS itself requires that the paired load’s Point of Withdrawal connect to the Transmission System. The 200 MW minimum is the only thing separating the two, and MISO offers no transmission-based reason for departing from the Show Cause Order. LARS should therefore apply to loads of 50 MW or more that connect to the Transmission System. A 75 MW load connected to the 115-161 kV network that must await a paired generator's GIA faces the same delay as a 200 MW load. Nor is a 200 MW minimum needed to protect the process. MISO's other safeguards already screen out speculative and de minimis requests: the $250,000 non-refundable deposit, the milestone payment, full site control, and the bar on aggregating multiple loads.16 And the pairing agreement, the same LRZ requirement, and the 15-year commitment – not load size – are what keep LARS from becoming a bypass of the competitive DPP queue. A 200 MW minimum load size adds no protection beyond what these safeguards already provide.

Finally, if the Commission agrees that the minimum load size should be lowered, it should direct a conforming change to Section 3.12.2, which separately requires at least 200 MW of Network Resource Interconnection Service at each Point of Interconnection. That generation- side minimum derives from the load threshold and should scale with it, so that a qualifying load can be served by appropriately sized generation.

II. Conclusion
Bloom does not ask the Commission to delay LARS. Bloom respectfully requests that the Commission (1) accept LARS effective December 2, 2026; and (2) direct MISO, in a compliance filing within 60 days, either to (a) align LARS eligibility with MISO's Large Load definition, with a corresponding generation-side minimum, or (b) provide record evidence that loads below 200 MW connected to the Transmission System do not warrant coordinated study.
