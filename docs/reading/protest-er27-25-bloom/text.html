LIMITED PROTEST OF BLOOM ENERGY CORPORATION 
 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Commission's Rules of Practice and Procedure, 18 C.F.R. § 
385.211, Bloom Energy Corporation ("Bloom") submits this limited protest of the October 2, 
2026 filing by the Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc. ("MISO") to establish the 
Large Load Addition Resource Study ("LARS"). 
Bloom supports LARS and urges the Commission to accept it by MISO's requested 
December 2, 2026 effective date. LARS solves a real problem: today, load and generation studies 
run on different timelines, so each must wait on the other. By studying a large load and its paired 
generation in parallel, LARS removes that delay. It does so in a technology-neutral way, tying 
generation to load through a 150% cap. And it protects the Definitive Planning Phase (“DPP”) 
queue from speculative entry through serial ten-pair cycles, deposits, milestone payment, and 15-
year pairing requirement. Bloom protests one element only: the 200 MW minimum load size. 
I. 
Limited Protest 
MISO bears the burden under FPA section 205 to show that each element of its proposal 
is just and reasonable. Its only evidence of transmission system impact does not support a 200 
MW threshold. MISO initially proposed 250 MW, relying on Expedited Project Review (“EPR”) 
results showing that loads of 250 MW and above created "meaningful grid impacts," with 
upgrades "increasingly likely between 300–500 MW."1 MISO then lowered the threshold to 200 
MW, not on the basis of new analysis, but in response to stakeholder feedback that many projects

Section 205 prohibits any public utility from maintaining “any unreasonable difference in 
rates, charges, service, facilities, or in any other respect” between classes of service.7 A paired 
load and generation that interconnect to the Transmission System are similarly situated whether 
the load is 75 MW or 200 MW. Each takes transmission service, each faces the study timing 
mismatch LARS cures, and each presents the same coordination problem. MISO must show that 
paired loads and generation below 200 MW affect the Transmission System differently. It has not 
done so. 
MISO responds that the 200 MW minimum preserves LARS’s “focus on large, impactful 
load additions.”8 But MISO has already determined that loads of 50 MW or more are impactful 
enough to warrant its large load reliability requirements.9 MISO cannot treat those loads as large 
and impactful for reliability purposes yet not for the study process designed to integrate them. 
Nor does the load size bear on the problem LARS solves – the timing mismatch between MISO’s 
load study and its generator interconnection queue arises for any paired load awaiting a 
generator’s GIA. And the ten-pair cap, not a size threshold, already preserves the focus of each 
study cycle.10 
MISO's supporting testimony also relies on stakeholder feedback that data center loads 
"come in 200 MW blocks,"11 but cites no data. Industry sources indicate that data center capacity 
is typically built and energized in smaller increments.12 Smaller loads also fall within the

The proper screen for LARS is interconnection of Large Load to the Transmission 
System, not load size. MISO’s own Large Load definition draws that exact line: it applies to 
loads of 50 MW or more that connect to the Transmission System. That definition implements 
the Commission’s direction to tailor the large load definition to MISO’s system.15 LARS itself 
requires that the paired load’s Point of Withdrawal connect to the Transmission System. The 200 
MW minimum is the only thing separating the two, and MISO offers no transmission-based 
reason for departing from the Show Cause Order. LARS should therefore apply to loads of 50 
MW or more that connect to the Transmission System. A 75 MW load connected to the 115-161 
kV network that must await a paired generator's GIA faces the same delay as a 200 MW load. 
Nor is a 200 MW minimum needed to protect the process. MISO's other safeguards 
already screen out speculative and de minimis requests: the $250,000 non-refundable deposit, the 
milestone payment, full site control, and the bar on aggregating multiple loads.16 And the pairing 
agreement, the same LRZ requirement, and the 15-year commitment – not load size – are what 
keep LARS from becoming a bypass of the competitive DPP queue. A 200 MW minimum load 
size adds no protection beyond what these safeguards already provide.

Finally, if the Commission agrees that the minimum load size should be lowered, it 
should direct a conforming change to Section 3.12.2, which separately requires at least 200 MW 
of Network Resource Interconnection Service at each Point of Interconnection. That generation-
side minimum derives from the load threshold and should scale with it, so that a qualifying load 
can be served by appropriately sized generation. 
II. 
Conclusion 
Bloom does not ask the Commission to delay LARS. Bloom respectfully requests that the 
Commission (1) accept LARS effective December 2, 2026; and (2) direct MISO, in a compliance 
filing within 60 days, either to (a) align LARS eligibility with MISO's Large Load definition, 
with a corresponding generation-side minimum, or (b) provide record evidence that loads below 
200 MW connected to the Transmission System do not warrant coordinated study.
