COMMENTS OF EXELON CORPORATION 
 
Exelon Corporation and its affiliates1 (collectively, “Exelon”) welcome the opportunity to 
comment in response to the Notice Requesting Post-Technical Conference Comments2 issued by the 
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission”) in the above-captioned docket. 
I. 
INTRODUCTION AND COMMENTS 
Exelon appreciates the Commission’s attention to issues related to the governance structure of 
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) at this important time of change in the electricity industry. The 
efficacy of the PJM governance framework and stakeholder process has been a simmering issue for 
years but has come to a head over the looming resource adequacy crisis paired with the inability to 
move rapidly to interconnect new large loads while maintaining reliability and affordability. Forecasted 
resource adequacy concerns are especially acute in PJM, the region where Exelon’s utility operating 
companies provide electric service to over 10 million customers across five states and the District of 
Columbia.  These circumstances present at a time when electricity has never been more important to

The Commission, in initiating the instant docket, has properly identified pains stemming from

critical issues. For instance, most recently, only approximately 70 members—representing less than 
15% of more than 500 qualified voting members—voted in the Reliability Backstop 
Procurement/Connect and Manage critical issues fast path effort.3

To this end, Exelon uses two primary objectives as a waypoint for potential reforms. First, the 
need to enhance independent decision-making by PJM. The PJM Board of Managers (“Board”) should 
be empowered to make decisions untethered from the need to obtain approval from the Members 
Committee, including being granted the filing rights to do so. This extends to the transmission planning 
protocols as well as the energy and ancillary services markets, as the Board has today for the capacity 
market. Furthermore, the terms of Board members should be extended and they should have more open 
lines of communication with the membership to enable receipt of a wider range of perspectives filtered 
through fewer intermediaries and overly structured meetings. 
Exelon’s second objective is to modify the stakeholder process to more fully recognize the 
broader political context in which PJM operates. PJM has always included a diverse makeup of states, 
but today’s energy landscape is more political than it has ever been and pressing issues increasingly 
straddle the state-federal jurisdictional divide. States need a greater role in PJM, recognizing the 
inherently political nature of many decisions and the need to align PJM processes with state policies 
and, in turn, to make sure that states’ decisions (e.g., permitting) support PJM outcomes. There is a 
need for coordinated action between PJM and the states, and buy-in from the states to achieve PJM’s 
objectives. PJM cannot act without cognizance of, or in opposition to, the states if it hopes to be 
successful.  
With this in mind, Exelon supports giving states a greater role in PJM. Exactly what form such a 
role takes should be a matter of discussion and could include representation of state interests on the 
PJM Nominating Committee, on the Board, and/or mandatory consultation processes between state 
representatives and the Board. We would be open to incorporating best practices from other Regional

reform should not subordinate the Board to the states. An RTO must remain independent for its region

As PJM stakeholders chart a path forward for PJM’s governance framework, Exelon emphasizes

the footprint that will need to be addressed separately, and by a different set of stakeholders and using 
different tools.  
Exelon remains committed to continuing to work with our states, federal policymakers, and PJM 
on governance reform for the region. We look forward to partnering with other stakeholders to retain 
existing structures that work, advance needed reforms, and drive compatibility of reforms with the 
diverse views of states in the PJM region. We recognize and appreciate that what takes place at the 
Commission and at PJM must also accommodate and, indeed, facilitate actions states may take to 
support their own policies and priorities. To date, Exelon has tried to focus external stakeholder

RESPONSES TO QUESTIONS IN NOTICE 
A. 
Question 1 – Board Member Terms  
 
Yes 
No 
Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years? 
x 
 
 
Exelon’s view is that Board members’ terms should be longer than the current three-year 
duration. The structure of PJM is extremely complex and the issues facing the region are multi-faceted. 
Thus, it takes time to come up to speed once a Board member is elected and installed. Then, when 
issues arise, it can take years to develop solutions through the stakeholder process, have them approved 
by the Commission, and then be implemented with time for stakeholders and market participants to 
respond and gain experience with new rules. Ideally, Board members would have terms of adequate 
duration to support both the education and initiation process and to serve for long enough to have a 
meaningful amount of continuity in oversight as PJM staff and the stakeholder process develop and 
implement improvements to markets, planning, and operational rules.   
In addition to serving longer terms—and only if Board terms are extended—Exelon offers that 
Board members should only be able to serve at most three five year terms and must be younger than 75 
years old to be eligible for election or re-election consistent with existing obligations. Maintaining 
these standards of conduct while extending individual term limits would enhance Board independence, 
recognizing that Board members would undertake votes with less concern about how those votes would 
impact their ability to be renominated. Especially as PJM faces increased political pressure and Board 
actions have been subject to unprecedented levels of scrutiny, not facing continual renomination will 
free Board members to act with the highest level of independence in line with what they think is the 
best course of action for the PJM region.

Question 2 – Board Service Limits 
 
Yes 
No 
Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member 
may serve (e.g., 10 years)? 
x 
 
 
Exelon’s view is that Board members should be limited in the maximum number of years they 
may serve. An appropriate maximum likely depends on whether Board members are eligible to be 
renominated for more than one term that is extended beyond the current three-year period (see answer 
to Question 1). If Board members are not eligible for renomination, an appropriate single term would 
be seven years. This duration balances the considerations of 1) allowing Board members to come up to 
speed on PJM and oversee a number of meaningful reform efforts with 2) the fact that there is risk with 
placing too much weight on the one-time decision of the Nominating Committee. As with any 
corporate hiring process, experience has shown that not every Board member ultimately proves to be 
the right fit. If Board members are eligible for renomination and terms are not extended, this balance 
may be struck by allowing one or two renominations (for a total of six or nine total years of service). If 
Board members are eligible for renomination and terms are extended, the balance will depend on the 
new length of the term. In any case, Exelon’s view is that fifteen years of service is a reasonable upper 
bound on total number of years of Board service, which would arithmetically limit allowed 
renominations to two so long as the term length is five years.

Question 3 – Footprint Residence Preference 
 
Yes 
No 
Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide 
a preference for some Board members that reside in the PJM 
region? 
x 
 
 
Exelon would support a residency preference for Board members. Residence in PJM may have 
several benefits for Board members, including that it may increase likelihood of prior experience with 
the RTO, may increase Board members skin-in-the game with respect to outcomes in the region, and 
may make it easier for stakeholders to engage actively with Board members. However, Exelon does not 
support a mandate of residence in the PJM region so as not to overly constrain the pool of Board 
candidates. There are already challenges identifying qualified and interested Board members (even 
without the preference or requirement); it would be ill advised to exacerbate such challenges. We 
would not wish to overly constrain PJM’s ability to recruit qualified candidates that can bring a 
diversity of valuable experience, including experience from other regions, to the Board.

Question 4 – Cooling Off Period  
 
Yes 
No 
Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the 
cooling off period for Board members from two years to one year? 
x 
 
 
Exelon supports shortening the current two-year cooling off period to instead be one year. We 
understand the value of a cooling off period as a general matter, creating space between the time when 
a Board member may be affiliated with a PJM member and that member’s interest, and when she or he 
may serve on the Board and need to shift their perspective to the broader interests of the region. 
However, it is also important to, within reason, maximize the pool of quality, qualified candidates to 
serve on the Board. The current two-year cooling off period is unnecessarily restrictive and a one-year 
cooling off period may be a more appropriate duration to balance the interests. Note that this position is 
consistent with the implementation of a preference, but not requirement, for Board member residence 
in the PJM footprint—ultimately, the objective is to improve and expand the range of individuals that 
the Nominating Committee may consider to serve on the Board.

Question 5 – Ex Parte Restrictions  
 
Yes 
No 
Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable 
more communication between the Board and stakeholders? 
x 
 
 
Exelon supports relaxation of restrictions to enable more communication between the Board and 
stakeholders. Especially if PJM’s governance structures are reformed in alignment with Exelon’s 
recommended reforms—including a shift to an advisory polling with enhanced section 205 rights for 
the Board—it will be more important than ever that the Board is able to effectively gather direct, 
candid, and unfiltered information from stakeholders. The Board may benefit from having more 
frequent and informal communication channels with stakeholders as it makes important forward-
looking operational, strategic, and market-design decisions. It should not be required to rely only on 
communications through overly structured meetings with stakeholders (e.g., Liaison Committee 
meetings) and summaries filtered through PJM staff, however fair they may be. This would prevent 
situations where nuanced, non-conforming, or minority viewpoints are lost or reframed through the 
perspective of others. It may also allow for the Board to provide feedback to stakeholders earlier in the 
committee development process, allowing for Board guidance on paths forward that are aligned with 
the Board’s vision and most likely to garner the Board’s support. Of course, increased access needs to 
be balanced and cannot be unconstrained. Board members do not serve PJM on a full-time basis and 
cannot hear an unlimited number of stakeholder perspectives. There would need to be practical limits 
on the amount of time that each Board member should be available and mechanisms put in place to 
ensure that no one stakeholder or sector is able to monopolize Board bandwidth.

Question 6 – Board Vote Transparency  
 
Yes 
No 
In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made 
public or the Board be required to explain its reasoning? 
 
x 
 
Exelon does not support publication of Board votes or requirements for Board members to 
explain their votes. Thematically, Exelon supports PJM reforms to empower the Board and to increase 
independence, particularly in a period of heightened attention regarding developments in PJM. 
Requiring the Board to make public each of its votes would expose Board members to increased 
scrutiny and pressure at a time when they should be encouraged to take positions aligned with their 
best, unbiased judgement and insulated from blowback from market participants. The Board functions 
as a collective decision-making body and, as such, transparency is adequately served by disclosing the 
Board’s final action rather than the votes of individual members. The ability of the Board to act behind 
closed doors aligns with how most corporate boards function and allows appropriate levels of 
deliberative privilege and confidentiality. Moreover, it is Exelon’s view that governance changes 
should be focused on ensuring that the Board has more time to focus on the issues and engage 
meaningfully with stakeholders, a goal that is not advanced by requiring Board members to spend 
precious time documenting the reasoning for each vote that they take.

Question 7 – Public Board Meetings 
 
Yes 
No 
In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be 
open or available to the public? 
 
x 
 
Exelon does not support making meetings of the Board available to the public. Similar to the 
reasoning for not supporting publication and explanation of voting, this would not advance the 
objective of Board independence. Instead, it would likely lead the Board to hold curated, selectively-
scoped public meetings that, over concerns about optics and politics, would not involve meaningful, 
open dialogue or the taking of controversial votes. The Board would then do the bulk of its business in 
private executive sessions. Not only would these meetings be a drain on scarce Board time that is best 
spent on other activities, but they would also be unsatisfying to those who advocate for the value of 
public Board meetings to enhance transparency, thereby creating even more dissatisfaction with PJM 
governance. The Commission should seek reforms that provide more, not fewer, opportunities for the 
Board to engage in the deliberative process. In Exelon’s view, public Board meetings do not advance, 
and may undermine, that goal.

Question 8 – Public Interest Principle  
 
Yes 
No 
Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state 
that the responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public 
interest? 
x  
 
 
Exelon would support revisions to PJM’s governing documents to explicitly state that PJM and 
its Board are responsible to serve the public interest, as long as it is solely intended to memorialize, and 
not expand, requirements within the Federal Power Act (“FPA”). PJM is a public utility under the FPA, 
having received rights and obligations under that act from its public utility members. It already has 
appropriate legal and operational obligations for an entity regulated by the Commission to work in the 
public interest, primarily by ensuring grid reliability, providing non-discriminatory transmission access, 
and running markets and planning mechanisms that produce just and reasonable wholesale electricity 
rates.  Thus, depending on how the language is worded, it could be acceptable to include a public 
interest principle in the operating agreement as embedded in the FPA.

Question 9 – State Nominating Committee Representation  
 
Yes 
No 
Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the 
Nominating Committee? 
x 
 
 
Exelon supports a change to the Nominating Committee makeup that would give state 
representatives one vote. Providing states a vote would expand the perspectives represented on the 
Nominating Committee and ensure that perspective is included in the selection of Board members, in 
equal weight with the sectors currently represented on the Nominating Committee. Having this 
perspective and input from the states would serve to increase the confidence that the states have in how 
the Nominating Committee operates and makes its decisions, ultimately building trust in the Board on 
the part of the states and facilitating alignment between PJM processes and state action. Of course, the 
state representatives on the Nominating Committee would be expected to adhere to the same strict code 
of conduct and confidentiality rules as all other Nominating Committee members during and after their 
period of service. It would be up to the various relevant state entities to determine who to put forward 
as the Nominating Committee participant. Finally, Exelon does not support having more than one state 
representative voting member on the Nominating Committee, as that could create imbalance in how 
various interests are represented on the Nominating Committee and ultimately the Board.

Question 10 – Performance Metrics  
 
Yes 
No 
Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management 
compensation to their achievement? 
 
x 
 
Exelon does not support establishment of public performance metrics that are tied to PJM 
management compensation. Such a change would create several problems. First, as a general matter, 
there is concern that, per Goodhart’s Law, any time a measure (here: metric) becomes a target, it ceases 
to be a good measure. It would be undesirable to reorient PJM management’s personal incentive 
structure towards narrow—even if well intentioned—objectives. The result could likely be that 
achieving those metrics could become the overriding priority, potentially 1) prioritizing short-term 
outcomes at the expense of balance and long-term perspective that is necessary to achieve the broader 
mission of PJM, and/or 2) giving precedence and attention only to those issues tied to performance 
metrics, leaving other important but unincentivized issues by the wayside. Second, the PJM process 
and, indeed, the overall electric utility industry structure, is likely not conducive to tying work product 
in one period to actual outcomes—whether they be regulatory, reliability, price/affordability, etc. — in 
the same period. Owing to the long timescales of the utility industry, outcomes are often a significant 
lagging indicator to actions, often by years. Judging the quality of management performance, especially 
at an RTO, is only something that can be done over time. Third, PJM management is already facing 
unprecedented levels of scrutiny in an environment where it can be hard to identify high-caliber 
personnel alternatives. Layering publicly-reported performance assessments on top of already 
significant pressure would not be constructive to management retention and, if it caused attrition of top 
staff, could actually reduce outcome quality.

Question 11 – Advisory Stakeholder Process  
 
Yes 
No 
Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes 
only inform the Board? 
x 
 
 
Exelon supports transitioning the PJM stakeholder process to be advisory. This would be a 
change from the current binding endorsements process, and would entail a move to advisory polling for 
operations, planning, and market rules as well as for tariff changes and other activities. Our view is that 
this—paired with the necessary shift in section 205 rights—is likely the most impactful available 
governance reform and would be a significant way to address the concern expressed by Commission 
Chair Swett that PJM’s governance structure is unacceptable and the region may be “too big to 
function.” This perspective is aligned with the view advanced by the PJM Transmission Owners that 
the “PJM’s stakeholder process, which culminates in PJM Members Committee votes, has been widely 
acknowledged as an often fractious, difficult and time-consuming exercise. Intended to promote 
collaboration and compromise, it has in practice instead led to stasis, with many self-interested parties 
effectively exercising veto power over critical issues.”4 Instead, advisory polling would maintain the 
benefits associated with the existing large, coordinated regional grid that provides backbone reliability 
to 67 million customers across a wide 13 states and the District of Columbia. 
Binding member endorsement voting has limited PJM’s ability to act independently, nimbly, and 
effectively. The current binding structure has constrained PJM’s ability to modify its governing 
documents, either leading to inferior, compromised rule proposals or, in some cases, no ability to 
present proposals to the Commission at all. By its current process, PJM and its members are forced into

perspective to being considered as just another vote.

Question 12 – PJM 205 Rights Over Energy and Ancillary Service Markets 
 
Yes 
No 
Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over the energy market 
and ancillary services unencumbered by the requirement to obtain 
approval of two-thirds of the Members Committee? 
x 
 
 
Exelon supports PJM having section 205 rights over the energy and ancillary services markets, 
unencumbered by the requirement to obtain any approval from the Members Committee. This would 
align with the section 205 rights that PJM currently has for the capacity market. This conclusion 
follows, in part, from similar reasoning that leads Exelon to support a change to an advisory 
stakeholder process: the current binding endorsement voting approach gives stakeholders outsized 
authority over section 205 filings to the Commission. Moreover, and beyond the practical challenges 
that have flowed from the current process that binds the Board to the actions of the Members 
Committee, the Members Committee should not have anything resembling 205 rights in the first 
instance. These rights appropriately lie with PJM as the Commission-jurisdictional public utility, 
unencumbered by the obligation to act in accordance with action of the Members Committee, a third 
party. Under the FPA, only a public utility has section 205 rights to file to change its tariff without 
showing that its existing provision is unlawful, and PJM is that public utility. The Members Committee 
is not.  
This shifting of rights to the Board and away from the Members Committee restores the RTO’s 
proper rights and responsibilities. Under the current arrangement, in many instances PJM is prohibited 
from acting to modify its rules and processes without approval of the Members Committee; that is the 
definitional opposite of independence. It puts PJM in a position subservient to the Members Committee 
and in an inferior legal posture. PJM cannot act under section 205 to change its tariff without approval 
from the Members Committee, a body that may not act consistent with PJM’s interests, and may

obstruct changes to maintain reliability and advance market efficiency. Instead, perversely, in many 
instances PJM may only change its own tariff by filing a complaint under section 206 against itself. 
Restoring section 205 rights to the proper party (i.e., PJM) and advancing the necessary and 
corresponding shift to an advisory stakeholder structure puts the ultimate accountability and 
responsibility for federal regulatory filings with PJM, rather than wrongly transferring them to private 
market participants. Then, the Commission is put in the proper role of adjudicating the merit of those 
filings and whether they are just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory, rather than only having 
the chance to review those limited, delayed, watered-down filings that the PJM stakeholder process 
currently produces.

Yes 
No

Question 16 – Sector Weighted Voting Threshold 
 
Yes 
No 
Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be 
lowered from two-thirds sector-weighted voting? 
 
x 
 
Exelon does not support lowering the Members Committee sector-weighted voting threshold 
from two-thirds. This is not the solution to what ails the Members Committee process. While it is 
correct that the current sector-weighted voting thresholds have been a significant barrier to advancing 
reforms through the PJM stakeholder process, lowering the threshold could do as much to allow poorly

Question 17 – Affiliate Voting in Lower-Level Committees 
 
Yes 
No 
Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to 
senior standing committees, also apply to lower-level committees? 
 
x 
 
Exelon does not support extending the rules barring affiliate voting to the lower-level 
committees. The original motivation for the implementation of affiliate voting at lower-level 
committees was to foster innovation and ideation in discussions in those committees. We believe that 
affiliate voting still holds the potential to advance this objective, and to maintain the standing 
committees as open workshops for ideas as PJM stakeholders seek to address challenging issues. In this 
context, lower-level committees focus on highly technical operational and market mechanics. The 
affiliate structure allows companies that have more diverse business interests to express differentiated 
positions on behalf of those interests where there may be distinctions on technical issues. In the current 
context, it is also noteworthy that the affiliate voting bar at the senior committee level acts as a 
safeguard against affiliate abuse. Furthermore, should the stakeholder process shift to being advisory in 
nature, the importance of affiliate voting would diminish—allowed or not—as all votes would be 
informative to the ultimate decisions of the Board. Because the overall change to an advisory 
stakeholder structure is the more impactful change, changes to affiliate voting rules in the lower-level 
committees is of lesser consequence.

policymakers in the states, and therefore there may be room for an additional group, or groups, to 
ensure representation of those broader perspectives.

Question 19 – State Representative Voting 
 
Yes 
No 
Should state representatives have a formal voting role in the 
stakeholder process (limited to certain topics, such as resource 
adequacy and regional transmission cost allocation) in addition to 
the consumer advocate votes?   
x 
 
 
Should they want it, Exelon supports the option for representatives of state interests to have a 
formal voting role in the stakeholder process, which be cast in addition to consumer advocate votes. 
This position is consistent with our general view of the appropriateness of enhancing the role of the 
states in the PJM governance structure. Allowing for such voting, if this is a mechanism among others 
that state representatives view as appropriate and constructive, will allow them to formally express 
their position on issues of interest, along with the votes of other stakeholders. Granted, Exelon also 
supports shifting the PJM stakeholder process to be advisory (i.e., stakeholder votes only inform the 
Board), so in our view the process that the states would be voting in would inform but not bind the 
Board. This would have distinct advantages, however, when adding state representative voting, 
including 1) there would not be a need to determine how to integrate state representative votes into 
current binding sector-weighted structures and 2) by not constraining state representative votes to a 
specific treatment within a broader structure (e.g., sector-weighted voting), the Board would be free to 
consider state representative votes as they assess as appropriate and in a manner that may give such 
votes higher significance related to certain issues (e.g., resource adequacy).

Question 20 – OPSI Consultation on Resource Adequacy and Transmission Cost 
Allocation Filings  
 
Yes 
No 
Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a 
section 205 filing related to resource adequacy and transmission 
cost allocation? 
x 
 
 
Exelon supports consultation between PJM and OPSI prior to PJM making section 205 filings on 
either resource adequacy or transmission cost allocation. Regarding the latter, it must be recognized 
that PJM does not have section 205 filing rights, as they were retained by the PJM Transmission 
Owners, so such consultation would be with the PJM Transmission Owners and not PJM. PJM makes 
transmission cost allocation 205 filings on behalf of the PJM Transmission Owners and the PJM 
Transmission Owners consult with the Members Committee pursuant to CTOA Section 7.3.2. 
Additionally, the PJM Transmission Owners typically consult with OPSI prior to making 205 filings.  
Although OPSI already has the ability and does participate in stakeholder processes as well as 
conducting consultation with PJM and the PJM Transmission Owners, Exelon supports greater 
engagement by OPSI as well as opportunities for both the state commissions and the executive 
branches of each state to be involved with mechanisms that allow each state to speak with one voice 
through OPSI.
