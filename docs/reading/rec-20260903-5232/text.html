MOTION TO INTERVENE AND COMMENTS
OF APPALACHIAN VOICES

Pursuant to Rules 212 and 214 of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (the Commission) Rules of Practice and Procedure,1 Appalachian Voices submits this motion to intervene and comments supporting PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (PJM) proposed revisions to its Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities and the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff to establish Interim Resource Adequacy Service (IRAS), filed on August 13, 2026, in the above-captioned proceeding (IRAS Proposal).2

PJM’s IRAS Proposal is an ambitious, yet narrowly tailored solution to address the primary issue facing the region:  the outsized and unprecedent development of large loads and the unsustainable bill impacts they have created for residential ratepayers.  Appalachian Voices joined the Limited Protest of Public Interest Organizations—which supports PJM’s proposal with four modifications—yet writes separately to highlight the IRAS Proposal’s impact on Virginia, the birthplace of the data center boom, and address associated legal issues under the Federal Power Act.  By applying only to Load Serving Entities serving New Large Loads, whose size and operational attributes create unique concerns for the bulk transmission system and resource adequacy, PJM’s IRAS Proposal does not unduly discriminate.  Nor does it inappropriately cross jurisdictional lines, but will instead complement efforts already underway to address large load integration in Virginia.

I. MOTION TO INTERVENE
Appalachian Voices moves to intervene in this proceeding.  Appalachian Voices is a non- profit organization dedicated to bringing people together to protect the land, air, and water of Central and Southern Appalachia and advance a just transition to an equitable clean energy economy.  Appalachian Voices strives to shift to clean, 21st-century energy sources, including energy efficiency and low-emitting power sources, and stand up to monopoly utility practices that put profits over people.  Its ultimate goal is to establish economic solutions that create community wealth and sustain Appalachia’s mountains, forests, and waters.

Appalachian Voices has a substantial number of dues-paying members living in the PJM footprint, primarily in Virginia and North Carolina.  The outcome of this proceeding will impact the reliability of PJM’s system during constrained periods, the procurement of generation to supply large loads, and capacity costs that feed into retail rates.  Accordingly, Appalachian Voices has a substantial interest in this proceeding that cannot be adequately represented by any other party.  Further, by virtue of its organizational mission, Appalachian Voices’ participation in this proceeding is in the public interest.  Pursuant to Rule 214 of the Commission’s Rules of Practice and Procedure,3 Appalachian Voices respectfully requests that the Commission grant it full rights associated with party status in this proceeding.

Virginia originated the data center boom and remains its epicenter.  As of late 2024, it was home to thirteen percent of global data center capacity and twenty-five percent of data center capacity in the Americas.4  This trend only projects to accelerate.5  The heavy concentration of data center load in the state places significant demand on its electric grid. Dominion Energy Virginia (Dominion), a vertically integrated utility and PJM Transmission Owner, has asserted that “no other utility in the country has the magnitude of high load demand that Virginia is facing.”6  The utility has argued that the “scale, density of data center development, and load that [Dominion] must support significantly outpace[s]” other markets, making it a “uniquely concentrated and capital-intensive environment.”7  State and federal policies are only just now starting to catch up to the drastic changes that data centers have imposed on the state’s electric power system.

Traditional approaches to wholesale and retail cost allocation have had the effect of socializing the cost of procuring capacity for large loads across the broader customer base. 2. PJM’s Solution Is Narrowly Tailored to Maintain Reliability and Ensure that New Large Loads Pay their Fair Share of Capacity Costs.

Without appropriate protective measures, large loads will continue to jeopardize reliability and affordability in the PJM region.  PJM’s RPM, the mechanism created to enable the delivery of reliable capacity to PJM’s system, has failed to procure enough capacity to meet PJM’s reliability requirement two years in a row.  The BRA for the 2027/2028 Delivery Year cleared 6,623 MW short of the PJM Region Reliability Requirement and at the price cap of $333.44/MW-day.19  The BRA for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year, held this summer, cleared 6,831.3 MW short and also hit the market price cap of $325/MW-day.20  Large load development is the principal cause for PJM’s market clearing short and reaching the price cap.21 Subjecting Load Serving Entities that serve New Large Loads and have not arranged for their required capacity to IRAS appropriately balances two competing concerns.  On one hand, the IRAS Proposal appropriately protects regular ratepayers from reliability and cost impacts caused by New Large Loads.  As the primary drivers of the capacity shortfall, large loads and the Load Serving Entities that procure capacity on their behalf should be responsible for securing and paying for sufficient capacity to meet their demand.  To the extent New Large Loads and/or their Load Serving Entities are unable to bring sufficient capacity online in time to meet their demand, it is just and reasonable to keep those New Large Loads out of the capacity market and

B. The IRAS Proposal Reflects an Appropriate Exercise of Jurisdiction under the Federal Power Act and Will Complement Virginia’s Efforts to Integrate Large

Loads.

PJM’s proposal also respects jurisdictional boundaries and initiatives underway in the states seeking to address the challenges that large load integration presents.  The Federal Power Act grants the Commission jurisdiction over “the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce and the sale of electric energy at wholesale in interstate commerce[.]”23  The states maintain jurisdiction over “facilities used for the generation of electric energy or over facilities used in local distribution[.]”24  The Federal Power Act also reserves for the states jurisdiction over retail sales, i.e., sales to end-use customers.25

C. The IRAS Proposal Does Not Unduly Discriminate.
PJM’s IRAS Proposal is not unduly discriminatory because any such differentiation among capacity buyers is warranted by the size, operational characteristics, and grid impacts caused by New Large Load.  The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit (D.C. Circuit) has held that discrimination is undue only if differently treated entities are “similarly situated,”34 such that “there is no reason for the difference.”35  There is no undue discrimination where the Commission articulates relevant, significant facts to provide a rational basis for treating two entities differently,36 and the Commission has “wide discretion . . . to determine what constitute[s]” undue discrimination.37

The D.C. Circuit has specifically held that utilities may discriminate between two entities due to capacity constraints.  In Complex Consolidated Edison Company v. FERC, the Commission upheld differential treatment of two natural gas pipeline customers on this basis. The Commission found in the underlying order that, without the pipeline company’s disparate treatment of two different customers, one customer “could pull enough gas off the system to leave inadequate quantities available to meet other customers’” needs.38  The D.C. Circuit affirmed the Commission’s decision, stating that “the Commission gave an adequate explanation of how it reached its conclusion that there was no undue discrimination[.]”39  Further, the court found that “a rational, non-discriminatory basis existed for the difference in situation, namely operational constraints.”40

A similar principle underlies the treatment at issue here.  PJM expects to experience 29.7 gigawatts of load growth between 2025 and 2030.41  According to the Independent Market Monitor for PJM, “[d]ata center load growth is the primary reason for recent and expected capacity market conditions, including total forecast load growth, the tight supply and demand balance, and high prices[.]”42  Accordingly, it is reasonable to assume that any requirement to meet 100 percent of data center demand 100 percent of the time would jeopardize PJM’s ability to meet the demand of existing residential customers, given the current capacity constraints.  For this reason, a policy that requires only Load Serving Entities serving New Large Loads to match this demand with sufficient capacity or for the New Large Loads themselves to bring their own new capacity or be subject to IRAS, is not undue discrimination.  Here, the distinction between New Large Loads and existing load is based on legitimate operational constraints in PJM created by a sudden and unprecedented influx of large load demand.  Managing these operational constraints therefore provides a rational basis for differential treatment between Load Serving Entities serving New Large Loads relative to all other load.

The D.C. Circuit has also found that public utilities may discriminate between two entities based on different customer characteristics.  In Cities of Bethany, et al. v. FERC, the D.C. Circuit upheld the Commission’s approval of separate customer categories where two entities “have different customer profiles and load characteristics[.]”43  PJM’s IRAS Proposal likewise applies differential treatment to Load Serving Entities serving New Large Loads compared to all other load based on different customer profiles and load characteristics.
