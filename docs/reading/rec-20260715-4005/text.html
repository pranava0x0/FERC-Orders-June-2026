PRE-CONFERENCE STATEMENT OF J. ARNOLD QUINN 
SENIOR VICE PRESIDENT, REGULATORY POLICY 
VISTRA CORP. 
 
I appreciate the opportunity to participate in the Commission’s technical conference on 
PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms.  Vistra Corp. (Vistra) is the second-largest 
independent power producer in the United States, owning and operating a diverse fleet of 
generation facilities totaling approximately 44,000 megawatts (MWs) of capacity in markets 
across the country.  Vistra is one of the largest independent power producers in PJM (with 
approximately 15,000 MW of generation), serves millions of retail customers as a competitive 
power supplier, and is an active participant in all aspects of the PJM stakeholder process.  Vistra 
operates in every organized market in the United States except SPP.  Vistra is committed to 
stable, predictable and efficient market design, which makes the integrity and success of the PJM 
stakeholder process one of our top concerns – an interest we share with all market participants, 
customers, and States that depends on PJM’s markets to deliver reliable, affordable power. 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
PJM’s core mission is to ensure reliability through competitive market mechanisms, and 
its governance and stakeholder process are the foundation for the development of market rules 
that allow PJM to meet that mission.  Because the PJM stakeholder process is the mechanism 
through which technically complex, immensely consequential decisions are made, it is important 
to review the process’s health and efficacy, especially in this time of challenges and opportunities 
for the grid and industry.  The fundamental goal of the governance structure and stakeholder

2 
 
process is to ensure that PJM is prioritizing the right issues, anticipating future challenges, 
developing durable market-oriented solutions to current and future challenges, and being 
responsive to stakeholders while remaining independent.  Vistra recognizes that PJM operates 
with a political license that requires the PJM states to have a reasonable role in PJM’s 
functioning.  As a participant in five of the six FERC-jurisdictional RTOs/ISOs, we believe PJM 
compares favorably to other RTOs/ISOs in some respects but falls short in others.  PJM’s 
governance and stakeholder process is not fundamentally broken, but there is significant room 
for improvement.   
First, and critically, PJM governance has become distorted by the use of fast track 
processes that take the Board of Directors (Board) away from its essential mission – providing 
strategic guidance and management accountability – by tasking it with primary responsibility for 
decisions on complex market design on compressed timelines, often in crisis mode. Second, the 
stakeholder process is bogged down by lack of prioritization and discipline and by unnecessarily 
cumbersome and slow processes.  In addition, Vistra recognizes that PJM states are expressing a 
need for a more structured role in the governance and stakeholder process.  By addressing these 
issues through focusing on the fundamentals of the roles of the Board and PJM leadership, 
prioritizing initiatives and streamlining the stakeholder process (doing fewer, more impactful 
things faster), and supporting the states in collaborative participation, PJM can produce better 
and timelier solutions to the significant challenges before it. 
Vistra offers several actionable reforms that could improve PJM’s governance and 
stakeholder processes, as detailed in responses to many of Conference Agenda questions below: 
 Ensure the Board is in a strategic, rather than market design, role, including 
through limiting fast track processes that shift technical responsibilities to the 
Board.  
 Enhance participation of the PJM states.

 Institute a proactive, structured and collaborative process to plan, prioritize, and 
measure progress on stakeholder initiatives.   
 Streamline the mechanics of the stakeholder process, including through setting 
timelines for action. 
 Cautiously consider filing rights reforms.   
 
Most of these reforms can only be made by stakeholders in partnership with PJM’s management 
and Board.  It is critical that changes be considered deliberately and recognize the legal limits on 
the Commission’s jurisdiction over RTO/ISO governance and filing rights. 
II. 
PANEL QUESTIONS 
A. Panel 1: PJM Governance Mechanics – Board Authority, States’ Role, Filing 
rights, and Document Architecture. 
1. 
What is the PJM Board’s role in ensuring that PJM is sufficiently tackling the 
region’s operational and market challenges?  How does the Board evaluate its 
effectiveness in that role?  How often, and under what circumstances, does the 
Board act unilaterally, and should this change? 
The Board's appropriate role is strategic: providing guidance and accountability to ensure 
PJM ensures reliability through efficient competitive markets.  The Board should measure its 
success by the success of the organization as a whole – is it meeting operational and market 
needs while being responsive to stakeholders?  If the organization is falling short, the Board can 
adapt its guidance and hold leadership accountable. 
Unilateral Board action, such as in the Critical Fast Path Issues Process (CIFP) process 
that tasks the Board itself with designing a Federal Power Act (FPA) section 205 filing based on 
any number of stakeholder presentations, puts the Board in the inappropriate role of designing 
markets.  Market design is done best through the collaboration of committed stakeholders and 
PJM’s expert staff, under the leadership of an expert and engaged Board.  Fast track processes 
put the Board in the untenable position of choosing among numerous complex and competing

PDF page 4

4 
 
proposals from stakeholders and PJM staff, informed by fragmented materials and fractured 
indicative votes, and under time pressure and, often, significant public scrutiny.  Simply put, 
when the Board is forced to design markets rather than provide strategic guidance, it is put in a 
position to fail.  Moreover, unilateral Board action on market design undermines the health of the 
PJM organization because it diminishes the critical role of PJM leadership and its market design 
experts, who should be driving complex market reforms collaboratively with stakeholders.  
Therefore, unilateral Board action should be reserved for narrow issues that must be addressed 
quickly, like cost allocation for Department of Energy 202(c) actions to retain generating 
resources. 
To some extent, the Board may have felt compelled to use the CIFP process because PJM 
management and stakeholders had not already addressed an emergent issue.  Vistra believes this 
is a symptom of the stakeholder process taking on too many low priority issues, leaving too little 
space for the most pressing matters.  If the PJM Board is going to use CIFP less, PJM 
management and stakeholders need to better prioritize issues for attention, including identifying 
issues that have the potential to become emergencies if not addressed in a timely manner. 
2. 
How should states, through the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (OPSI) or other 
structures, interface with the Board to enable the region to meet operational and 
market challenges while respecting PJM’s independence? 
States have an important role to play in PJM governance and the stakeholder process.  
Their participation and buy-in can help build durable solutions to important issues facing their 
residents and market participants.  OPSI provides a platform for effective state participation, 
including interaction with the Board.  Indeed, when stakeholders need to reach sufficient 
agreement to enable a filing under FPA Section 205, OPSI would ideally serve as a hub for 
stakeholder consensus building.  However, it is clear that many state organizations do not feel

5 
 
that their voices are adequately heard, and that state voices can be fragmented and diffuse.  We 
support working with states and other stakeholders to address these concerns.  One way to 
enhance states’ role would be to support OPSI as an organization that reflects a broader spectrum 
of state voices with the resources to robustly participate in the stakeholder process.  Providing 
more resources to OPSI could allow it to expand its membership to include governors’ offices or 
other state representatives and increase its staffing and expertise to engage in PJM’s complex and 
technical process.  Another enhancement would be to include states on the PJM Nominating 
Committee so that they are represented in selecting members of the PJM Board.  This is similar 
to ISO-New England, where the nominating process includes representatives from the New 
England Conference of Public Utilities Commissioners (NECPUC). 
3. 
What governance reforms would best improve accountability and responsiveness? 
The most important change that would best improve accountability and responsiveness is 
limiting unilateral Board action to design markets, especially through the use of fast track 
stakeholder processes discussed in responses to Panel 1, Q1 in Panel 2, Q 8.  Adopting a 
structured initiative prioritization and tracking process as described in response to Panel 2, Q 5 
would also increase accountability and responsiveness. 
4. 
How does the division of Federal Power Act section 205 filing rights among PJM, 
Transmission Owners, and the Members Committee affect PJM’s ability to act 
effectively and quickly, when warranted, to address operational and market needs? 
The appropriate allocation of FPA Section 205 filing rights in PJM has become a 
prominent and controversial topic, with alternative allocations being drawn from other regions, 
particularly the “jump ball” FPA Section 205 filing option in ISO-New England.  The ISO-NE 
“jump ball” process is an example of a division of filing rights that has historically driven 
stakeholder consensus and organizational accountability.  Specifically, when ISO-NE develops a

PDF page 6

6 
 
tariff revision proposal for filing at FERC, the stakeholder body, NEPOOL, can file an 
alternative proposal under FPA section 205 if 60% of stakeholders support it.  It is critical to 
emphasize that in ISO-NE, it is the stakeholders as a collective – rather than just one interest – 
that can direct an alternative FPA Section 205 filing.  In PJM, adopting the “jump ball” process 
would require PJM stakeholders to relinquish its existing unilateral Section 205 rights over the 
PJM Operating Agreement that covers energy market provisions in exchange for rights to 
independently direct alternative FPA section 205 filings for changes to the Reliability Assurance 
Agreement (and having the right to direct alterative filings for changes to the Operating 
Agreement)if there is sufficient stakeholder support. 
In ISO-NE, the ability of the stakeholders to make an independent section 205 filing 
when there is a supermajority of stakeholders in support provides a strong incentive for diverse 
stakeholder groups to work together to develop an alternative proposal and for the ISO to be 
responsive to stakeholder views when developing a proposal.  States have significant influence in 
this process because it is extremely difficult to reach a supermajority for any alternative proposal 
without state support.  
In addition to representing a supermajority of stakeholders, a primary reason the “jump 
ball” process functions well in ISO-NE is because it is supported by dedicated stakeholder 
process infrastructure administered by NEPOOL, a non-ISO entity, that enables stakeholders to 
independently reach consensus. Any consideration of a similar mechanism for PJM would need 
to ensure that all stakeholders are represented in the alternative filing process and grapple with 
how comparable supporting infrastructure, which would be a fundamental change to PJM’s 
stakeholder process structure, would be built.  Adopting something like a “jump ball” without 
that infrastructure would not be an easy or complete answer.

6. 
Recognizing regional differences, are there lessons from other regional 
transmission organization/independent system operator (RTO/ISO) governance 
structures that PJM could reasonably adopt to improve its responsiveness to the 
region’s operational and market challenges? 
 
As noted below, a common, successful practice among many RTOs/ISOs is the formal 
and collaborative planning, prioritization and tracking of stakeholder initiatives like ISO-NE’s 
Annual Work Plan and Multi-Year Roadmap process and CAISO’s Policy Catalog and Policy 
Roadmap prioritization process discussed below.  This is a common-sense best practice that – 
because it provides a rational, disciplined, and forward-looking structure to the stakeholder 
process – would rapidly improve PJM’s ability to timely respond to the full spectrum of 
operational and market challenges. 
B. Panel 2: PJM Stakeholder Process – Design, Transparency, and Decision-making 
1. 
Do sector-weighted voting and existing voting thresholds strike the right balance 
between achieving consensus and taking timely action? 
Sector-weighted voting and PJM’s existing thresholds are not the primary source of the 
stakeholder process’s dysfunction, and any change to them should be approached cautiously, on a 
consensus basis, and only after deliberate consideration. The more significant drivers of slow, 
unsatisfying outcomes are procedural: the absence of a formal prioritization and roadmap process 
and an overly granular, form-over-substance proposal architecture discussed in response to Panel 
2, Q 7 below.  Reforming those elements would do more to improve the speed and quality of 
decisions than adjusting voting thresholds. 
2. 
What transparency reforms would improve the quality of PJM’s stakeholder process 
and increase stakeholder confidence in its decision-making? 
Adopting a proactive issue identification, prioritization, planning and tracking process, 
along the lines of ISO-NE’s and CAISO’s models described above, is a straightforward

PDF page 8

8 
 
transparency reform available to PJM.  It would provide a central process for stakeholders to 
propose initiatives and PJM to publicly react to them; the resulting work plan or roadmap would 
show stakeholders and the region what PJM plans to take up, why, and on what timeline, and 
would let stakeholders see when and why priorities shift – and, crucially for speed, when 
processes are falling behind planned timelines. 
3. 
Are there structured opportunities in the stakeholder process for state participation 
(e.g., defined consultation windows, joint technical sessions) to provide input on 
topics that are of interest to the states? 
 
OPSI and the Board are in the best position to determine the most productive way for 
them to consult on relevant issues, whether through regular consultations, ad hoc technical 
meetings, or other avenues.  Whatever the forum, it would be important for OPSI to be an 
effective conduit for that engagement by having sufficient resources to represent diverse state 
interests  
5. 
What reforms to the stakeholder process could ensure PJM can meet the region’s 
challenges?  
In addition to the limitation of fast track stakeholder process and unilateral Board market 
design action discussed in other responses, a deceptively simple reform that would improve both 
quality and speed is for PJM to adopt a formal and collaborative process to proactively identify, 
prioritize, and track its major initiatives. Unlike other RTOs/ISOs, PJM currently lacks such a 
mechanism to transparently evaluate the importance of an initiative proposed by either PJM staff 
or stakeholders, assess the organization’s bandwidth to take it on, or coordinate it with other 
ongoing reform efforts. As a result, initiatives are introduced by PJM or stakeholders, at any 
time, without a plan — producing an undisciplined, uncoordinated process that too often falls

PDF page 9

9 
 
behind emerging critical issues.  Without such a process, there is no benchmark – including 
timelines– that the stakeholder process can be measured against. 
ISO-New England's Annual Work Plan and Multi-Year Roadmap offer a proven model. 
The Annual Work Plan sets and publishes ISO-NE’s major priorities, projects, and activities for 
the coming year, with a mid-year update, developed with stakeholder input through the NEPOOL 
Participants Committee and state representatives via NECPUC and the New England States 
Committee on Electricity (NESCOE). The Multi-Year Roadmap provides a long-term view of 
major programs — capacity market reforms, transmission planning, operational tools, IT 
modernization — so stakeholders can anticipate when major topics will come forward and the 
ISO can be confident that it has the resources needed to complete them. 
California ISO’s Policy Catalog and Policy Roadmap serve a similar function: 
stakeholders submit potential initiatives, CAISO runs a stakeholder-led prioritization process 
using workshops and surveys, considers and the results in the context of CAISO’s priorities and 
resources and develops three-year Roadmap that is voted on by its Board of Directors.  The 
Roadmap is then updated as circumstances change, including through a regular mid-year review. 
These processes create transparency (what the RTO plans to do, and why), legitimacy 
(stakeholders help shape the plan), resource alignment (initiatives are matched to budget and 
staffing), and accountability (timely progress can be measured against a public plan).  Adopting 
an equivalent process is low-hanging fruit that could transform how the PJM stakeholder process 
functions. 
6. 
What near-term reforms to stakeholder representation could PJM and its Members 
take to improve speed in decision-making while preserving stakeholder input? 
The current stakeholder representation structure is deliberately built to prevent any one 
stakeholder group from capturing market design outcomes. That balance should not be sacrificed

10 
 
in the name of speed, which can be improved through other reforms like initiative prioritization 
and process streamlining.  Recognizing that Vistra sees some merit in the ISO-NE/NEPOOL 
“jump ball” process and infrastructure to drive accountability and responsiveness, we should be 
cautious about reforms whose primary effect is to reduce representation rather than to improve 
the quality or timeliness of decisions. 
7. 
Are there elements of PJM’s committee structure that routinely contribute to delays?  
Are there stakeholder processes or issues that could be expedited or changed to 
provide greater focus on key issues and allow faster or more unilateral action on 
others? 
Significant streamlining and simplification of the PJM stakeholder process would 
improve both focus and speed.  As it stands, the process is needlessly complex, time-consuming, 
and lacks clear objectives or timelines.  The PJM stakeholder process begins with a problem 
statement and issue charge that can take months to be finalized.  Despite significant time devoted 
to debating these documents, they frequently lack any clear objective or guiding principle and 
are often ignored or changed as the process progresses.  The Deactivation Enhancement Senior 
Task Force (DESTF) problem statement, for example, contains a vague statement about a need to 
reevaluate the rules for generator deactivation rather than a concrete problem that needs to be 
addressed. 
The stakeholder process is also needlessly multi-layered: stakeholders move proposed 
reforms through task forces, subcommittees, and higher- level committees, each with different 
voting structures.  These groups typically meet only on a monthly basis, which prevents timely 
action.  And these meetings do not discuss concepts or shared goals but rather break proposals 
down into minute component parts in an often impenetrable excel spreadsheet—“the matrix”—
that acts as the driving force for discussion.  The DESTF matrix, for example, contains 18 
“interests” reflecting objectives of all stakeholders.  When complex proposals move from a task

11 
 
force to a higher committee, the process of education and consensus building must begin all 
over.  Stakeholders and state representatives that do not have the resources to devote full-time 
representatives or technical experts to every issue at the task force level are at a significant 
disadvantage.  Streamlining and simplifying the committee structure, establishing clear 
objectives for reforms in advance, and adopting reasonable timelines could dramatically improve 
the speed and quality of reforms.   
8. 
Should PJM increase the use of fast-path or time-bound review procedures to 
expedite committee action on high-importance topics?  
The best response to a slow stakeholder process that fails to act on major issues in a 
reasonable amount of time is common sense reforms that bring prioritization, discipline and 
accountability to that process and remove unnecessary and unproductive layers from it, as 
discussed above.  PJM can and should do fewer things faster, and every stakeholder initiative 
should have a reasonable timeline to measure progress against.  Vistra also supports deadlines 
(“shot clocks”) for task force, working group, or subcommittee actions on more urgent reforms.       
Fast track processes like CIFP are not the solution to a stakeholder process that fails to 
act on major issues in a reasonable amount of time.  Indeed, last year’s large load CIFP process 
shows that fast track processes do not guarantee quick action.  And even if these processes can 
result in faster filings, they shortcut the stakeholder input and collaboration that is critical for 
developing successful, durable market design.  They do not allow stakeholders to coalesce 
around core principles and they put the Board in the position of designing markets—which, as 
discussed above, is a misuse of its role.  It is no wonder that last year’s CIFP process to tackle 
large load issues failed and this year’s reliability backstop struggled to reach consensus: 
stakeholders lacked direction and the Board was asked to make major market design decisions

under compressed timelines and information overload.  Fast track processes are suited to narrow 
issues that must be addressed quickly.   
III. 
CONCLUSION 
I thank the Commission for the opportunity to participate in this technical conference.  As 
noted at the outset, it is healthy to review the efficacy of PJM governance and its stakeholder 
process at this time of industry transformation.  Improving governance and the stakeholder 
process is also important because when the stakeholder process does not provide solutions to 
problems of reliability and affordability, policymakers are motivated to apply public pressure to 
drive solutions.  But that is not how the system was intended to work.  Informed stakeholders 
and expert staff should be able to leverage an effective process to develop needed tariff reforms 
and timely file those proposals under FPA section 205.  When PJM and stakeholders have not 
adequately prioritized and addressed an emergent challenge, FPA section 206 – which is subject 
to notice and comment and ultimately judicial review - provides a path for the Commission and 
other policymakers to drive change where the tariff is unjust and unreasonable.
