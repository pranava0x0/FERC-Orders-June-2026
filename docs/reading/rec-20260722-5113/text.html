COMMENTS OF THE PJM CITIES AND COMMUNITIES COALITION
The PJM Cities and Communities Coalition respectfully submits these written comments in connection with the Commission-led technical conference on PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms in Docket No. AD26-7-000.  We applaud the Commission’s focus on governance issues within PJM, and we hope to aid the Commission’s investigation into these matters via the comments below. About PJMCCC

The PJM Cities and Communities Coalition (PJMCCC) is a group of 21 local governments representing ~15% of the population within PJM's territory. Our members are energy managers and sustainability staff of large municipal end-users. As local governments, PJMCCC members are both large end-use consumers of energy and public-sector entities responsible for the health and well-being of our communities. This dual role places us in a unique position: we are affected by the impacts of PJM’s energy market decisions on our bills while being directly accountable to our approximately ten million residents who depend on reliable and affordable electricity. Furthermore, local government policy goals depend in part on consideration in PJM’s planning and decision-making so that the technologies and strategies needed to meet our mandates and goals are appropriately valued by markets. PJMCCC’s interests are unique and are not fully represented by any other stakeholder group, making our coalition a valuable partner in planning for PJM. For example, we are currently collaborating with PJM’s State Policy Solutions team to shape the development of the local policy portal to support PJM’s compliance with FERC Order 1920. We have been helping inform the policy fields to be used for aggregating local government policies, goals, siting opportunities, and siting restrictions. Additionally, we submitted local building and vehicle electrification objectives to PJM’s Load Analysis Subcommittee that were used in the 2024 load forecast. PJM is at a critical juncture, and decisions made now will be felt for decades to come. We strongly believe that local government policies and perspectives are crucial for ensuring that the future energy system is reliable, affordable, and sustainable.

PJM politics
PJMCCC is excited to see FERC take proactive steps toward exercising its authority over RTO/ISO governance, particularly with respect to PJM. While FERC has long held authority over RTO/ISO governance, many issues with RTO/ISO governance have persisted without FERC action.1  The time for FERC to use its authority to address pervasive issues in RTO/ISO governance, particularly within PJM, is nigh.

FERC’s main premise for this was that California law required the governor of California to approve candidates for the CAISO board and that the state of California was also a major participant in CAISO markets.

However, the DC Circuit for the Court of Appeals rejected FERC’s attack on CAISO’s board selection process, writing that FERC had no authority to prescribe how a CAISO selects its board.5  While the DC Circuit preserved FERC’s authority over governance - noting that FERC could legitimately revoke an RTOs’ certification as an RTO on governance independence grounds - the DC Circuit found that FERC had overreached with regards to CAISO. While the DC Circuit left the door open for FERC to pursue further governance actions in CAISO, FERC declined to do so.

Despite CAISO v FERC, FERC has used its rates authority to assert authority over governance.  FERC used its rates authority to reject revisions to ISO-NE’s governance, specifically ISO-NE’s membership rules in the stakeholder process, stating that “stakeholder processes are a practice affecting rates.”6   FERC’s order on this was unchallenged.

A key issue with PJM is that its board is not truly independent from its members, who are also market participants.  PJM’s board is selected by its members, which limits the independence of the board from the outset. A further governance challenge is that PJM’s stakeholder process has become compromised as well.  As law professor Shelly Welton explains, the congressional repeal of the Public Utility Holding Companies Act (PUHCA) has allowed for swaths of mergers within the utility industry.7 This has resulted in merging together entities that once represented different sides of a transaction - creating problems for the balances of interests that RTO governance might intend to create.  When a “load” stakeholder is owned by the same entity as a “generation” stakeholder, neither is truly representative of its interests in an RTO stakeholder process. As professor Welton writes, these mergers “...create challenges by concentrating economic and political power in a small number of companies—not least for theories of RTO governance, which rely upon internal industry checks to legitimate RTO decision-making.  These internal checks presume opposing interests that do not exist because holding companies have consolidated across demand- and supply-side affiliates.”8  Professor Welton’s cross-RTO assessment of this issue holds true for PJM.

As such, FERC’s authority over governance can be usefully deployed at this critical moment to resolve serious issues in PJM - and FERC should feel empowered to do so. Panel 1: PJM Governance Mechanics – Board Authority, States’ Role, Filing Rights, and Document Architecture

1.​ What is the PJM Board’s role in ensuring that PJM is sufficiently tackling the region’s operational and market challenges?  How does the Board evaluate its effectiveness in that role?  How often, and under what circumstances, does the Board act unilaterally, and should this change?

PJMCCC believes that the PJM board’s role is to make responsible decisions for the entire PJM region, including all stakeholders, rather than simply reflecting the preferences of member utilities. As such, PJMCCC believes that the PJM board should be made more representative of regional stakeholders and their interests. However, the PJM board is unlikely to become representative in this way under the current process used to select PJM board members. Under the current process, only PJM members vote to decide who sits on the PJM board - a process that can only guarantee that the PJM board will only represent PJM members and, more specifically, PJM member utilities that have the most voting power. This in turn sets up PJM to be led by a board that prefers policies favoring member utilities’ interests over other stakeholders and the public interest.

Solution:
PJM’s board selection process should be re-organized to require participation from stakeholders other than PJM Members.  PJM’s board selection process should include input from states, consumer advocates, and other stakeholders so as to deliver a board that is representative not only of PJM member utilities but of the region’s many other stakeholder sectors as well.  This would deliver a board that is both representative

Panel 2: PJM Stakeholder Process – Design, Transparency, and Decision-Making 1.​ Do sector-weighted voting and existing voting thresholds strike the right balance between achieving consensus and taking timely action?

PJMCCC believes that non-utility stakeholders like local governments are underrepresented in the PJM’s stakeholder process.  Even if PJMCCC could become a member and vote in the process, PJMCCC would not have hardly any influence due to broader structural issues with PJM’s voting structure.  As described above, professor Welton explains how mergers and acquisitions have made the stakeholder sector definitions muddied when it comes to actually representing public interests.28

The solution

FERC should direct PJM to develop more cohesive sector formation and stricter requirements that a member must meet to join a sector, as well as a broader variety of sectors in order to represent stakeholder that currently aren’t eligible to become members.  In developing prescriptions for PJM’s stakeholder sector requirements, FERC could draw upon ROWE’s Stakeholder Representatives Committee design. ROWE’s design defines specific sectors and requirements that a member must have in order to vote with that sector.  Moreover, the sectors in ROWE’s design are broadly representative of a variety of stakeholders that PJM’s governance should aspire to represent as well.

Who would do it, under what authority, and how
As described for similar changes to the PJM stakeholder process, FERC could require PJM to make these changes under its own assertion that stakeholder processes are a practice affecting rates.  PJM could of course also voluntarily make these changes itself.

4.​ Do existing governing document provisions (e.g., Operating Agreement, Manuals) impede stakeholder decision-making and what changes could be made to address them?

As indicated elsewhere in these comments, PJMCCC leaves it to other entities to opine on the opportunities for improving stakeholder decisions via the governing documents. 5.​ What reforms to the stakeholder process could ensure PJM can meet the region’s challenges?

​
Beyond the reforms outlined elsewhere in these comments, a further useful step would be for FERC to direct PJM to undertake the creation of an independent transmission monitor.34  An independent transmission monitor would be useful for providing review of the local transmission projects that are proposed by PJM utilities, which currently have no review and fall through a regulatory gap.  An independent transmission monitor would also be useful for ensuring consideration of non-transmission alternatives, like advanced transmission technologies, that would improve the overall efficiency of the PJM grid while also improving the affordability of electricity for PJM residents.

As indicated elsewhere in these comments, PJMCCC strongly believes that stakeholder input needs to be expanded at this juncture, rather than merely “preserved.” PJMCCC views a lack of effort on PJM’s part to gather and respond to non-member stakeholder input as a key factor in getting the PJM region into the crisis it faces today. Much of the contemporary discourse on PJM issues focuses on the PJM capacity market.  Empirical research has found that PJM governance influences PJM capacity market outcomes.35  So the assertion that PJM’s unresponsive governance has led to the capacity market crisis facing the region should come as no surprise.

As such, PJMCCC believes that the crisis will be resolved primarily through expanding stakeholder input rather than expediting decisions.  To that end, PJMCCC refers the Commission to its recommendations elsewhere in these comments as to how stakeholder input and engagement could be expanded.  In addition, the PJMCCC refers the Commission to its earlier recommendations to reform the PJM board as a means of achieving both faster and more responsive decision-making from PJM.

PJMCCC notes that PJM has increasingly used fast-path procedures to address recent issues.36  However, In some cases those procedures have failed to deliver consensus and timely decision-making, such as the large load interconnection CIFP proceeding in which no proposal received enough votes to pass.37  As such, PJMCCC views the use of fast-path procedures as creating a strong risk of failing to resolve the underlying problems with PJM governance. 9.​ Recognizing regional differences, are there lessons from other RTO/ISO stakeholder processes that PJM could reasonably adopt to improve speed in voting processes and stakeholder representation?

As indicated elsewhere in these comments, PJMCCC believes that the ROWE’s newly proposed independent regional governance structure would be a valuable model for PJM.  The ROWE’s governance structure builds on lessons learned from other forms of regional market governance and develops robust processes for avoiding some of the failures seen in those other forms.  The ROWE’s structure moves away from a utility-centric governance model and toward one that gives more power to states, ratepayer advocates, and other public interest groups to influence market policies. FERC should consider the governance features of the ROWE and require PJM to adopt some of them, as outlined elsewhere in these comments.

We thank the Commission for the opportunity to comment, and we look forward to the Conference and to hearing remarks from both the panelists and Commissioners.
