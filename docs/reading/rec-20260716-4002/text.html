PRE-FILED STATEMENT OF PAMELA QUINLAN
 PRINCIPAL, GQS NEW ENERGY STRATEGIES

Preserving the Regional Compact: Governance, Accountability, and Resource Adequacy in PJM's Next Phase of Evolution

Chairman Swett and Commissioners Rosner, See, Chang, and LaCerte:
Thank you for the opportunity to participate in this technical conference. I appreciate the Commission's decision to convene this discussion at a time when PJM is confronting some of the most significant operational, market, and reliability challenges in its history. My name is Pamela Quinlan. I am a Principal and Co-Founder of GQS New Energy Strategies. I am participating today in my personal capacity, and the views expressed in this statement are my own and do not reflect the views of my clients.

I have participated on PJM matters from a number of different perspectives for the past two decades. I first became involved in PJM in 2005 while working for one of the early competitive demand response providers. I later represented Rockland Electric Company as a voting member of PJM's Transmission Owner Sector. I subsequently served at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission as an Energy Industry Analyst, Technical Advisor to Commissioner Rich Glick, as well as Chief of Staff. During my time at the Commission, I worked on numerous rulemakings, proceedings and technical conferences involving various aspects of PJM's market design, transmission planning, and resource adequacy. Today, as a consultant, I advise a broad range of market participants—including utilities, transmission developers, generation developers, storage developers and large electricity customers investing billions of dollars across the PJM footprint. Those experiences have provided a unique vantage point from which to observe PJM's evolution. They have also led me to one consistent conclusion: every constituency believes PJM's governance process is not working for them.

Suppliers have argued that stakeholder processes move too slowly to respond to changing market conditions. Consumer advocates have argued that markets fail to adequately protect customers. States have expressed frustration that regional institutions are insufficiently responsive to state policy objectives. Transmission owners, public power entities, large end use customers,

PDF page 2

environmental organizations have all, at different times, concluded that PJM's governance structure is contributing to the challenges confronting the region.

The consistency of those criticisms is striking. When every constituency believes the system is working against it, the natural instinct is to conclude that the governance model itself is broken. I am not convinced that is the correct or complete diagnosis.

PJM unquestionably faces serious challenges. It was slow to modernize its generation interconnection process, slower than many of its peers to embrace forward-looking transmission planning, and unprepared for the unprecedented pace of large-load growth now reshaping the region. Those shortcomings contributed directly to today's resource adequacy concerns, even as PJM has more recently undertaken significant reforms to address them.

At the same time, electricity demand is increasing at a pace not experienced in decades, while the infrastructure necessary to support that growth has become increasingly difficult and time- consuming to develop. Importantly, the region's greatest constraint today is not a lack of commercial interest or private capital. Billions of dollars are prepared to be invested in new generation, transmission, storage, and large load development.

The defining constraint on the electric system today is infrastructure: our collective ability to site it, permit it, interconnect it, finance it, and build it quickly enough to maintain reliability. No governance reform, market design, or resource adequacy construct will succeed unless the institutions responsible for planning and developing that infrastructure are capable of working together effectively. Those challenges deserve the Commission's attention, and governance reform can undoubtedly improve how PJM responds to them. But governance is only part of the story.

Increasingly, the decisions that create new reliability obligations are made by institutions outside PJM's direct control, while PJM remains responsible for maintaining reliability across one of the largest synchronized electric systems in the world. The recent resource adequacy challenges confronting PJM did not cause this disconnect. They revealed it. The institutions creating new reliability obligations are increasingly different from the institutions responsible for ensuring those obligations can be met. State legislators, Governors and commissions make policy decisions that either advance or halt new load, generation, supply, and transmission. Governance reforms can improve PJM’s decision-making, but they cannot, by themselves, resolve that growing disconnect. In my view, aligning authority, responsibility, and accountability across the regional compact is the central challenge confronting PJM today.

The objective of this proceeding, therefore, should not simply be to improve PJM's governance. It should be to preserve and strengthen the regional compact that has made PJM one of the nation's most successful electric institutions.

PDF page 3

Regional coordination remains one of the greatest achievements of modern electricity policy. Through coordinated system operations, regional transmission planning, competitive wholesale markets, and security-constrained economic dispatch, PJM has delivered substantial economic and reliability benefits to the 67 million consumers across thirteen states and the District of Columbia. Those benefits become even more valuable as electricity demand accelerates, resource portfolios diversify, and the bulk power system grows more complex.

Ultimately, the purpose of preserving the regional compact is to preserve the reliable and affordable electric service those institutions were created to deliver. Reliability and affordability are too often treated as competing objectives when durable affordability depends upon maintaining a reliable electric system supported by adequate infrastructure and timely investment. Consumers bear the costs of inadequate planning and resource shortages just as surely as they bear the costs of unnecessary investment. The challenge before the Commission is to ensure that the institutions supporting the regional compact can deliver both. Meeting that challenge requires more than governance reform alone. It requires stronger institutional leadership, a more clearly defined partnership between PJM and the states, and a renewed focus on aligning responsibility for creating reliability obligations with accountability for meeting them.

This statement offers six principal observations.
1. The regional compact remains fundamentally sound and should be strengthened rather than weakened.

2. Many of PJM's recent challenges were preceded by failures of strategic long-term planning and institutional adaptation rather than defects inherent in the governance model itself.

3. PJM does not principally lack legal authority. It lacks the institutional confidence to exercise the authority it already possesses. Governance reform should therefore focus less on expanding legal authorities and more on empowering an independent Board to establish priorities, manage the stakeholder process, make difficult decisions, and exercise its expert judgment when consensus cannot be achieved.

4. States should be recognized as institutional partners, not merely stakeholders, with a defined role in the development of long-term planning and resource adequacy initiatives. States occupy a unique constitutional and regulatory role. They determine where generation and transmission facilities may be built, regulate the utilities responsible for serving retail customers, and increasingly shape the economic development and resource policy decisions that drive future reliability needs. That partnership should begin earlier, be more strategic, and be supported by the technical resources necessary for states to participate effectively, while allowing each state to determine how it wishes to be represented.

5. The next evolution of resource adequacy should better align responsibility for serving load with responsibility for demonstrating that sufficient accredited resources exist to support that load.

6. Finally, governance reform should be evaluated not by whether it redistributes influence among stakeholders, but by whether it strengthens the regional institutions that consumers increasingly depend upon.

The recommendations that follow are offered in that spirit.

Preserving the Regional Compact
The debate over PJM's future should begin with a simple question: Why does the regional compact exist in the first place?  Recent discussions have understandably questioned whether PJM has become too large, too complex, or too difficult to govern effectively. Those concerns deserve careful consideration. But they should not cause us to lose sight of the extraordinary benefits that regional coordination has delivered, or assume that today's challenges demonstrate that the regional model itself has reached its limits.

PJM was never intended to replace the authority of states, utilities, or private investment. Rather, it was created because certain functions are performed more cost-effectively at a regional scale than they can be by individual utilities or states acting alone. Its success has always depended on each institution performing the responsibilities that only it can perform. Operating an interconnected bulk power system, dispatching generation economically across multiple jurisdictions, planning transmission over a broad geographic footprint, and administering competitive wholesale markets all produce benefits that no single state or utility could achieve independently.

Every day, PJM coordinates the operation of generating units across thirteen states and the District of Columbia. Through security-constrained economic dispatch, it continuously selects the lowest-cost combination of available resources capable of serving demand while respecting the physical limitations of the transmission system. Regional transmission planning recognizes that reliability challenges do not stop at state boundaries, allowing infrastructure to be developed where it provides the greatest overall benefit rather than where individual jurisdictions happen to exercise authority. Competitive wholesale markets likewise benefit from regional scale by supporting greater liquidity, broader competition, and more efficient investment than would be possible in a collection of fragmented balancing authorities.

The scale of PJM is therefore not an accident. It is one of its greatest strengths. A larger footprint allows operators to share reserves across a wider geographic area, dispatch a broader and increasingly diverse resource portfolio, respond more effectively to extreme weather, and maintain reliability at lower overall cost than would otherwise be possible. As electricity demand

PDF page 5

accelerates, weather patterns become more volatile, and resource portfolios continue to diversify, those regional benefits become increasingly important.

None of that means PJM should operate exactly as it did twenty years ago. The electric system has changed dramatically. Load is growing at rates not seen in decades. Data centers and other large industrial facilities are developing on timelines that frequently outpace traditional utility planning cycles. Generation, transmission, and natural gas infrastructure take longer to permit, interconnect, and construct. States are pursuing increasingly diverse policy objectives, while new technologies are transforming both the supply and demand sides of the electric system. Those changes require the institutions supporting the regional compact to evolve.

That need for institutional evolution, however, should not be mistaken for evidence that regional coordination itself has failed. Quite the opposite. The more dynamic and interconnected the electric system becomes, the greater the value of regional coordination. The question before the Commission is therefore not whether PJM is too large to function. It is whether the institutions supporting the regional compact have evolved quickly enough to govern a much larger, faster- moving, and more complex electric system. In my view, they have not. But that is an argument for strengthening the regional compact, not abandoning it. The challenge is not that regional markets have outlived their usefulness. It is that governance, planning, accountability, and institutional coordination have not kept pace with the profound changes occurring across the electric sector. The sections that follow explain why I believe stronger Board leadership, a more meaningful partnership with the states, and clearer accountability for resource adequacy provide a more durable path than weakening the regional model that has served consumers so well for more than two decades.

How PJM Fell Behind
None of this is to suggest that PJM has not made important mistakes. It has. Acknowledging those mistakes is important because they contributed directly to many of the challenges confronting the region today. At the same time, it is equally important to distinguish between those mistakes and the broader institutional questions now before the Commission. Better governance alone would not necessarily have prevented them, nor will correcting them alone prepare PJM for the electric system that is emerging.

The common thread running through many of PJM's recent challenges is not poor governance, but delayed institutional adaptation. As the electric system changed, PJM was often slower than the pace of that change. Three examples illustrate the point.

First, PJM waited too long to modernize its generation interconnection process. While other regional transmission organizations transitioned earlier to cluster studies and more forward- looking queue management, PJM continued to rely on a serial interconnection process that became increasingly unworkable as project volumes accelerated. By the time comprehensive

PDF page 6

reforms were adopted, the backlog had grown so large that PJM ultimately paused new interconnection requests for several years while implementing a new framework. That pause occurred at precisely the moment electricity demand began accelerating, delaying the interconnection and availability of new resources when they were becoming most needed. The region continues to feel the effects of that delay.

Second, PJM was slower than many of its peers to fully embrace long-term, forward-looking planning. Historically, its planning processes excelled at identifying and correcting discrete reliability violations on the existing system in the near-term. As the electric sector evolved, however, planning increasingly required anticipating where the system was heading rather than simply responding to where it had been. PJM is in the midst of substantial progress through capacity expansion modeling, Order No. 1920 adoption, and more comprehensive assessments of transmission and generation needs. Those reforms represent meaningful progress, but they arrived only after the underlying trends had already begun reshaping the region. Finally, the explosive growth in large electricity demand exposed shortcomings in PJM's forecasting assumptions. No forecasting process will perfectly anticipate disruptive change, but the warning signs surrounding accelerating data center development and other large industrial loads emerged over a number of years. Earlier recognition of those trends could have prompted earlier transmission planning, earlier consideration of resource adequacy reforms, and earlier coordination among PJM, the states, utilities, and market participants. PJM's recent reforms, including independent validation of large-load forecasts and more rigorous review of utility submissions, are important improvements that should significantly strengthen planning going forward.

These were meaningful mistakes, and they deserve acknowledgment. But I do not believe they fully explain PJM's current predicament, nor do I believe different governance alone would necessarily have prevented them. Institutions often struggle to recognize structural change while it is occurring, particularly when that change accelerates rapidly.

The recent surge in electricity demand did not create the disconnect now confronting PJM. It revealed it. For many years, relatively stable demand growth and comfortable reserve margins allowed the region to absorb institutional misalignment without immediate reliability consequences. Today's resource adequacy challenges have exposed that those margins no longer exist. Increasingly, questions involving governance, planning, market design, and stakeholder processes are all converging on a single structural issue: who is ultimately responsible for ensuring that sufficient infrastructure and accredited resources exist to serve future load. That is the challenge the remaining sections of this statement seek to address. They focus less on correcting yesterday's mistakes than on preparing PJM for tomorrow's electric system by strengthening institutional leadership, creating a more meaningful partnership with the states, and restoring clearer accountability for resource adequacy. None of those recommendations

requires abandoning the regional compact. To the contrary, each is intended to strengthen it by ensuring that every institution performs the responsibilities it is uniquely positioned to carry out.

What Governance Can—and Cannot—Fix
The Commission has convened this technical conference to consider whether reforms to PJM's governance and stakeholder processes are necessary. I believe that question is both timely and important because governance matters. The way institutions make decisions affects not only the quality of those decisions, but whether they are made early enough to avoid crises. Good governance cannot guarantee good outcomes, but it can improve an institution's ability to recognize change, establish priorities, and act before emerging challenges become reliability problems.

That is particularly important today because the defining challenge facing the electric system is no longer simply attracting investment. It is ensuring that the infrastructure necessary to support that investment is planned, permitted, interconnected, and constructed before reliability needs arise. Markets remain extraordinarily effective at allocating capital. They are far less effective at overcoming delays in permitting, siting, transmission development, and interconnection. Those challenges require earlier planning, stronger institutional coordination, and clearer accountability across the regional compact.

Governance has an important role to play in meeting that challenge, but it should not become a catch-all explanation for every challenge confronting the region. Good governance should produce institutions that anticipate change rather than react to it. It should encourage long-term planning instead of crisis management, establish clear priorities, and provide decision-makers with the confidence to act before reliability is threatened. It cannot, however, eliminate the physical, economic, and jurisdictional realities that ultimately determine whether sufficient infrastructure can be developed to meet future demand.

Many of the decisions that shape future reliability occur outside PJM. Utilities undertake new obligations to serve load. States establish resource policies and promote economic development. Local governments determine whether transmission and generation infrastructure can be sited and permitted. Private developers decide when projects move forward. PJM must then forecast demand, plan transmission, administer markets, and maintain reliability after many of those decisions have already been made. Governance can improve how PJM responds to those realities. It cannot substitute for earlier planning, stronger institutional coordination, or clearer accountability for the decisions that create future reliability obligations. Viewed through that lens, the challenge confronting PJM is not simply that decisions take too long. It is that the pace of change affecting the electric system has begun to outstrip the institutional mechanisms through which those changes are identified, evaluated, and acted upon. The answer is therefore not to abandon stakeholder governance, nor to centralize every important

PDF page 8

decision within the PJM Board. It is to ensure that governance is used for the purpose for which it is best suited: establishing strategic direction, identifying emerging risks early, aligning the institutions responsible for reliability, and enabling timely action before reliability challenges become crises.

That naturally leads to the next question: if governance is intended to provide strategic leadership rather than solve every operational challenge, how should responsibility be shared between the Board and the stakeholder process?

Leadership, Consensus, and the Role of the Board
Much of the discussion surrounding PJM governance has focused on two related questions: Has the stakeholder process become too slow? And should the Board exercise greater authority? Both are appropriate questions, but I do not believe they fully capture the issue confronting PJM today. The more fundamental question is whether PJM is using the right governance process for the type of decision it is trying to make.

One of PJM's greatest institutional strengths has always been its commitment to an open, transparent, consensus-based stakeholder process. Few organizations are able to bring together the breadth of operational, engineering, market, regulatory, and commercial expertise that exists within PJM's membership. That collaborative process has consistently produced better tariffs, stronger implementation, and more durable reforms than would likely emerge from a purely top- down regulatory approach. It should remain one of PJM's defining characteristics. At the same time, not every issue benefits from the same governance model. Technical questions involving Manuals, market mechanics, operational procedures, and implementation details benefit enormously from extensive stakeholder engagement because the objective is to improve the quality of implementation. Strategic questions are different. Decisions involving resource adequacy, transmission planning, market design, and emerging reliability risks require balancing competing public interests, establishing institutional priorities, and determining how much risk the region is willing to accept. Those are not simply technical questions, nor are they questions that can always be resolved through negotiation among stakeholders whose commercial interests are directly affected by the outcome.

That distinction explains why consensus and leadership serve different, but complementary, purposes. Consensus is exceptionally effective at improving solutions. Leadership defines the problem to be solved, establishes the objectives that must be achieved, and determines when action is necessary.

It should therefore not be surprising that consensus becomes increasingly difficult when stakeholders are asked to redesign the very markets in which they participate. That is not necessarily a failure of the stakeholder process. Rather, it reflects the reality that stakeholders

PDF page 9

with fundamentally different interests will not always agree on questions involving the allocation of costs, risks, and opportunities. Those are precisely the circumstances in which Board leadership becomes most important.

Recognizing those distinct roles, I believe the Board should lead on direction while stakeholders should lead on design. The Board should define the problem requiring resolution, establish the objectives any solution must satisfy, set an appropriate timeframe for action, and identify where stakeholder expertise is most valuable. Stakeholders should then do what they do best: improve proposals, evaluate alternatives, identify operational concerns, and build consensus wherever consensus can reasonably be achieved.

This distinction also informs the current discussion regarding Section 205 filing rights. Considerable attention has been given to whether PJM should possess broader Section 205 authority, whether the Board should have additional filing rights over matters currently governed by the Operating Agreement, or whether states should possess greater authority to initiate Commission action. These are thoughtful questions, and there may well be merit in considering targeted changes over time. I am not persuaded, however, that they address PJM’s principal challenge.

The region's most pressing challenge today is resource adequacy. Yet the Board already possesses broad Section 205 authority over the capacity market and many of the reforms necessary to address that challenge. Where the Board concludes that changes are needed in areas where PJM does not possess unilateral Section 205 filing authority, it retains the ability to seek relief from this Commission under Section 206 when it believes existing tariff provisions have become unjust, unreasonable, or unduly discriminatory. The limiting factor has therefore not been the absence of legal authority. It has been the willingness to exercise the authority that already exists.

Over time, stakeholder consensus has increasingly come to be viewed as a prerequisite for action rather than an important input into institutional judgment. Consensus should remain the preferred outcome wherever it can reasonably be achieved because reforms developed collaboratively are often stronger, more durable, and easier to implement. But consensus is a means of improving decisions; it should not become a prerequisite for exercising judgment.

An independent Board is valuable only if its independence produces leadership. The purpose of an independent Board is not to insulate its members from disagreement or controversy. It is to enable them to exercise their expert judgment when consensus cannot be achieved and difficult decisions nevertheless must be made.

Once the Board concludes that action is necessary to preserve reliability or maintain just and reasonable rates, it should be prepared to exercise the authorities already available to it. Consensus should remain the preferred outcome, but it should not become a justification for inaction. When meaningful stakeholder engagement has occurred and the Board concludes that

PDF page 10

change remains necessary, exercising its existing authority—including seeking Commission action under Section 206 where appropriate—is not a failure of the stakeholder process. It is the governance model functioning as intended.

Recent Board elections suggest that stakeholders increasingly express dissatisfaction with substantive policy outcomes through the election process. Accountability to stakeholders remains an important feature of PJM's governance, but accountability should not come at the expense of independence. PJM should therefore consider whether longer Board terms, coupled with the expectation that Board members generally serve a single term, would better balance accountability with the independence necessary to exercise expert judgment.

Twenty years ago, waiting another six months for consensus often carried relatively modest consequences. Today's system is fundamentally different. Infrastructure takes longer to develop, electricity demand is growing more rapidly, reserve margins are tighter, and the margin for error has narrowed considerably. As the cost of institutional delay continues to rise, the value of an independent Board lies not simply in its independence, but in its willingness to exercise independent judgment. That is the form of leadership the next era of regional coordination will require.

The States as Partners in Reliability
The Commission has appropriately asked whether states should play a larger role in PJM's governance and whether additional procedural rights or filing authorities are necessary to ensure that regional markets remain responsive to state priorities. In my view, the more important question is not whether states should have greater influence within PJM's governance structure. It is whether the institutions responsible for solving the region's most pressing reliability challenges are working together early enough, and with sufficient shared accountability, to develop durable solutions.

States are fundamentally different from other PJM stakeholders. They are sovereign governments exercising authorities that PJM neither has nor should have. States determine where generation and transmission facilities may be built. They regulate the utilities responsible for serving retail customers. They oversee resource planning in many jurisdictions and increasingly influence where new economic development—including large industrial facilities and data centers— occurs. Those decisions directly shape both electricity demand and the infrastructure available to meet it.

PJM's responsibility is equally unique. It must forecast those developments, determine what infrastructure and resources will be required, operate the regional transmission system reliably, and administer wholesale markets that support efficient investment.

PDF page 11

This division of responsibilities is not new. It is the foundation of the cooperative federalism upon which organized wholesale markets have always depended.

What has changed is the environment in which those responsibilities are exercised. For much of PJM's history, the region benefited from relatively modest load growth, stronger reserve margins, and sufficient flexibility within the system that imperfect coordination among institutions could often be absorbed without immediate reliability consequences. Today, we are operating under very different conditions. Electricity demand is increasing at rates not experienced in decades. Infrastructure takes significantly longer to permit and construct. Reserve margins are tighter, and the consequences of inaccurate planning or delayed investment are felt much more quickly.

As the margin for error narrows, coordination among institutions becomes substantially more important.

Resource adequacy provides perhaps the clearest example. Reliable service depends on competitive markets producing efficient investment signals. It depends on timely development of generation and transmission infrastructure. It depends on utility planning, state permitting and siting decisions, and economic development policies that recognize the infrastructure necessary to support new load. No single institution controls all of these decisions, yet each institution influences the outcome.

This also requires recognizing the relationship between affordability and investment. States quite appropriately seek to protect consumers from unnecessary cost increases. PJM appropriately relies on competitive markets to signal when additional resources are needed. Both objectives are legitimate. But they cannot be pursued in isolation. Markets cannot be expected to attract the investment necessary to address resource shortages if scarcity prices are consistently moderated without corresponding actions to accelerate the infrastructure needed to relieve those shortages. Likewise, stronger market signals alone cannot solve reliability challenges if new infrastructure cannot be sited, permitted, or constructed in a timely manner. The answer is not to choose between affordability and investment, or between state authority and regional markets. The answer is to better integrate those decisions before they become crises. That is why I believe the relationship between PJM and the states should evolve beyond consultation toward shared accountability for those initiatives that inherently require both regional and state action.

Today, the states already engage regularly with PJM through the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (OPSI). Those discussions are valuable and should continue. The issue is not that the states lack access or that PJM is unwilling to listen. Rather, the governance structure does not adequately recognize that success on many of today's most important issues ultimately depends on actions

PDF page 12

that only the states can take. Influence alone is not enough when implementation requires shared ownership of the solution.

Accordingly, I believe major initiatives involving long-term transmission planning, resource adequacy, and other issues that directly implicate state authorities should be developed by PJM in collaboration with a standing committee of state representatives before entering the stakeholder process. I do not believe the Commission or PJM should prescribe who represents each state. That decision should remain with the states themselves. Depending on the issue, a public utility commission, governor's office, energy office, or another designated entity may be the most appropriate representative.

If states are expected to assume a more strategic role, they must also have the institutional capacity to fulfill it. These issues are extraordinarily technical, requiring expertise in engineering, economics, market design, and transmission planning. Other regions have recognized this need by providing states with the budget for independent technical support.1 PJM should consider similar approaches so that states have the professional resources necessary to participate as effective partners in developing long-term regional strategies.

None of this should diminish PJM's responsibility or the authority of its Board. PJM must remain wholly responsible for determining what reliability requires and for making the regional decisions necessary to preserve the integrity of the interconnected system. Shared accountability does not mean shared veto authority. It means recognizing that regional strategies are more likely to succeed when the institutions responsible for implementing them help develop them from the outset.

Viewed through that lens, the question is not whether states should have a larger seat at the table. It is whether the institutions whose decisions collectively determine reliability are organized in a way that allows them to solve increasingly complex problems together. As the electric system continues to evolve and the margin for error continues to narrow, I believe the answer must increasingly be yes. That partnership should not end with consultation. Its purpose is to restore accountability for resource adequacy by aligning the institutions that create new reliability obligations with those responsible for ensuring they can be met.

Restoring Accountability for Resource Adequacy
The discussion above ultimately points to what I believe is the central institutional question confronting PJM. If resource adequacy is increasingly shaped by decisions made across multiple institutions, then the question is no longer simply how PJM procures capacity. The question is

The Reliability Pricing Model provides a useful illustration of why the region's resource adequacy framework must evolve. RPM was developed during a period of relatively stable demand growth, shorter infrastructure development timelines, and a market in which the Base Residual Auction was intended to function largely as a residual procurement mechanism. Bilateral contracting, self-supply, and other forward procurement activities were expected to satisfy much of the region's resource adequacy needs, with the auction procuring only the remaining capacity necessary to maintain reliability.

That is no longer how the market functions.
Today, the overwhelming majority of capacity clears through the centralized auction. More importantly, the auction has increasingly become the institution expected to reconcile decisions made by others. New wholesale load is added through utility service obligations and competitive retail arrangements without any corresponding requirement that the entity undertaking that obligation demonstrate how it intends to secure the accredited resources necessary to serve that load. States likewise make decisions regarding economic development, environmental policy, and the future composition of the generation fleet pursuant to their own statutory responsibilities. Those decisions are entirely appropriate exercises of state authority. But they also have direct implications for regional resource adequacy. If a state encourages significant new electricity demand or adopts policies that accelerate the retirement of existing resources, there should also be a credible strategy for ensuring that sufficient replacement resources and supporting infrastructure will be available when they are needed.

Even if scarcity prices were allowed to fully reflect market conditions, price signals alone cannot solve what has become fundamentally a planning and accountability challenge. Scarcity pricing may indicate that additional resources are needed, but it cannot shorten permitting timelines, accelerate transmission construction, resolve interconnection delays, or eliminate the uncertainty associated with developing major infrastructure. Generation, transmission, and natural gas infrastructure frequently require five to ten years—or longer—to finance, permit, interconnect, and construct. By contrast, the Base Residual Auction provides a single year of revenue guaranteed only a few years before delivery. That mismatch between investment timelines and market timelines has become increasingly significant.

The issue, therefore, is not whether competitive markets have failed. It is that we have increasingly asked the centralized capacity market to solve problems it was never designed to solve.

At its core, this reflects a broader shift in the nature of the resource adequacy challenge. When RPM was developed, the principal question was whether markets would produce sufficient investment in new generation. Today, the greater challenge is whether the infrastructure

PDF page 14

necessary to support that investment can be planned, permitted, interconnected, and constructed before reliability needs arise. Competitive markets remain highly effective at allocating capital among competing resources. They are far less capable of coordinating the planning, permitting, siting, and long-term infrastructure development that increasingly determine whether those resources can reach commercial operation when they are needed. Resource adequacy has therefore become as much an infrastructure and institutional coordination challenge as it is a market challenge.

That shift has also placed increasing pressure on PJM's governance. As long as the region continues to rely on the centralized capacity market to reconcile structural issues that originate elsewhere, participants will continue to debate market rules in an effort to solve problems whose root causes lie outside the market itself. The stakeholder process has increasingly become the forum for debating broader questions of resource adequacy, economic development, infrastructure planning, transmission development, and state policy. That is not because those issues are fundamentally questions of market design, nor because stakeholder negotiations are the appropriate forum for resolving them. Rather, it is because no institution has been clearly accountable for addressing those issues before they reached PJM.

By restoring clearer accountability for resource adequacy, much of that structural tension can be removed from the stakeholder process. PJM's markets can then focus on what competitive markets do exceptionally well: discovering efficient prices, facilitating competition, and allocating investment among competing resources. Likewise, stakeholders can return to what the process has always done best: improving implementation, refining market design, and applying their collective technical expertise to produce better solutions, rather than attempting to resolve broader institutional questions that markets alone cannot answer.

This is the central institutional reform PJM now requires. The objective is not to replace competitive markets with centralized planning. It is to reconnect the obligation to serve load with the obligation to ensure that adequate resources exist to serve that load. Governance reforms can improve how decisions are made, but reconnecting responsibility for creating reliability obligations with responsibility for meeting them addresses why so many governance debates have become increasingly difficult in the first place. For more than twenty years, resource adequacy has largely been treated as something PJM procures on behalf of the region. I believe the next evolution of organized markets should instead treat resource adequacy as a shared institutional responsibility, with clearer accountability placed on the entities creating new reliability obligations.

The guiding principle is straightforward: every decision that predictably creates a future reliability obligation should be accompanied by a credible plan for meeting that obligation. That principle begins with wholesale load-serving entities because they undertake the obligation to serve load. Before undertaking the obligation to serve load, an LSE should demonstrate a credible path for satisfying the resource adequacy requirements associated with that service.

PDF page 15

Importantly, this is not simply a new obligation on LSEs. It also creates a stronger connection between wholesale resource adequacy and the state regulatory decisions that increasingly shape the future resource mix.

This approach also clarifies the respective responsibilities of PJM, the states, and load-serving entities. PJM should remain responsible for determining the region's resource adequacy requirements. Given the size, complexity, and interconnected nature of the PJM system, no other institution is capable of performing the regional analysis necessary to determine how much accredited capacity is required, where it is needed, and when it must be available. States should continue to determine how the utilities and competitive suppliers they regulate satisfy those obligations, whether through long-term contracts, utility-owned resources, bilateral purchases, self-supply, or other market-based approaches. The obligation of the load-serving entity is to demonstrate that sufficient accredited resources will be available to serve the load it has undertaken to supply.

PJM's role should not end with establishing the reliability requirement. It should also verify that entities undertaking wholesale load obligations have demonstrated a credible path toward satisfying those obligations over an appropriate planning horizon. As the delivery year approaches, those demonstrations should become increasingly rigorous. By the delivery year, the obligation should be clear: an LSE should either demonstrate that it has secured sufficient accredited resources to serve its load or bear consequences. Those consequences should include meaningful financial penalties that compensate other market participants for the reliability and scarcity costs imposed by the shortfall, together with operational consequences designed to protect system reliability and reinforce accountability.

This framework also reinforces the partnership between PJM and the states discussed above. It does not assign new legal responsibilities to the states or diminish their authority over resource policy, utility regulation, or economic development. Today, a state may appropriately encourage significant economic development, approve new large electricity customers, or adopt policies that accelerate the retirement of existing generation. Those decisions remain matters of state authority. But if the utilities or competitive suppliers serving that load are also responsible for demonstrating compliance with PJM's resource adequacy requirements, those same decisions will naturally be accompanied by planning for the resources needed to support them. Rather than asking PJM's centralized capacity market to reconcile those decisions after they have been made, responsibility begins moving upstream to the institutions creating the new reliability obligation. Importantly, this framework works equally well in both traditionally regulated and competitive retail markets. The obligation attaches to the entity undertaking wholesale load service, not to a particular retail market structure. States remain free to determine how that obligation is satisfied, whether through vertically integrated utilities, competitive suppliers, bilateral contracting, self- supply, or other market-based approaches. The objective is not to prescribe procurement

decisions or diminish competition. It is to align authority, responsibility, and accountability while preserving competitive markets.

Viewed from that perspective, this evolution strengthens rather than diminishes PJM's role. PJM should continue doing what only a regional transmission organization can do: establish regional and locational resource adequacy requirements, administer transparent competitive markets, plan the transmission system, operate the bulk electric system, evaluate emerging reliability risks, and enforce compliance with regional reliability requirements. Reliability should no longer be treated as something PJM delivers on behalf of everyone else. It should become a shared obligation of the institutions whose decisions determine the future needs of the system.

Five Principles for Strengthening the Regional Compact
The recommendations in this statement can be distilled into five practical principles. None requires abandoning competitive markets or fundamentally restructuring PJM. Together, however, they would better align governance with the realities of today's electric system while preserving the regional compact that has served consumers well for more than two decades.

1. Empower the Board to Lead
The Board should clearly define strategic priorities, establish the objectives stakeholder processes are intended to achieve, set reasonable timelines for action, and be prepared to exercise its independent judgment when consensus cannot be reached. Consensus should remain the preferred outcome, but it should not become a prerequisite for leadership. That independence should also be reflected in Board structure. PJM should consider longer Board terms, coupled with an expectation that members generally serve a single term, to ensure that Board members can exercise independent judgment without concern that difficult but necessary decisions may affect their continued service.

2. Institutionalize State Partnership
The most significant planning initiatives, particularly those involving transmission planning and resource adequacy should be developed by PJM in collaboration with a standing committee of state representatives before entering the stakeholder process. States should determine how they are represented, and they should have access to the technical resources necessary to participate as effective partners.

3. Align Responsibility for Resource Adequacy
The obligation to serve wholesale load should be accompanied by a demonstrated plan for meeting the resource adequacy obligations that accompany that load with increasingly rigorous demonstrations as the delivery year approaches. This approach does not require re-regulation or

diminish competitive markets. Rather, it better aligns responsibility for creating new reliability obligations with responsibility for ensuring those obligations can be met.

4. Plan Earlier Because Infrastructure Takes Longer
The defining challenge of the next decade is no longer attracting capital. It is ensuring that generation, transmission, and other critical infrastructure can be planned, permitted, interconnected, and constructed before reliability needs arise. Every institution participating in the regional compact should evaluate its decisions through that lens. Better planning cannot eliminate infrastructure challenges, but it can ensure the region begins solving them before reliability is threatened.

5. Let Markets and Stakeholders Do What They Do Best
Competitive markets should be responsible for allocating investment among competing resources, not for correcting planning failures or reconciling decisions made outside the market. Likewise, the stakeholder process should focus on improving implementation and market design, not on resolving structural questions of resource policy, infrastructure development, and economic growth that require broader institutional leadership. By restoring accountability for resource adequacy and engaging the right institutions earlier, PJM can free both the market and the stakeholder process to perform the functions they were designed to perform.

Conclusion
The questions before the Commission extend well beyond the governance of a single regional transmission organization. They concern the future of regional coordination itself. For more than two decades, PJM has demonstrated that states can retain authority over their own energy policies while participating in regional institutions that improve reliability, lower costs, and support efficient wholesale markets. That achievement should not be taken for granted. It remains one of the most important institutional accomplishments in modern electricity policy. The pressures confronting PJM today are real, but they are not evidence that the regional model has failed. They reflect an electric system that has changed more rapidly than the institutions designed to support it. The answer, therefore, is not to weaken the regional compact. It is to strengthen it.

That means a Board with the confidence to provide strategic leadership while remaining informed by stakeholder expertise. It means a more meaningful partnership between PJM and the states. It means planning earlier because infrastructure now determines the pace at which reliability can be maintained. And it means reconnecting the obligation to serve load with accountability for ensuring that sufficient accredited resources exist to serve it.

If we can restore that alignment, many of the governance debates now consuming PJM will become more manageable because the market, the stakeholder process, the Board, and the states will each be focused on the responsibilities they are uniquely positioned to perform. Ultimately, preserving the regional compact is not about changing who has the loudest voice in the stakeholder process. It is about ensuring that every institution entrusted with responsibility for the electric system has the authority, accountability, and confidence to fulfill the role only it can perform.

If we accomplish that, we will do more than improve PJM's governance. We will preserve one of the nation's most successful regional institutions while ensuring it is prepared for the challenges of the next generation of the electric system.
