(“AMP”), focuses on the subject matter of that panel discussion, as described in the Commission’s notice.1 As Vice President of Transmission & Regulatory Affairs, I provide oversight and take responsibility for AMP’s advocacy in the Midcontinent Independent System Operator, Inc. (“PJM”) RTO stakeholder processes, along with AMP’s participation in the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (“NERC”) standards development process. These comments share the perspective of AMP as a non-profit public power wholesale supplier of electricity for 135 public power communities located across nine states, including seven PJM states. AMP’s member systems serve approximately

650,000 meters, and while AMP represents many of the larger public power systems in PJM, the average AMP member community is less than 5,000 meters and rural. Our member officials are keenly aware of the impact of PJM policies and governance on their local operations and costs for their customers. But AMP’s advocacy is not biased towards pricing outcomes. Said differently, AMP does not advocate for rules that are motivated to intentionally raise prices or lower prices. Instead, AMP advocates for sustainable, commonsense rules that will protect end-use customers from degraded reliability, while ensuring affordability.

PJM’s existing governance framework generally works well and does not require major structural reform. The current sector-weighted voting process appropriately balances consensus-building with timely decision-making and ensures that significant proposals receive broad stakeholder support before advancing.

AMP’s primary recommendation is to improve coordination and consistency between voting at senior committees and voting at lower-level committees, where voting results often determine which proposals move forward. AMP believes coordination between voting results at lower-level committees and votes taken by senior committees would strengthen stakeholder confidence, improve consensus-building, and make decision-making more efficient.

Stakeholder representation and committee structure are not the source of delays. States and other interested parties appropriately have multiple opportunities to participate in PJM processes, and the speed of decision-making is largely determined by PJM’s prioritization of issues and meeting schedules. Accordingly, better issue prioritization is needed, rather than changes to stakeholder representation or governance.

PJM’s most significant challenge remains resource adequacy and reliability, but the existing governance framework, with the adjustments proposed below, is sufficient to address those concerns and there is no basis for weakening stakeholder processes or reducing stakeholder input. Expanded use of expedited stakeholder procedures may result in compressed timelines that hinder stakeholder collaboration, reduce opportunities to build consensus, and create greater regulatory uncertainty. Better outcomes result when stakeholders have adequate time to develop and refine proposals. Finally, PJM should enhance transparency at the Board level. Adopting practices similar to those used by other regional transmission organizations, such as providing greater public access to Board meetings, agendas, and materials, would improve accountability and stakeholder confidence. Overall, AMP supports targeted transparency improvements while preserving the consensus-based processes that have historically promoted reliable and affordable electric service.

II. COMMENTS
Question 1: Sector-weighted Voting

Do sector-weighted voting and existing voting thresholds strike the right balance between achieving consensus and taking timely action?

Sector weighted voting (SWV) is used for votes of the Members Committee (MC) and the Markets and Reliability Committee (MRC) (i.e., the Senior Standing Committees). SWV and the related voting thresholds for issues to be deemed approved (or endorsed) achieves a reasonable balance between developing consensus and timely action. The threshold is two-thirds support from the five sectors, in which each sector provides 1.000 votes towards the total. Thus the threshold for an issue to be deemed approved or passed via SWV is 3.334 (rounding up).

PJM facilitates nearly all stakeholder meetings except for the MC and the Liaison Committee (LC). There is no voting at the LC, thus there is only one instance in which a PJM Member facilitates a committee in which voting occurs (the MC). In terms of timely action, the committee or task force facilitator or a stakeholder can “call the question” on an agenda item to ensure timely action.

Achieving consensus does not mean unanimous support. In some instances,
consensus is a simple majority threshold and in others it is a super-majority. While it may seem difficult to achieve a super-majority threshold, particularly on contentious issues, when it is attained, it signals to FERC that the proposal before it is supported by a broad range of PJM Members.

Question 2. Transparency Reforms

What transparency reforms would improve the quality of PJM’s stakeholder
process and increase stakeholder confidence in its decision-making?

Transparency reforms are desperately needed, particularly for matters of
significance that come before the PJM Board following stakeholder consideration and that result in FERC filings. There is substantial opaqueness in the PJM Board’s deliberations on such matters. One aspect that PJM should consider adopting from SPP is that “[a]ll SPP Board meetings that are not in executive session are open to the public. All meeting materials, agendas, and minutes are publicly posted on the SPP website.”2 Transparency is key, recognizing that certain issues necessarily lend themselves to being discussed in executive session.

MISO’s similarly open approach is also instructive. The MISO Board holds an open meeting each quarter. The committees of the Board hold open meetings at least each quarter. Stakeholders and the public are allowed to listen to these meetings. The Board and its committees cover general materials in open session, but do have executive sessions for material related to confidential business (personnel matters, legal matters, contract issues, etc.). At the end of their meetings the MISO Board and its committees allow an opportunity for public comment. During MISO’s Board week, when the Board holds an open meeting, the Board joins the MISO Advisory Committee (AC)—the stakeholder committee with representatives from all of the stakeholder sectors—to hold a discussion on a topic decided prior to the meeting. The discussion for June 2026 was “Maintaining Resource Adequacy in a Time of Transition.” The MISO Board and the AC receive reports from the chairs of various MISO stakeholder groups.  After each report on the topics of interest being discussed at the stakeholder committees, the MISO Board and the AC have an opportunity to ask the chair of each group questions about the work of the committee. Additional opportunities for the PJM Board to interact with stakeholders and for stakeholders to hear PJM Board deliberations and understand the basis for decision- making, more like other RTOs, could increase stakeholder confidence in, or at least understanding of, PJM Board decisions.

Question 4. Governing Documents

Do existing governing document provisions (e.g., Operating Agreement, Manuals) impede stakeholder decision-making and what changes could be made to

address them?

Although the current PJM governance framework remains generally sound, there are targeted reforms that could be made to improve the efficiency of the stakeholder process. Existing provisions of Manual 34 (PJM Stakeholder Process) impede stakeholder decision-making and a key example is the impact of voting that occurs at the “lower level” committees in determining which proposals may be voted on in the senior committees. The lower-level committees should play a critical role in screening and developing the proposals that are ultimately considered by the senior committees. For proposals to be viable, their ultimate approval at the Members Committee should be reasonably foreseeable by the lower-level committees. In order to efficiently build proposals that may achieve consensus at the critical Members Committee, it is imperative to ensure that the voting structure at the lower level Standing Committees adequately captures the views of stakeholders represented in MC voting. Under the existing rules, it does not.

The current process impedes stakeholder decision-making because at the lower- level committees, all PJM Members receive one vote, including Affiliates, but at the senior committees, each corporate family receives only one vote.  As a result, Members with more Affiliates control the flow of proposals from the lower-level committees to the senior committees, meaning that proposals that are not viable at the senior-level committees

Thus the disparity in voting participants at the lower level compared to the senior level committees may and often does greatly impact the voting outcome at the MRC and MC. This is because a Member with multiple Affiliates has an outsized opportunity to influence the order of voting, and therefore the proposals that are voted on, at the senior level. Manual 34 explains that voting at the Standing Committees includes both Members and Affiliates, and that if a simple majority prefers a proposal over the status quo the proposal with the greatest level of support will become the Main Motion for consideration at the MRC, while other proposals will be Alternate Motions. If the Main Motion passes, no vote is taken on Alternate Motions.

The status quo voting paradigm that allows Affiliates to vote at Standing Committees favors Members with registered Affiliates. Question 5. Stakeholder Process Reforms

What reforms to the stakeholder process could ensure PJM can meet the region’s challenges?

The single biggest challenge PJM continually faces is how to conduct a capacity auction to ensure sufficient quantities of capacity are available to meet anticipated demand plus adequate reserves to maintain a 1-in-10 level of reliability, consistent with the FERC-approved Reliability Pricing Model (RPM) capacity construct rules. RPM’s structure is contained within PJM’s Open Access Transmission Tariff (OATT or Tariff). PJM has Federal Power Act section 205 filing rights over the OATT.10 No changes are needed to the stakeholder process to ensure PJM can meet the region’s resource adequacy challenges, given the section 205 filing rights that PJM currently enjoys over the terms and conditions of RPM. Rather than reforming the stakeholder process, significant reforms to how PJM prioritizes and schedules meetings to address the challenges should be made.  PJM already has the ability to do so but has not.

PJM’s Manual 34 offers a useful high-level description of the interplay between the PJM stakeholder process and filing rights under Federal Power Act sections 205 and 206, as follows:

Sections 205 and 206 of the Federal Power Act set forth the requirements that must be met to obtain FERC approval of a proposed revision to a governing document and are germane to the governing documents of PJM (the Operating Agreement, the Open Access Transmission Tariff (Tariff) and the Reliability Assurance Agreements (RAA)), and to the operation of the PJM stakeholder process. Sections 205 and 206 establish the standards for demonstrating why a proposed revision to a governing document should be approved by the FERC. Section 205 requires that the proposer of a revision demonstrate the proposed revision is “just and reasonable.” Section 206 requires a potentially higher hurdle in that the proposer of a revision to the governing documents must demonstrate that the then current provisions are “unjust and unreasonable,” and that the proposed revisions are “just and reasonable.” Within the PJM stakeholder process it is recognized that the Members Committee maintains Section 205 authority over the Operating Agreement, and that the Board of Managers maintains Section 205 authority over the Open Access Transmission Tariff (Tariff) (with the exception of certain Tariff provisions that are under the exclusive control of the Transmission Owners) and the Reliability Assurance Agreements. Any party not possessing 205 authority over one of the governing documents may propose a revision to the document to the FERC under Section 206. It is also recognized that the Members provide input into the Regional Transmission Expansion Plan (“Plan”), and that the Plan is approved by the Board.11

Question 6. Near-term Reforms

What near-term reforms to stakeholder representation could PJM and its Members take to improve speed in decision-making while preserving stakeholder input?

PJM needs to focus on issues related to reliability and resource adequacy above other issues. Stakeholders work on issues that require rapid development based on PJM’s meeting schedules. PJM sets the cadence for the stakeholder process through the calendar. PJM sets the priority of issues when it develops the calendar. If there is a concern about the speed of decision-making, that is not cause for reforming stakeholder representation. Instead, it only requires PJM to prioritize and schedule stakeholder meetings so key discussions can occur more quickly.

Question 8. Expedited Action

Should PJM increase the use of fast-path or time-bound review procedures to expedite committee action on high-importance topics?

Simply scheduling additional meetings closer together to address mission-critical topics may be more effective than invoking truncated procedures. As we saw in the just completed CIFP-RBP/Connect & Manage process, when PJM attempts to use fast-path or time-bound review procedures to expedite action, PJM may trip itself up, resulting in wholesale changes to its own proposal. Similarly, stakeholders do not have sufficient time to work amongst themselves to consolidate their approaches, resulting in an unwieldy number of alternative proposals that often fail to consolidate into a clear consensus position.
