PROTEST AND COMMENTS OF EASTMAN CHEMICAL COMPANY 
 
 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal 
Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC or Commission), 18 C.F.R. § 385.211 (2025), 
Eastman Chemical Company (Eastman) submits this protest and comments on the 60-
day compliance filing that PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) submitted to FERC on 
February 23, 2026 (Compliance Filing). For the reasons set forth herein, the 
Commission should deny portions of the Compliance Filing because they do not 
comport with the Commission’s specific directives in the Order1 and would result in 
economic harm to Eastman. 
I. 
BACKGROUND 
A. 
Eastman’s Kingsport Facility 
 
Eastman is an industrial products manufacturer located in Kingsport, Tennessee, 
with significant on-site generation at its facilities in Kingsport (Kingsport facilities). 
The on-site generation facilities carry a name-plate capacity of 194 MW, with

While the Kingsport facility requires a significant amount of energy to fulfill its

Eastman has been operating under its current service agreement with Kingsport 
Power since July 1, 1998. This service agreement was subject to a one-year term and 
has rolled over year to year under the terms of the agreement. As part of this service 
agreement, Kingsport Power has agreed to provide Eastman back-up power and surplus 
power. Eastman pays charges for both of these services. For almost 50 years, Eastman 
has relied on its interconnection with Kingsport Power, and for at least the past 28 years 
on this service agreement and the commensurate netting provisions to ensure that its 
energy costs remain economically reasonable at the Kingsport facility. 
 
Additionally, due to the configuration of its tightly coupled generator-steam 
combined heat and power network (comprised of 17 boilers—6 coal and 11 natural 
gas—and 19 turbine generators), Eastman is never able to pull the full load of power

B. 
Commission Order 
 
In the Order, the Commission required PJM to implement considerable changes 
to the “behind the meter generation” (BTMG) rules to eliminate the netting approach 
that allows BTMG network customers to pay for the services they use from PJM. As 
relevant to this protest, FERC directed PJM “to propose a new MW threshold for the 
amount of load at a particular electrical location that Network Customers may net by 
using BTMG.”4 In addition, FERC directed PJM to revise its Tariff to “grandfather 
entities with existing contracts for the specific purpose of effectuating a BTMG 
arrangement for the current term remaining under the existing contract.”5 FERC also 
directed PJM to allow for a three-year transition period for those retail BTMG facilities 
to shift to the new services that FERC directed PJM to create.

II. 
PROTEST 
 
Eastman protests the Compliance Filing because the proposed threshold ignores 
the operational realities of industrial plant BTMG facilities; the proposed threshold is 
too low; and because the proposed threshold is “all or nothing.” In addition, Eastman 
comments on the proposed grandfathering and seeks clarification that grandfathering 
should apply to the facilities that are operating under a currently effective BTMG

PDF page 5

5 
service agreement, rather than simply the existing service agreement (as renewed or 
extended). 
 
First, the proposed BTMG changes ignore the operational realities of a facility 
like Eastman’s Kingsport facility. The Commission acknowledged that not all loads 
using BTMG are large loads that create reliability and resource adequacy risks that the 
Commission described in the Order.6 The Kingsport facility is a BTMG load that does 
not create reliability and resources adequacy risks because the Kingsport facility uses 
the electric generation at its facilities not only to provide electric power to the facilities, 
but also to generate steam necessary for the industrial processes that take place within 
the facilities. If the Kingsport facility is unable to generate power, it is unable to 
generate steam, which reduces the industrial output at the facility along with reducing 
the need for additive power from the grid. Thus, the limited times when the Kingsport 
facility would have the greatest need for additional power (i.e., when its generators are 
running at full capacity) are the times when the grid is most likely able to provide 
additional power – which Eastman pays for through its service agreement with 
Kingsport power. In contrast, in periods of grid distress, the Kingsport facility is more 
likely to support the grid through using its own BTMG to power the Kingsport facility 
than it is to create operational constraints for the grid. Accordingly, PJM and FERC 
should recognize the importance that industrial BTMG facilities have in maintaining

the stability of the grid by maintaining BTMG netting for facilities like the Eastman 
Kingsport facility, not through punishing them to make uneconomic choices. 
 
Second, if FERC and PJM do not maintain netting for BTMG facilities like the 
Kingsport facility because of their operating realities, FERC must require PJM to 
implement a threshold of at least 200 MW on a permanent basis. The nameplate 
capacity of the generation at the Kingsport facility is 194 MW. However, the Kingsport 
facility cannot pull that amount through its interconnection with Kingsport Power, nor 
could it operate solely on electrical power delivered from the grid. Yet the Kingsport 
facility has been able to operate its BTMG facilities reliably and without creating 
reliability and resource adequacy risks on the grid. This is largely due to the incentives 
that BTMG netting provides to industrial facilities like the Kingsport facility to operate 
their generation in a manner that ensures that BTMG is available when the grid is 
constrained (e.g., maintenance is planned for non-critical periods). Imposing an 
arbitrarily low threshold eliminates the netting benefits for those BTMG facilities that 
exceed the threshold and eliminates the grid benefits that those BTMG facilities have 
provided. In addition, the proposed threshold would remove the economic benefits that 
Eastman has relied on to continue operating its BTMG at the Kingsport facilities – i.e., 
the arbitrarily low threshold renders Eastman’s BTMG uneconomic after almost 28 
years of operations. By implementing a 200 MW threshold, FERC and PJM will ensure 
that BTMG facilities, like the Kingsport facility, which has supported the grid for many 
decades, are able to operate economically.

Third, the Commission must reject PJM’s proposed “all or nothing” approach to 
the MW threshold. In the Order, FERC directed PJM to create a MW threshold for the 
amount of load that Network Customers may net by using BTMG.7 Said differently, 
FERC directed PJM to allow for netting up to a certain level, such that all BTMG 
facilities would continue to receive some benefits of BTMG. There is nothing just and 
reasonable regarding PJM’s proposal to allow a facility with nameplate generation 
capacity of 50 MW to net all of its BTMG, but a facility with nameplate generation 
capacity of 51 MW to net none of its BTMG. FERC should deny the proposed threshold 
limitations that PJM has proposed and require PJM to submit a revised compliance 
filing that allows for all BTMG to net up to the threshold level. 
 
Finally, Eastman requests the Commission to clarify that the PJM grandfathering 
provision would apply to the facilities rather than simply the service agreement. As 
crafted, the PJM proposal would unnecessarily impede the ability of the BTMG 
operator to amend or modify an existing agreement to better reflect the then-current 
operational realities of BTMG facilities. For example, facilities could be modified to 
become more efficient such that an existing agreement should be modified to reflect 
lower electrical energy needs. As drafted, the PJM proposal would no longer allow for 
those BTMG facilities to be grandfathered because the existing service agreement had 
been modified. In short, attaching grandfathering to the BTMG facilities (and capping 
their ability to net at the current nameplate capacity) would ensure that BTMG operators

can continue to operate their facilities in the most economically efficient manner 
possible without concern over losing grandfathering rights. 
 
For the foregoing reasons, the Commission should require PJM to continue to 
allow BTMG facilities like the Kingsport facility to net. Alternatively, the Commission 
should require PJM to increase the MW threshold to 200 MW on a permanent basis and 
allow for netting at any BTMG facility up to the MW threshold. Finally, the 
Commission should require PJM to attach grandfathering to the BTMG facility with a 
currently effective service agreement rather than the term of the existing service 
agreement as extended.
