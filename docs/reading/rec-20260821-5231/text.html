PJM’s “status quo is unacceptable.”2 Commissioner See framed the problem at the outset: “PJM’s

confidence[.]”3 Stakeholders echoed those observations throughout the technical conference and 
in pre-conference filings.

COMMENTS 
I. 
PJM Must Swiftly Implement Solutions to Help Address Its Most Significant 
Challenge: Getting Generation Online in Time to Meet New Load 
PJM’s most urgent challenge is getting needed generation online quickly enough to meet 
rapidly growing load. But PJM cannot solve that problem if integrated solutions are forced through 
a governance structure that divides responsibility across fragmented committees and distinct filing 
authorities. The same governance defects that prevent PJM from evaluating integrated proposals 
like AEP’s Accelerated Load, Infrastructure, Generation and Network Planning (“ALIGN”) 
concept also prevent PJM from implementing solutions to get generation online promptly. Indeed, 
the persistent governance problems in PJM directly contribute to its current resource adequacy 
challenges: while the region has long seen the current problems coming, potential solutions 
languished for years in deadlocked stakeholder processes. Governance reform thus is not separate 
from generator interconnection or resource adequacy concerns; it is the mechanism needed to 
move necessary reforms from concept to implemented solution. 
The relevant measure of reform should be whether it helps new resources reach commercial 
operation in time to serve customer demand—not whether PJM has merely improved on certain 
governance, queue administration, or study throughput metrics. PJM’s current committee structure 
can take an integrated generation solution and divide it into separate pieces—separating out 
generator interconnection, load interconnection, transmission planning, market design, 
deliverability, and cost allocation—before PJM, stakeholders, or the Board can evaluate whether 
the package solves the underlying reliability problem. That fragmentation does not align with the 
integrated nature of the challenge: generation cannot meet new load unless the project, the network 
upgrades, deliverability, and load-service arrangements are synchronized. A committee-by-

II. 
The Technical Conference Record Demonstrates the Need for Reform 
The record here demonstrates that stakeholders are broadly aligned in three key areas: First, 
governance reform is necessary to allow PJM to act more efficiently and effectively. Second, 
governance reform must navigate the genuine tension between Board independence and 
stakeholder participation. Third, any governance changes should be solutions-oriented, facilitating 
decisive action to address the region’s most significant challenges rather than focusing on process 
for its own sake. Although there are outer limits to what governance reform alone can accomplish 
to address PJM’s current ills, as Commissioner Chang put it, the simple question on the table is: 
“How can we help PJM make decisions more swiftly?”7

B. 
The Record Reflects a Need to Carefully Calibrate the Board’s and 
Stakeholders’ Roles 
The technical conference confirmed that governance reform must balance two key 
interests: Ensuring PJM is truly independent to achieve its important objectives as an RTO while 
providing a meaningful opportunity for stakeholders and states to participate in PJM decision-
making processes. Several parties—including PJM, OPSI and various other state representatives, 
the PSEG Companies, and Google—called for greater Board authority and independence. They 
offered proposals ranging from expanded or unconditioned Section 205 filing rights for PJM and

stakeholder stalemates that can block timely filings.19 Others, meanwhile, cautioned against 
reforms that could diminish or limit the stakeholder process. For instance, the PJM Power 
Providers Group, AMP, Constellation, LS Power, Vistra, the Independent Market Monitor, and 
Kent Chandler—R Street Institute fellow and former Kentucky Public Service Commission 
Chairman—generally favored more targeted reforms focused on execution, transparency, state 
engagement, and workflow prioritization rather than wholesale restructuring.20

a robust role are not mutually exclusive; they are mutually reinforcing. A well-functioning

A. 
PJM Must Reexamine Section 205 Filing Rights for Core Functional Matters 
PJM’s current division of Section 205 filing rights can impede timely action, particularly 
where an integrated reform requires changes to more than one governing document. In 
apportioning Section 205 rights, PJM and the Commission should distinguish between rights over 
corporate governance of the RTO and rights over core functional matters, such as energy markets, 
regional transmission planning, resource adequacy, and interconnection reform. The Members 
Committee’s Section 205 authority over core functional matters too often operates as a barrier to 
timely and technically sound action.28 Such authority should instead reside with the PJM Board. 
As PJM explained, it holds unconditioned Section 205 rights in some core areas, principally 
the capacity market, but its ability to file changes to the energy and ancillary services markets and 
to the Regional Transmission Expansion Plan process is “conditioned on Members Committee 
approval,” such that, absent such approval, “PJM may be required to proceed under the more 
restrictive requirements of section 206.”29 PJM candidly characterized this division as “in many 
cases[] an accident of history” and noted that its peer RTOs “do not have such conditioning of 
their 205 rights.”30 PJM therefore asked the Commission to consider whether PJM should have

C. 
PJM Must Limit Its Ex Parte Restrictions to Improve Board Transparency, 
Accessibility, and Stakeholder Engagement 
PJM’s broad-ranging challenges—including accelerating load growth, resource adequacy 
concerns, transmission expansion, interconnection reform, and rising expectations from states, 
customers, and federal policymakers—require more frequent and meaningful communication 
between the PJM Board and affected stakeholders. The current rules restricting informal 
communications, however, unnecessarily limit the Board’s ability to understand the real-world 
implications of its governance, market, planning, and reliability decisions. They significantly 
constrain informal engagement between stakeholders and individual Board members outside of 
formal stakeholder proceedings.43 Although safeguards against improper influence are important, 
PJM’s current restrictions are broader than necessary and prevent the Board from hearing directly 
from those most affected by issues of regional significance—including reliability, transmission 
planning, and interconnection reform.  
PJM’s approach is more restrictive than the governance practices of its peer RTOs, and no 
other RTO or ISO imposes such severe restrictions on Board-stakeholder communication.44

D. 
PJM Must Implement Additional Targeted Board Reforms to Improve 
Expertise, Engagement, and Accountability 
As detailed in AEP’s pre-conference statement, PJM should adopt several additional, 
targeted Board reforms drawn from the practices of other RTOs and ISOs.49 Without repeating its 
prior comments, AEP reiterates its recommendations of the following reforms to strengthen the 
Board’s ability to address reliability, affordability, resource adequacy, and infrastructure 
challenges swiftly, while preserving the Board’s independence: 
1. PJM should make its President and CEO a voting member of the Board. The 
President and CEO is responsible for implementing Board decisions and managing 
PJM’s operations and therefore should be fully involved in Board decision-making. 
The CEOs of MISO, SPP, and NYISO already serve as voting members of their 
respective boards, subject to appropriate recusal limits.50  
2. PJM Board meetings should be open by default. PJM’s Board meetings are not open 
to the public or stakeholders, leaving very little transparency into how some of the most 
consequential decisions are made. For example, following the Critical Issue Fast Path 
stakeholder process for the reliability backstop procurement, the PJM Board developed 
its own proposal and submitted it to FERC. Stakeholders do not know how the Board 
reached its final decision, what tradeoffs it made, and why certain features were 
selected. AEP strongly supports the Board’s decisive adoption of its own proposal 
following robust stakeholder input. However, future processes should be more 
transparent, which in turn will promote better future engagement and enhance the 
legitimacy of the PJM Board’s decisions. To promote transparency, the Board should 
provide public access to meetings not involving confidential topics (e.g., personnel, 
security).51 
3. PJM should adopt an in-region preference for Board candidates. Given the 
importance of regional knowledge to reliability, affordability, and economic-
development decisions, AEP recommends a preference for Board candidates who 
reside within the PJM footprint or have significant professional experience with the 
region’s electric system. Both ISO-NE and NYISO maintain such preferences.52

IV. 
PJM Must Implement Reforms that Allow Greater State Input 
AEP supports targeted reforms to PJM’s governance framework that improve the ability of 
responsible entities to bring concrete, actionable proposals to the Commission under Section 205. 
Any expansion of filing rights should be balanced and carefully defined—preserving the existing 
Section 205 rights of transmission owners while recognizing the central and evolving role of states 
in resource adequacy, affordability, economic development, and public policy matters. The 
Commission should encourage governance reforms that promote timely action, transparency, and 
accountability without undermining the legal responsibilities and investment obligations of 
transmission owners. 
A. 
General Principles: Filing Rights Must Align with Legal Responsibility and 
Reforms Must Enable Timely Action While Preserving FERC’s Role 
Section 205 filing rights should rest with, or be made available to, entities that have direct 
responsibility for serving customers, maintaining reliability, making long-term infrastructure 
investments, and implementing public policy requirements. PJM’s current governance 
framework—including the allocation of Section 205 rights—was developed in a different 
operating environment, one characterized by slower load growth, surplus generation, and 
incremental infrastructure needs.56 As that operating environment has changed, PJM’s governance 
must be updated to align filing authority with legal responsibility regarding today’s reliability, 
resource adequacy, transmission-expansion, and affordability challenges.

As states bear increasing responsibility for resource adequacy policy, economic 
development strategy, transmission siting, and customer affordability, they should have a defined

feedback or challenge the proposal before the Commission,” which “is ultimately responsible for

C.

filing framework that allows competing Section 205 proposals to be presented to the Commission 
in appropriate circumstances. Such a framework could apply when PJM, the transmission owners, 
or a supermajority of states advance competing proposals on issues implicating their respective 
responsibilities and stakeholder consensus cannot be reached. A jump-ball process follows an
