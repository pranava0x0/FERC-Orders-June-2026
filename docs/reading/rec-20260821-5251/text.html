POST-CONFERENCE COMMENTS OF ENGIE NORTH AMERICA, INC. 
 
(August 21, 2026) 
ENGIE North America, Inc.,1 (“ENGIE”) appreciates the opportunity to provide post-conference 
comments following the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or “Commission”) 
technical discussion of Governance and Stakeholder Reforms in the PJM Interconnection LLC (“PJM”) 
region on July 23, 2026. We commend the Commission for its leadership in facilitating the candid and 
enlightening discussion. ENGIE submitted pre-technical conference comments and has registered to 
participate in the upcoming Alternative Dispute Resolution process.  
ENGIE encourages reforms that strengthen confidence in PJM’s governance and stakeholder 
processes but that also preserve the critical role that PJM’s competitive wholesale markets play in 
maintaining reliability across the region..  
I. 
COMMENTS 
 
ENGIE’s pre-technical conference comments address several of the questions issued by the 
Commission in the Appendix to its Notice Requesting Post-Conference Comment (“Notice”) issued on 
July 30, 2026. One important matter is the critical need to modify the role of the PJM Board.  We 
continue to believe that the role of the PJM Board is not to over involve itself in the detailed matrix of

X 
Comments: 
Increased communication between Board members and market participants and other

informal opportunities to meet with the Board to discuss items of interest. For example,

and the New England Conference of Public Utilities Commissioners and are generally 
responsive to individual market participant outreach.

Comments: 
ENGIE’s pre-Technical Conference comments urge PJM to refocus its board’s role on 
appropriate board-level activities. In this context, the Board would not vote on market rule 
changes and therefore, Board reasoning and votes may not need to be made public.  
Importantly, the PJM Board should focus on the traditional and essential matters of 
governance: providing strategic oversight, ensuring accountability, managing risk, and 
promoting PJM’s long-term success. The Board should not be excessively involved in the 
tactical stakeholder process. It is an inefficient use of time and resources for the Board to 
participate directly in stakeholder decision-making exercises, such as evaluating a matrix of 
many complex and competing choices. The Board should instead be advised of and 
consulted on major reforms so that Board members are appropriately informed, remain 
accountable and, in turn, can hold the PJM organization responsible for implementation of 
market design and other changes.  
7) 
In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or 
available to the public? 
X 
Comments: 
Some level of openness of the Board meetings would provide stakeholders with a better 
understanding of how the Board functions and is likely to engender trust. As importantly as 
open board meetings are opportunities for stakeholders to interact with Board members and 
a generally openness of Board members to meet with stakeholders. Increased transparency 
should not interfere with the work of the Board to the extent that the work cannot be done 
efficiently.  
8) 
Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the 
responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest? 
 
 
Comments: 
Much of the language in the governing documents that is used to describe the duties and 
responsibilities of the PJM Board and the formation of market prices signify the 
organization’s intent to serve the public interest. Embracing the meaning of terms like 
competitive, non-discriminatory, transparent, efficient, just, and reasonable should drive 
market results that are sustainable for buyers and sellers.  Sustained market success does 
not mean preferrable results to either buyers or sellers but at least acceptable or tolerable to 
both. Importantly, PJM is already subject to a public interest framework through FERC 
oversight since it operates under FERC-approved tariffs and governing documents. FERC 
itself is charged under the Federal Power Act with ensuring that rates and practices are just, 
reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory. 
 
Further, the term “public interest” could mean reliability, affordability, competition, 
environmental goals, consumer protection, competition, economic development, and state 
policy objectives. There is something for every perspective. Implementing such a mandate

9) 
Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the 
Nominating Committee? 
X 
Comments: 
It is important that states share the opportunity and burden of identifying and electing PJM 
Board members. However, the Nominating Committee process must not become a political 
process. So, assuming rational actors behave reasonably and considering the existing slate 
of five Sector voting members and two Board of Directors voting members, ENGIE 
recommends the addition of onevoting slot for the PJM states.  This would allow the states 
to have a meaningful impact on the process.  However, adding one member to the 
Nominating Committee results in an even number of voting members and the potential for 
a vote with no majority.  In order to address this possible outcome, ENGIE supports the 
addition of one “tie-break” member to the Nominating Committee jointly elected by the 
five Sectors.  This ninth voting member could participate in the Nominating Committee 
process but only vote if needed to create a majority choice. 
 
10) 
Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management 
compensation to their achievement? 
X 
Comments: 
Adopting public performance metrics may be counterproductive and could lead to 
unintended consequences. We have observed that there is already an environment of 
heightened and unproductive scrutiny.  Metrics may tend to promote near-term thinking 
and action, particularly at the Board level, while a professional Board should be thinking 
about long term strategy and organizational health.   
 
Any metrics adopted must be clearly defined and support the organization’s strategy and 
priorities whenever these items are clearly defined by leadership.

14) 
Should the Organization of PJM States Inc (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights 
over resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a 
supermajority of OPSI agrees on an alternative proposal? 
 
X 
Comments: 
State participation in the PJM stakeholder process through an organization such as OPSI is 
vital to capturing the full scope of stakeholder interests. However, it is not necessary for 
states to have their own jump ball rights if the PJM stakeholders, generally have their own 
right. Creating a single jump ball right for all stakeholders improves the chances for a 
communicative and efficient stakeholder process.  If states have a proposal that they would 
like to bring to PJM and the stakeholders it should be brought through the designated 
stakeholder process. It should be voted the same way as any other stakeholder proposal and 
put forward as a jump ball alternative to a PJM proposal upon obtaining the necessary 
share of stakeholder votes. 
In the New England Power Pool (NEPOOL) stakeholder process, the states effectively have 
a jump ball right through their collaboration through the New England States Committee on 
Electricity (NESCOE), because NESCOE may bring a proposal through the NEPOOL 
stakeholder process and if it receives a sufficient percentage of votes may become a jump 
ball filing.  
 
15) 
Should the Members Committee have “jump ball” filing rights over the 
portions of the tariff where PJM has 205 filing rights when a supermajority 
of stakeholders approves an alternative proposal? 
X 
Comments: 
Jump ball filing rights would provide stakeholders not only with a right to have the 
Commission review the stakeholders’ proposal on par with PJM approval but also an 
effective preventative measure that would encourage increased engagement between 
PJM and its stakeholders.  
 
ENGIE has experience in ISO-NE, where the ability to pursue a jump ball filing 
incentivizes ISO-NE to work with stakeholders to resolve their concerns with its

16) 
Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered from 
two-thirds sector-weighted voting? 
X 
Comments: 
Two-thirds remains a reasonable threshold.  
 
17) 
Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing 
committees, also apply to lower-level committees? 
X 
Comments: 
Affiliate voting should be strictly prohibited. It provides an unwarranted advantage to a 
disproportionately small percentage of the PJM member population. Affiliate voting may 
lead to increased gaming opportunities and is inherently unfair.

18) 
In addition to OPSI, should there be a second state representative group

19) 
Should state representatives have a formal voting role in the stakeholder 
process (limited to certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional 
transmission cost allocation) in addition to the consumer advocate votes? 
X 
Comments:  
ENGIE believes that a State within the PJM footprint could coordinate its energy policy 
interests across its Consumer Advocate, Public Utility Commission and Governor’s Energy 
Policy Office to vote as part of its existing rights as a PJM Member in the End-Use 
Customers sector.  Allowing the different departments within a State’s energy policy 
making apparatus to have enhanced Stakeholder input such as OPSI or a new model 
similar to NESCOE is addressed in Question 18. 
 
20) 
Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a section 205 
filing related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation? 
X 
Comments: 
Whether or not this consultation is mandated, PJM should be consulting with all of its 
stakeholders prior to making any section 205 filing, regardless of subject matter.  While the 
level and frequency of engagement will depend on the complexity and impact of the filing, 
early and meaningful engagement with OPSI and all stakeholders is key to a successful 
filing at FERC.

ENGIE appreciates the opportunity to engage with the Commission, PJM, the PJM states,

PJM.
