I. Summary
The unprecedented growth in demand in the PJM region driven by large customers, primarily data centers, is driving the need for significant new transmission and exasperating resource adequacy concerns due, in large part, to an aging generation fleet and long delays in interconnecting new generation.  While co-location allows large loads to bypass the conventional load connection process and accelerate data center deployment, if not carefully managed, co-location also has the potential to push the PJM region into a resource adequacy and affordability crisis.

Since the formation of PJM, load customers seeking to connect to the grid have taken Network Integration Transmission Service (“NITS”) to connect to the grid.  While the

III. Comments

A. PJM’s Existing NITS Rules are Just and Reasonable and Not Unduly
Discriminatory or Preferential to Co-location Customers.

The existing PJM Tariff unambiguously affords the ability to connect new customers in a co-location arrangement, provided the customer load is willing to be treated as network load and contributes to the fixed costs of operating the transmission grid through its NITS allocation.  While changing needs on the grid certainly leave room for improvements to the PJM framework for serving new data center load, it is also important to recognize that the existing system provides a stable foundation that permits new load to connect to the grid.  As PJM notes,5 the options available to customers today include the ability of a generator to be physically co-located with generation.

Critical to the Commission’s approach is the fact that co-location arrangements can occur with a rapidity that our existing planning processes are not designed to address.  While existing rules also allow utilities to ensure that new loads are connected in an orderly manner and that new service is only allowed to commence once transmission needs have been addressed, any accelerated co-location process must address what happens if a co-location request would precipitate a resource adequacy or affordability crisis.  This should include upfront rules about deadlines for providing advance notice, study processes, and, ideally, an opportunity for relevant state agencies to provide feedback.  Any rules coming out of this proceeding should continue to prioritize system reliability and affordability and avoid creating a situation where data center expansion contributes to a transmission or resource adequacy shortfall.  While it is important that PJM and the Commission facilitate access for new users of the electric grid, the desire of data center customers for accelerated grid access must be balanced against the threat of increased rates and diminished reliability.

The lost transmission revenue can be significant.  Based on data provided by the Indicated Transmission Owners, the potential cost impact to customers by large loads evading

B. Any Just and Reasonable Changes to PJM’s Tariff Should Ensure that
Comparable Electrical Impacts Incur Comparable Transmission Charges.

If the Commission elects to direct changes to PJM’s existing tariff methodology for assigning transmission charges to co-located load, it should ensure that use-cases with similar electrical impacts pay comparable rates.  As we explained in our prior comments, PJM’s electrical studies “studies do not generate different results because a load is co-located with generation on one side of the breaker versus on the other side as network load.  Electrically, the flows are the same.”7

The corollary to this principle is that what “type” of meter is used to connect a new load can have significant implications for cost allocation.  Load located behind a “generator meter” pays one rate; load located behind a “load meter” pays a different rate – even though the electrical impacts are identical.  Customers located behind a load meter are subject to a host of charges that simply do not apply to customers locating behind a generator meter.8  For example, generators only pay for “but-for the generator” system upgrades when they go through the initial interconnection cost allocation process and are categorically exempt from transmission service, ancillary service, and Schedule 12 Regional Transmission Expansion Planning (“RTEP”) baseline upgrade costs.  If any new (i.e., post-interconnection) upgrades are required to maintain a reliable grid and comply with mandatory NERC standards, generators will not be assigned cost responsibility. C. Principles for a New Interconnection Service if FERC Determines that
Alternative Tariff Rules are Required.

One of the key questions facing the Commission in this docket (assuming it elects to take action) is whether generators that have gone through the existing PJM interconnection process need to be re-analyzed when adding co-located load, and if so, what that study should look like.  As a guiding principle, we recommend that the Commission adopt a principle of “comparability” designed to ensure that new co-location arrangements go through a study process that is comparable in terms of timing and cost responsibility to a new load locating in- front-of-the-meter.  Such an approach would help the “regulatory gap”9 between the generator interconnection process and the Commission’s prior holding in Order No. 2003-A that “Interconnection Service is separate from the delivery component of Transmission Service.”10 In its answer, PJM identified five options as new options that are not currently available to customers without revisions to the tariff.  These options all fall short of achieving the type of comparability between co-located and front-of-the-meter customers that we advocate. One option would be a four-party agreement among the co-located generator, co- located load, interconnecting transmission owner, and PJM.  A new pro forma process would entail the following steps:

1. Establish new customer categories: Co-located Generator and Co-located Load, where the generator and the load parties would be eligible to interconnect to a single POI under a single interconnection agreement and both the generator and load would be parties to the agreement.

Under either a three- or four-party agreement, the co-located generator would be able to connect to the grid, make wholesale sales to network load or point-to-point load customers, and make retail sales to its directly connected customers, subject to any applicable state tariff governing such retail sales.  The generator would be responsible for paying for transmission service commensurate with the maximum amount of load that it seeks to supply.  If the new co-located load and generation are appropriately sized to minimize new power flows on the grid, such a configuration would provide the least impact to the grid and therefore likely a candidate for an expedited study process.

D. The Need for a Co-Location “Safety Valve” to Ensure System Reliability and Customer Affordability.

The Show Cause Order asked the parties to “explain whether the existing Tariff rules are sufficient to ensure resource adequacy if increasing numbers of large existing generators

10

choose to co-locate with load.”15  In our view, the mass migration of existing generators to a co-location configuration could absolutely threaten the reliability of the PJM grid and worsen the already dire affordability crisis faced by many PJM consumers.  To the best of our knowledge, none of the studies proposed by PJM or the Indicated Transmission Owners would evaluate resource adequacy considerations.

The Commission should strongly consider a co-location “safety valve” that ensures that co-location does not drive PJM into shortage conditions.  As noted in NERC in 2023, “[d]riven by electrification, hydrogen production, data centers, crypto mining, and other computational and energy-intensive methods such as artificial intelligence (AI), new loads can emerge and grow faster than generation and transmission can be built.”16  And the situation has only grown more challenging since then.17  Requiring completion of the study process that addresses both transmission reliability and resource adequacy prior to energizing any new co-located load additions would help ensure system reliability and provide adequate time for markets to adjust to the changing supply-demand balance.

As noted above, PJM had only 514 MW of excess capacity in the 2025/2026 delivery year.  Load is projected to grow substantially in 2026, with over 2,700 MW of new load growth now expected above levels projected in 2024.  There is no need to recount the litany of challenges that PJM has faced interconnecting new generators or the fact that its three-year forward auction is now effectively a prompt auction.  Nuclear generating units, for example, have been particularly attractive candidates for co-location.  Removing even a single nuclear generator from the capacity supply stack would, assuming current market trends hold, drive PJM into a resource adequacy shortfall. E. The Commission Should Address the Risk of Stranded Transmission
Investment Associated with Co-Location.

One significant consumer protection concern is how to manage the risk associated with transmission facilities to support new co-located or network load if that load does not materialize.  The transmission investment necessary to serve these new mega-loads is large and the payback period is long, and their electricity usage is highly uncertain.  Data center energy efficiency breakthroughs, better algorithm optimization, quantum computing, and the normal ups and downs of a new industry could all result in data centers using less – or more – energy than anticipated.  The stranded cost risk to consumers is real, particularly as virtually

12

all of PJM’s proposed solutions continue to have data center-driven transmission investments collected through socialized cost allocation methodologies that are often spread costs over multiple jurisdictions.

Indeed, individual states and utilities have begun implementing minimum contract terms, minimum service level requirements, and “take or pay” structures backed by financial security requirements, these measures only protect customers in the specific utility zone that is implementing those protections.  While long-term contracts, backed by postings of financial security may protect the customers of a single utility, such payments do little to protect customers in the rest of PJM.

While the Commission is certainly not in the business of managing the economic development decisions of individual states, it should ensure that any PJM tariff changes account for the potential that data center load may not materialize and collaborate with its state partners to manage this risk.  The need for federal-state coordination is perfectly illustrated in an ongoing proceeding before the Ohio Public Utilities Commission.18  There, Ohio utilities are battling over whether data center customers should be required to enter long-term contracts before being allowed to connect to the grid.  Thus, the Commission should consider imposing comparable “take or pay” structures for new large loads triggering transmission upgrades and backed by financial security postings.

H.  The Commission Should Ensure that any Proposed Changes go Through a
Thorough Stakeholder Vetting.

The Commission should ensure that any new tariff process arising out of this Show Cause Order be subject to further development in an open stakeholder forum.  There is ample evidence of prior attempts by customers to effectuate rules outside the stakeholder process and attempt to implement new business models not defined in the PJM rules.  Invariably, PJM rejected such attempts and worked with members to convene a stakeholder process to determine if tariff changes were warranted, and if so, what those tariff changes and accompanying manuals should be.25  PJM and FERC should again reject implementing new rules for customers without allowing for an open stakeholder process to vet such efforts.

IV. Conclusion
The current PJM Tariff process for connecting network load or point-to-point load is just and reasonable as it currently exists.  The well-published concerns about inadequate resource additions to meet the projected load, however, warrant active FERC and PJM efforts to actively manage the amount of co-location load coming onto the system, the rate of such additions, and the protection of consumers from the risks that the load does not materialize as expected.
