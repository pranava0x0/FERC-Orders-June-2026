II. RESOURCE ADEQUACY AND INADEQUATE CAPACITY ARE THE
CENTRAL CHALLENGE

A. The Current Resource Adequacy Challenge in the PJM Footprint Is Not
the Result of Poor Governance.

As the Commission and industry have recognized, electric load demand is outpacing supply at an unprecedented rate due to the rise in new large loads interconnection requests.4 Further compounding the challenge is the premature retirement of existing generation and the delayed rate of entry of new generation resources.5 This mismatch between rapidly increasing demand and inadequate generation supply is the preeminent threat to grid reliability and affordability. Peak demand in PJM is projected to increase dramatically, driven largely by data center growth.6 At the same time, state policies have accelerated retirements of existing dispatchable generation. Since 2015, PJM has seen over 34 GW of generation retire.7 Even with PJM’s interconnection queue reforms, the region still needs new dispatchable generation. Although PJM has the authority to administer markets, plan transmission, and operate the transmission system, PJM does not have the authority to compel or otherwise direct the construction of generation resources.8 For that reason, governance reform alone cannot solve the capacity shortage.

B. PJM Governance Reform Will Not Solve the Missing Megawatt Problem.
Section 201 of the Federal Power Act reserves to the states the authority over generation development, siting, and resource adequacy.10 While PJM has limited tools to support resource adequacy, including administering markets to produce price signals and curtailment mechanisms, under the cooperative federalism framework, ultimate direct authority over resource adequacy rests with the states. Thus, only the states have the authority to dictate whether sufficient generation is built on a timely basis.

The PJM interconnection queue reforms have done little to add new capacity to the system. As a result, capacity prices for the two most recent Base Residual Auctions cleared at or near the administrative cap producing record high prices.11 Yet PJM still faces regionwide reliability concerns tied to insufficient new generation resources. As such, PJM governance is not the sole constraint. The solution therefore must include state level action and state policy reforms that account for large load growth and remove barriers to entry for new generation including permitting

C. PJM’s Diverse Footprint is Distinct from Neighboring RTOs/ISOs and
Contributes to both Resource Adequacy and Governance Challenges.

There is no question that the states have the primary role in ensuring resource adequacy and have a significant influence on PJM governance. How that influence is exercised, however, raises complex and important considerations. The Commission should consider the noteworthy variation across PJM when compared to other RTO regions. The geographic footprint of PJM covers all or part of thirteen states and the District of Columbia. Those jurisdictions do not share a single energy policy, regulatory structure, or resource adequacy model. PJM includes both vertically integrated and restructured states, and its states have different generation policies, retail structures, load growth profiles, resource adequacy preferences, and political priorities. Those differences have affected regional infrastructure development and resource adequacy, including through generation retirements.

This diversity makes PJM materially different from RTOs/ISOs in which state engagement can be represented by a shared, or at least substantially similar, set of state policies. The coordination required to reach state consensus in PJM on complex market and transmission planning issues would likely further delay efforts to bring new generation resources online. The

III. THE COST OF ADDRESSING THE RESOURCE ADEQUACY
CHALLENGE SHOULD NOT FALL TO EXISTING CUSTOMERS

A. Existing Customers Are Bearing the Costs of the Growing Resource
Adequacy Challenge.

FirstEnergy recognizes the importance of protecting existing customers from dramatic cost increases caused by the growth of data centers and new large loads. As the supply and demand imbalance persists, the costs increasingly appear in consumer bills. Despite soaring capacity auction prices which resulted in all-time high clearing prices or the market cap in multiple auctions, consumers have paid $16 billion with minimal new generation resulting.12 The Commission should focus any reforms on solutions that will equip PJM with the ability to implement solutions that improve reliability and mitigate the costs customers are now shouldering.

The Commission should not rely solely on governance reforms to protect customers from increased rates and reliability risks caused by the ongoing resource adequacy challenge. As explained above, the most effective solution will require state level changes that support the development of and investment in new dispatchable generation.

B. New Large Load Customers Should Be Required to Bring the Generation
Necessary to Serve Their Load or Take Service Through a Reliability
Backstop Procurement Mechanism.

New large loads, including data centers, are driving much of the unprecedented growth in electric demand. Under longstanding cost causation principles, the cost of meeting that demand should be borne by the new large loads, not existing customers. As the Commission considers appropriate reforms to address the resource adequacy challenge, the central focus of any solution must be to bring more generation online.

A bring-your-own-generation construct protects existing customers from subsidizing new large loads while helping bring new generation online. Alternatively, new large loads that do not bring their own generation should take service through a targeted reliability backstop procurement construct. That backstop would secure the resources needed to meet increased demand and maintain system reliability.

The current framework both raises affordability concerns and worsens the resource adequacy challenge. New large loads that materially increase system demand should be expected either to bring adequate supply resources or bear the costs of procuring adequate additional generation necessary to support their demand.  Under the status quo interconnection framework, new large loads will continue to adversely impact existing customers that are currently absorbing the increasing costs of generation. A construct that requires that new large loads either bring their own generation or bear the costs of targeted reliability backstop procurement better aligns cost responsibility with cost causation and mitigates adverse impacts on existing customers.

C. Generation Procured for Native Load Growth Should Flow Through a
Pass-Through Tariff.

FirstEnergy supports a framework that ensures the customer causing the need for or benefiting from the availability of increased generation bears the costs. The Commission should

further consider reforms that allow costs to serve native load growth, separate from new large load demand, to flow through to native-load customers through a pass-through tariff. Consistent with cost causation principles, once a state, utility, or other responsible entity determines that new generation must be procured to serve native load growth, distinct from large load growth, the associated costs should be recovered through a transparent pass-through tariff. Under a pass-through tariff, a state-regulated utility would recover the prudently incurred costs of generation resources procured to serve incremental native-load growth through a dedicated retail rider charged to the customers benefiting from that procurement. The rider would permit periodic recovery of capacity, generation, and associated resource adequacy costs and would align cost responsibility with the customers whose load growth necessitated the new generation investment. Such a tariff would reduce the risk that customers not causing the need for or benefiting from the new capacity procurement subsidize resources procured for incremental native load growth.

D. FirstEnergy Supports Resource Adequacy Solutions that Assign Costs to
the Customers Driving the Demand or Benefiting from the New
Generation.

FirstEnergy shares the Commission’s goal of ensuring that customers have timely and efficient access to adequate, affordable, and reliable service. To meet that goal, FirstEnergy continues to support a transmission expansion framework that promotes affordability by requiring large load customers pay an expansion rate designed to recover network upgrade costs needed to support their interconnection.

This framework requires the large load driving new demand to bear the cost of interconnection and associated network upgrades. It also reduces costs to existing customers while preserving operational benefits for all customers.  FirstEnergy submitted in the large load

IV. GOVERNANCE REFORMS SHOULD BE LIMITED TO REFORMS
THAT IMPROVE EFFICIENCY WHILE RESPECTING EXISTING
JURISDICTIONAL AND CONTRACTUAL BOUNDARIES

FirstEnergy agrees that PJM governance is important and warrants Commission attention, particularly because efficient decision-making is needed to address the resource adequacy challenge threatening affordability and reliability. If the Commission directs reforms to PJM governance or the stakeholder process, those reforms should be targeted and should not diminish the rights and responsibilities established under the Consolidated Transmission Owners Agreement (“CTOA”), which is the foundational agreement that created and defines PJM’s authority over regional transmission planning and operation.

FirstEnergy supports limited reforms to the PJM governance structure in three respects. First, FirstEnergy recommends that the stakeholder process be reformed to assume a purely advisory role in PJM governance.  Second, FirstEnergy recommends that the states be given formal standing at PJM with a defined right to bring issues directly to the Board. Finally, PJM governance reform should respect both the jurisdictional divide between the Commission and the states and the balance of rights and obligations between PJM and PJM transmission owners established under the CTOA.

FirstEnergy recommends that the stakeholder process transition to an advisory vote approach that is less capable of producing prolonged deadlock. The reformed process would follow a course under which PJM would develop proposals, those proposals would be subject to stakeholder discussion and advice, and the Board would then, with the benefit of stakeholder input,

The PJM Board should be the final decision-maker on solutions to issues that are within the authority of the Board under the PJM governing documents including the CTOA. This streamlined process would retain PJM Board accountability while enabling quicker decision-making.

FirstEnergy also recommends an enhanced role for states with a more meaningful “seat at the table.” States currently have only a limited role in PJM governance despite the fact that they have primary authority over resource adequacy and transmission siting.  States need a formal governance role rather than soft influence through fragmented OPSI interactions and one-off communications.  The PJM governance structure must incorporate meaningful input from the PJM states. In order to provide the States with greater access to the PJM Board, the States should have formal standing at PJM, and a defined right to bring issues directly to the Board. Participation at formal meetings with the Board will give States greater access and will improve accountability and responsiveness.

As a threshold consideration to PJM governance reform, it is material to recall that the CTOA is the cornerstone upon which PJM obtained the authority to operate as an RTO.  Any modification to the PJM governance rules must consider the implications for the division of rights and obligations established under the CTOA.  An essential and exclusive right of the PJM Transmission Owners is the FPA section 205 filing rights over transmission rates and rate design, including local and regional rates.14 Moreover, because FPA Section 205 grants filing rights to “public utilities,” independent Section 205 filing authority cannot be granted to the states absent a statutory amendment by Congress. Consistent with the Federal Power Act, the CTOA assigns section 205 rights and responsibilities to PJM and the Transmission Owners—both jurisdictional

V. CONCLUSION
For the reasons set forth above, FirstEnergy respectfully urges the Commission to issue an order that: (i) recognizes resource adequacy, not governance alone, as the central challenge facing PJM; (ii) directs PJM to pursue targeted governance reforms that improve efficiency while preserving CTOA rights; (iii) directs PJM to develop mechanisms under which new large loads either bring generation or bear the costs of reliability backstop procurement; and (iv) directs PJM to make a compliance filing proposing tariff revisions consistent with those principles.
