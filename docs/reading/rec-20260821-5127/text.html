The PJM Power Providers Group ("P3") submits these post-technical conference comments in response to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's ("Commission" or "FERC") July 30, 2026, Notice Requesting Post-Conference Comments in Docket No. AD26-7-000. P3 is a non- profit organization dedicated to promoting properly designed and well-functioning competitive markets in the PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. ("PJM") region. Combined, P3 members own and operate over 108,000 MWs of generation assets and produce enough power to supply over 63 million homes across 13 states and the District of Columbia.1

I. CORE GOVERNANCE PRINCIPLES
•  Anchor All Reforms in Market Integrity & Merchant Capital Investibility: PJM's core mandate must remain operating robust, competitive wholesale markets that ensure reliability at the lowest reasonable cost. Governance reforms must not become a vehicle for compromising Regional Transmission Organization (“RTO”) independence or upending the stakeholder model. PJM's success hinges on attracting private merchant capital; reforms that introduce political volatility or regulatory uncertainty will damage investor confidence at a moment in time when investor confidence must be paramount.

•  Uphold Section 205 Balance of Filing Rights: The division of Federal Power Act (“FPA”) Section 205 filing rights is a core structural feature of the PJM region. Suggestions to unilaterally broaden the PJM Board's or any state/stakeholder body's filing rights over matters traditionally

•  Resist Expansion of Unilateral Board Authority: While the Board possesses necessary Tariff authority to act in true emergencies, unilateral action must remain an extraordinary tool. Routine expansion of unilateral Board authority erodes transparency, bypasses stakeholder expertise, and results in poorly formulated filings that burden the Commission and courts.

•  Preserve Board Independence:  Having an independent Board that is accountable to the mandate of the PJM Operating Agreement is essential to the integrity of the PJM mission.  Where they are authorized to act, Board members should act in the best interest of the grid and the market after hearing input from PJM members and states.  PJM’s mission is defined in the Operating Agreement and that should be the standard by which the Board is evaluated.

•
Respect Core Mission vs. Political Priorities: PJM sits at the crossroads of 14 jurisdictions with divergent policy goals. PJM's core mission is to reliably operate an interstate grid and dispatch lowest-cost resources via a competitive market. PJM must not be diverted from this mission and must remain focused on the market that serves state in the entire region.

III.  COMMENTS
The governance reforms under consideration in this proceeding cannot be evaluated in a vacuum. PJM stands at a critical juncture in its history, defined by unprecedented demand expansion and a generational transformation of the power fleet. Driven by the explosive growth of data centers and widespread electrification, electricity demand across the 13-state and District of Columbia footprint is growing at rates never seen.

At the same time, power developers are stepping up to meet this moment with extraordinary commercial interest. Today, over 55 GW of new generation capacity has already cleared PJM’s interconnection queue and stands ready for construction, while PJM's recent interconnection queue Cycle 1 saw a record-shattering wave of project applications totaling over 220 GW.2 Furthermore, PJM’s request for information for generation developers interested in serving large loads drew over 130 GW of developer proposals eager to contract directly with new energy-intensive customers.3

The overwhelming regulatory mandate facing PJM and the Commission is clear: the region must turn its immediate, undivided focus toward executing contracts for new supply, establishing clear federal and state rules related to the integration of large loads, expanding transmission capacity, and delivering power reliably to the grid. Meeting this challenge will require billions of dollars in private, at-risk merchant capital and it is axiomatic that investor capital flows where regulatory and market rules are predictable, stable, and transparent.

Distracting PJM, state regulators, and market participants with prolonged structural governance debates—or worse, adopting governance changes that politicize PJM Board decisions, dilute sector-weighted voting, or destabilize FPA Section 205 filing rights—risks fracturing investor confidence at the exact moment it is needed most.
