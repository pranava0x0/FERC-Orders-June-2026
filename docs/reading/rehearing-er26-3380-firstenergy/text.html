I. BACKGROUND
Because the September 29 Order is the culmination of an expedited process that unfolded over approximately nine months, several major events are relevant to this request.  On January 16, 2026, the National Energy Dominance Council and the governors of all thirteen PJM states issued the Statement of Principles, urging PJM to "[p]rovide 15-year price certainty for new capacity resources" through a reliability backstop auction and to allocate the costs of new capacity to LSEs with data centers that have not self-procured new capacity or agreed to be curtailable.  Shortly thereafter the PJM Board announced its decision in the Critical Issue Fast Path ("CIFP") process addressing large load additions, which included accelerating a backstop capacity procurement.  In April the PJM Board initiated a targeted CIFP process to develop the RBP. On June 30, 2026, the PJM Members Committee voted on thirteen RBP proposals. Only one—sponsored by a coalition of Electric Distributors and the Data Center Coalition ("Joint Proposal")—received the two-thirds sector-weighted support needed for endorsement.  PJM's own proposal did not.  Then on July 14, 2026, PJM announced that the 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction ("BRA") cleared short of the PJM Region's reliability requirement.  In response, on July 27, 2026, the PJM Board adopted PJM's proposal, with three "targeted changes," rather than the Joint Proposal.  PJM filed its RBP proposal

III. ARGUMENT
A. The Commission Erred in Accepting PJM's Levelized Average-Price Framework Without Responding to FirstEnergy's Showing That It Permits Front-Loaded Contract Prices.

PJM proposes that the levelized procurement cost of all selected RBP Sell Offers not exceed $555/MW-day on a MW-weighted average basis over all delivery years of the RBP.24  PJM characterizes this as a "budget approach" that "allows for potentially fluctuating offers across the 15 delivery years of the RBP term," and contends that prices above $555/MW-day "in some

delivery years may nevertheless be appropriate."25  Levelized cost is calculated using a discount rate equal to a 9.5% after-tax weighted average cost of capital.26

FirstEnergy explained that the predictable consequence of this flexibility is that sellers will front-load their costs into the initial years of their contracts.  A seller could, for example, offer $1,000/MW-day for the first several years of the contract and balance that out with sub-market prices in the later years, forcing customers to bear extremely large increases in capacity costs in the initial delivery years—during a period of acute concern, including among members of this Commission, about affordability.27  FirstEnergy further demonstrated that a levelized structure that authorizes the front loading of payments is not needed: the Statement of Principles calls for fifteen- year price certainty; significant new generation is being built by vertically integrated utilities with straight-line, fixed-value cost recovery; and experience under PURPA shows that non-incumbent investors are generally willing to build new generation on a straight-line, fixed-value basis.28 The September 29 Order does not respond to this argument.  The Commission instead finds that a maximum willingness to pay "set at the MW-weighted average of the levelized procurement cost over the RBP commitment term reasonably balances the need to expeditiously procure capacity . . . with ensuring that the average procurement price does not exceed load's maximum willingness to pay," and that $555/MW-day is a reasonable proxy for load's maximum willingness to pay.29  Those findings address the average level of the cap, but not the temporal distribution of payments that the averaging methodology permits. B. The Commission Erred in Finding PJM's Resource Eligibility and Selection
Rules Just and Reasonable Without Considering FirstEnergy's Evidence
Regarding the Reliability Value of Dispatchable Resources.
PJM justifies the RBP as an extraordinary measure necessary to address the approximately 6,831 MW capacity shortfall identified in the 2028/2029 BRA and to preserve resource adequacy in the PJM Region.33  FirstEnergy demonstrated that, because the stated purpose of the procurement is to address a reliability need, the Commission should ensure that it prioritizes resources that provide firm and dependable capacity.34  FirstEnergy supported that showing with record evidence from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation ("NERC"), which has identified that conventional generators provide much of the essential reliability services that support reliable operation of the grid; that wind and solar resources lack the spinning mass and traditional excitation systems that provide voltage control and stability; and that dispatchable generators provide system inertia, dynamic reactive support, and frequency response for stable grid operation.35  FirstEnergy further showed that PJM's selection process is designed to select the "least-cost set of resources" without sufficient consideration of the relative reliability value of different resource types.36

The September 29 Order finds PJM's eligibility requirements and selection process just and reasonable.37  It disposes of the argument that the selection process should value dispatchable generation's reliability attributes in a single sentence, stating that the Commission does "not find such additional valuation metrics to be necessary for PJM's proposal to be just and reasonable"

C. The Commission Erred in Finding PJM's Supplier Performance and Credit
Provisions Just and Reasonable Without Addressing the Risk of Post-
Commercial Operation Underperformance.
FirstEnergy demonstrated that the Commission should ensure that customers are adequately protected against supplier non-performance over the full fifteen-year commitment term.42  PJM's proposed protections are directed primarily toward construction risk and the risk that a resource fails to come online in the first instance: PJM proposes an RBP UCAP MW shortfall charge when a committed resource fails to achieve commercial operation, and rescission of the commitment if the resource fails to provide any committed RBP UCAP MW after three consecutive delivery years.43  As FirstEnergy showed, those provisions do not protect customers against the possibility that a resource satisfies its initial commercial operation obligations and then underperforms during the balance of its commitment term—even though LSEs, and ultimately retail customers, remain responsible for funding the commitment throughout that term.44 FirstEnergy further showed that the asymmetry is particularly striking because PJM proposes substantial, and unnecessary, collateral requirements to protect suppliers against the risk of customer non-payment, and asked the Commission to require enhanced performance assurance requirements, ongoing credit support, or other mechanisms ensuring that resources receiving long- term RBP commitments remain accountable to customers throughout the commitment period.45

D. The Commission Erred in Accepting PJM's Demand Response Gating
Criteria Without Resolving Whether Eligible Demand Response Must Be
Supported by Binding Contractual Commitments.
FirstEnergy demonstrated that PJM's proposed gating criteria for demand response resources appear to require only a contract or agreement evidencing a "plan or evidence of deployed enabling infrastructure," not a contract or agreement demonstrating a clear and binding commitment to provide the MWs sold into the RBP.59  If demand response resources may satisfy the gating criteria based merely on a "plan," rather than a binding commitment for the underlying demand response capability, the proposal risks treating speculative resources as equivalent to committed resources—a result that would defeat the purpose of the RBP and require data centers to pay for capacity that has not genuinely been procured.60  FirstEnergy therefore asked the Commission to require PJM to clarify that eligible demand response resources must be supported by binding contractual commitments.61

The September 29 Order summarizes FirstEnergy's argument,62 but does not resolve it. IV. CONCLUSION
For the reasons set forth herein, FirstEnergy respectfully requests that the Commission grant rehearing of the September 29 Order on the issues specified above.  Specifically, the Commission should reject PJM's RBP filing, proceed under the Section 206 proceeding it has instituted in Docket No. EL26-108-000, and establish settlement procedures.  In the alternative, and at a minimum, the Commission should: (1) reject PJM's levelized average-price framework;

(2) direct PJM to strengthen supplier performance assurance and credit requirements for the full commitment term and to clarify that eligible demand response resources must be supported by binding contractual commitments; (3) reject PJM’s shortfall charge proposal because it fails to ensure that customers are adequately protected against supplier non-performance over the full fifteen-year commitment term; (4) vacate its preliminary findings that PJM's eligibility requirements and selection process are just and reasonable; and (5) expand the scope of the paper hearing to encompass the arguments identified herein that the September 29 Order did not address.
