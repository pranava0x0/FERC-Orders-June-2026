In accordance with Section 205 of the Federal Power Act (“FPA”)1 and Part 35 of the Commission’s regulations,2 the New York Independent System Operator, Inc. (“NYISO”) submits proposed revisions to its Open-Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT”) and Market Administration and Control Area Services Tariff (“Services Tariff”).3  The proposed revisions establish new Large Load Integration Procedures (“LLIP”) and related market participation provisions.  They are intended to fully satisfy the concerns raised by the Commission in its Order Instituting Proceeding Under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act (“Show Cause Order”)4 to the extent that they involve issues that are within the scope of the NYISO’s tariff filing authority.5

The proposals in this filing are designed to account for issues and conditions in the NYISO region.  They build on the NYISO’s recently adopted Standard Interconnection Procedures and on various NYISO-specific independent entity variations that are foundational to the interconnection process in New York and that bring many benefits.  They also: (i) address the unprecedented near-term resource adequacy challenges, driven in large part by an expected influx of large loads, that the NYISO now faces; (ii) leverage the NYISO’s existing “financial” transmission reservation service model to maximize the benefits to flexible large loads and other customers; and (iii) contain various mechanisms to protect customers.

A. Large Load
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission found that it would be reasonable to define a large load as “a new commercial or industrial customer, located at a single site behind one or more points of interconnection, and that has a peak load of 50 MW or greater and interconnects to the transmission system at a voltage level of greater than 69 kV, and is not part of a co- location arrangement.”14  The Commission stated that an “appropriate definition, including a load size and interconnection voltage threshold, should be based on the characteristics of NYISO’s transmission system.”15

The NYISO proposes to adopt the Commission’s definition of Large Load with certain variations described below.  In particular, the NYISO proposes to define Large Load for its OATT and Services Tariff as follows:

Large Load shall mean a commercial or industrial Load located at a single site or in close proximity, behind one or more Points of Interconnection, that has a peak Load of 50 MW or greater connected to the New York State Transmission

System, which is subject to and has completed the applicable requirements of the Large Load Integration Procedures in Attachment LL of the ISO OATT.  For

purposes of this definition, Load shall be considered to be located “in close proximity” if it: (i) is located within 2 miles and 2 electrical buses and (ii) is electrically located in the same Capacity Region.16

First, the NYISO proposes to provide that the Load may be located either at a single site or in close proximity.  As indicated in the definition, the NYISO proposes to define “in close proximity” to mean that the Load is both: (i) located within 2 miles and 2 electrical buses and (ii) is electrically located in the same Capacity Region.  Load projects could be spread across a geographic area that necessitate more than one Point of Interconnection (e.g., data center campus) and should be addressed as a single project in assessing their system impacts.  The requirement for both 2 miles and 2 buses constitutes a reasonable basis for defining close proximity based on the different topography across the New York State Transmission System, as: (i) there are parts of the system in which 2 buses are located many miles away from each other (e.g. upstate New York), and (ii) additionally, there are parts of the system in which numerous buses can be located within 2 miles (e.g. New York City). C. Large Load Integration Procedures
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission found that, notwithstanding the NYISO’s existing load interconnection requirements, the NYISO’s existing OATT appears to be unjust and unreasonable because it does not include provisions that address with sufficient clarity and consistency the study process for the provision of transmission service to Eligible Customers on behalf of large loads, including the unique operational characteristics of large loads.29 The NYISO proposes to establish new Large Load Integration Procedures, which will be located in Attachment LL to the OATT.30  The procedures include clear and detailed requirements pursuant to which: (i) an Eligible Customer may submit a Large Load Integration Request for the evaluation of the proposed interconnection of a Large Load Project to the New York State Transmission System and (ii) the NYISO and Transmission Owners, as applicable, will evaluate the proposed interconnection, identify any Attachment Facilities and Network Upgrades required for the reliable interconnection of such project and their estimated costs and schedule, and determine the project’s Firm Withdrawal Service or Non-Firm Withdrawal Service amounts or, if requested, its eligibility to proceed as a Zero Withdrawal Service Load. The NYISO highlights in Part II.C.i key elements of its proposed Large Load Integration Procedures.  The NYISO then provides a high-level overview of the new procedures in Part II.C.ii and in the figure in Attachment VI.  Finally, the NYISO describes the new procedures in detail in Parts II.D through III, including demonstrating that the procedures are just and reasonable and address the findings of the Show Cause Order in light of the NYISO’s distinct planning framework and market structure, its role as a single-state independent system operator (“ISO”), and other New York-specific conditions.  The proposed procedures are supported by the affidavit of Thinh T. Nguyen, NYISO Senior Manager of Interconnection Projects (“Nguyen Affidavit”).

c) The State Exempt Large Load Projects Proposal Is Consistent with
Cooperative Federalism
The NYPSC stated in the Show Cause Order proceeding that the FPA gives it the
primary role in ensuring the adequacy of generation resources in New York, and that it intends to exercise its authority with respect to large load resource adequacy.203 Meanwhile, the Commission has jurisdiction over resource adequacy issues to the extent that they affect wholesale electricity market, interstate transmission, or bulk power system reliability matters.204 The Commission’s interest in these types of resource adequacy issues is reflected in the Show Cause Order, including its informational report directive and its suggestion that the NYISO take other steps to address resource adequacy challenges.205  And the NYISO itself has resource adequacy responsibilities under applicable NERC, NPCC, and NYSRC requirements and its Commission-jurisdictional tariffs.

Thus, resource adequacy implicates both federal and state jurisdiction.  Consistent with the Show Cause Order,206 the NYISO has designed the State Exempt Large Load Projects proposal to respect cooperative federalism.  As the Altman Affidavit explains,207 the NYISO needs to consider the resource adequacy implications of Firm Withdrawal Service awards in order to fulfill its reliability obligations.  The NYISO is not seeking to displace New York State’s resource adequacy authority or “targeting” state-jurisdictional activity.208  The NYISO is simply accounting for resource adequacy implications when deciding whether to make Commission- jurisdictional transmission service available to Commission-jurisdictional Transmission Customers.  At the same time, the NYISO’s proposal would preserve state authority by enabling the NYDPS to determine that certain Large Loads should be allowed to receive firm service even if they would otherwise individually exceed the NYISO’s Resource Adequacy Impact Standard. Making this treatment unavailable to the extent that State Exempt Large Load Projects would collectively cause a resource adequacy violation is not an impermissible intrusion into state jurisdiction.  It is a reasonable safeguard to ensure that federal reliability requirements are maintained.

i. Overview of New Study Process
In the Show Cause Order, the Commission indicated that the NYISO’s tariff appears to lack sufficiently clear and consistent provisions establishing a study process for evaluating the provision of transmission service to Eligible Customers on behalf of large loads that considers the unique characteristics and impacts of large loads on the transmission system, including identifying the Direct Assignment Facilities and Network Upgrades necessary to provide such service reliably and providing a non-binding good-faith estimate of the cost and the time to construct such facilities.228  The Commission further indicated that the NYISO’s tariff may not clearly and consistently require the NYISO or the Transmission Owners to conduct studies that evaluate the transmission system’s ability to withstand risks observed from analyzed large load behavior created by the large load at the point of interconnection, or any other necessary supplemental studies to evaluate the impact of providing transmission service to Eligible Customers on behalf of large loads.229

The NYISO’s existing load interconnection procedures in Sections 3.9 and 4.5 of the OATT provide for it, in cooperation with the applicable Transmission Owner, to perform technical studies to determine whether the proposed interconnection may degrade system reliability or adversely affect the operation of the NYS Power System.230  The NYISO proposes to establish in place of these requirements a new Large Load Integration Study Process in Attachment LL of the OATT. The CSSP is supported by a six-step Transmission Demand Curve representing transmission shortage prices that increase in tandem with further utilization of a transmission facility’s Constraint Reliability Margin (“CRM”). The CRM is a portion of transmission facility transmission capacity that the NYISO has reserved between a predetermined market limit and the facility’s hard limit, i.e. its line rating, which serves to insulate the NYCA transmission system from severe overload risk by allowing transmission scarcity to be reflected in market prices. In times of severe transmission scarcity, i.e. when a transmission facility reaches 100% CRM utilization, flows across a transmission facility will near or meet the facility’s line rating and the facility’s Transmission Demand Curve will produce a transmission constraint price of $4,000/MWh.

The NERC Transmission Loading Relief (“TLR”) process is used to filter/sort external transactions for curtailment, based on a transaction’s ability to affect flows across a given flowgate.  This process uses an individual transaction quantity-share de minimis threshold of 5% when determining whether the curtailment of an individual transaction will materially impact flows across a given flowgate. The NYISO applied similar materiality logic to the CSSP construct, specifically to the Transmission Demand Curve, in determining the $200/MWh bid price cap for NFWS Loads.

The NYISO will use the 5% materiality threshold used in the NERC TLR process in determining which NFWS Loads have material impact on flows across a binding transmission constraint, i.e. an NFWS Load that has a minimum Shift Factor of 5% on a transmission constraint will be considered to have material impact on flows across that constraint. As discussed, when the Transmission Demand Curve binds at 100% CRM utilization, it produces a price of $4,000/MWh on the associated transmission constraint. NYISO believes this is the point at which it becomes most appropriate to ensure NFWS Loads that exacerbate this constraint are curtailed. An NFWS Load whose Shift Factor meets the 5% materiality threshold described above would, except in rare cases, receive a nodal price of $200/MWh when the correlated transmission constraint produces a $4,000/MWh price. By aligning the NFWS Load bid cap with this $200/MWh nodal LBMP, NYISO ensures that NFWS Loads that meet the 5% materiality threshold are curtailed automatically by the market optimization when the CRM of a relevant transmission facility is fully utilized, preventing the Large Load from exacerbating the constraint further and avoiding manual intervention by NYISO Grid Operators.

The proposed bid cap ensures that Non-Firm Withdrawal Service Large Loads will be curtailed in advance of significant transmission security or resource adequacy shortage events.  It also increases the probability that Non-Firm Withdrawal Service Loads that most aggravate a given transmission constraint, i.e. those with higher Shift Factors on a given constraint, will be curtailed in advance of those that do not. The NYISO is proposing to exclude Limited Withdrawal Service Loads, i.e., Loads that are not fully served via Firm Withdrawal Service, from participation in the ISO Administered Markets as a Demand Side Resource (e.g., Special Case Resource (“SCR”), Emergency Demand Response Program (“EDRP”), Distributed Energy Resource (“DER”) and Aggregations).515  It is reasonable to make Large Loads taking NFWS ineligible to receive compensation for demand reductions because Large Loads taking this service have voluntarily elected to take a lower level of transmission service in order to either avoid paying for Network Upgrades or to avoid bringing new ICAP supply to market to mitigate any resource adequacy and/or transmission security impacts they may cause, as determined in the LLIP.  Non-Firm Withdrawal Service Large Loads, by definition, are not entitled to consume Energy from the system at any time. NYISO’s market constructs, specifically the Transmission Congestion Contract market, also provides a pathway to manage congestion cost (i.e., the Transmission Usage Charge associated with firm transmission) for instances where ex-ante price certainty is valuable. This separation permits customers to obtain greater cost certainty while preserving efficient, nondiscriminatory access to transmission capability for all market participants. The financial reservation model also accommodates materially different Large Load operating characteristics without creating administratively defined transmission priorities. A Large Load that can reduce withdrawals during constrained conditions may express that flexibility through its scheduling parameters and applicable operational requirements, while a Large Load that requires uninterrupted withdrawals (and whose interconnection supports such un-interrupted withdrawals) remains exposed to the costs associated with that service. The model therefore preserves a direct relationship between the service a customer values, the flexibility it provides, and the congestion costs it causes, rather than socializing those costs or embedding them in a new class of physical transmission rights. This supports efficient customer decisions regarding location, consumption, onsite supply, contractual hedging, and willingness to curtail. The model is also adaptable as system conditions and Large Load consumption change over time. Financial schedules and locational settlements can reflect the actual hourly—and, in real time, interval-specific—effects of a Large Load’s withdrawals, avoiding the need to establish a static physical reservation based on forecasts that may prove inaccurate or operating assumptions that may later change. That adaptability is especially valuable for Large Loads whose development timelines, utilization levels, and operating profiles may be uncertain. It reduces the risk that scarce transmission capability will be stranded or inefficiently reserved, protects other customers from subsidizing uneconomic uses of the system, and permits Large

VII. THE FPA SECTION 205 STANDARD OF REVIEW AND SEVERABILITY
The Commission has explained that “[u]nder FPA section 205, the Commission is limited to considering the filing before it, … ”643  Federal courts have similarly noted that “[w]hen acting on a public utility’s rate filing under section 205, the Commission undertakes ‘an essentially passive and reactive role’ and restricts itself to evaluating the confined proposal.”644 Courts have further acknowledged that “FERC has interpreted its authority to review rates under [the FPA] as limited to an inquiry into whether the rates proposed by a utility are reasonable— and not to extend to determining whether a proposed rate schedule is more or less reasonable than alternative rate designs.”645 The Commission “is not required to choose the best solution, only a reasonable one.”646

Adhering to these precedents and principles is especially appropriate in this proceeding. The NYISO’s proposal addresses a large number of operationally and legally complex questions in the context of the resource adequacy challenges facing New York.  This included considering jurisdictional issues, particularly the NYPSC’s views as a state regulator in the process of devising its own large load interconnection policies.  The NYISO’s proposal also had to be developed exceptionally quickly while balancing the interests of many parties in order to obtain super-majority stakeholder support. The proposal should therefore be judged on whether it produces just, reasonable, not unduly discriminatory, and lawful rules – not on whether it will result in the best imaginable solution in every instance.
