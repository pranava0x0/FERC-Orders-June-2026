Comments of Advanced Energy United

Advanced Energy United (“United”)[fn:1] applauds the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or “Commission”) leadership in convening a technical conference to discuss the critical but thorny issue of Regional Transmission Organization (“RTO”) governance, and we especially endorse the solutions-oriented approach the agenda advances. We appreciate the opportunity to share our initial perspectives in these pre-conference comments, and look forward to the discussion on July 23 and any next steps that follow.
Before outlining challenges and potential solutions, it is critical to recognize that PJM Interconnection (“PJM”) has brought enormous value to the region it serves over the course of its 30-year history as an RTO in the form of cost savings and a strong record of reliable performance. The unprecedented challenges PJM faces are leading some parties to question its value, but that would be the wrong lesson to take from this moment. Instead, we must focus on keeping what is working and fixing what is not. Reform is needed; radical change is not.
As we collectively seek to diagnose the flaws in PJM’s current governance structure, it is also important to acknowledge that governance is not the sole—or likely even the primary—cause of the region’s current reliability and affordability challenges. Rather, the pace, scale, and uncertainty of load growth, which has rocked the entire industry, has impacted PJM more than any other region. Additionally, PJM’s governance structure and stakeholder process, while imperfect, are not without their merits. PJM’s iterative and deliberative stakeholder process allows different perspectives to be heard—and PJM does consider those perspectives, even if it does not always adopt them. And while it would have been preferable to avoid the need for the multiple Critical Issue Fast Path processes the region has undertaken over the past year, PJM has demonstrated that it can act swiftly on complex issues while taking stakeholder feedback into account. At the same time, PJM is not alone in having a stakeholder process and governance structure that do not function optimally; as noted below, United is also active in the stakeholder processes at the Midcontinent Independent System Operator (“MISO”), Southwest Power Pool (“SPP”), and ISO New England (“ISO-NE”), which each have unique challenges and merits.
With all these points in mind, Advanced Energy United agrees that PJM’s governance and stakeholder process can be improved, and urges incremental reforms to ensure that:
PJM maintains its role as the regional transmission organization across its current footprint;
States have a clear and meaningful voice in PJM’s decision-making process that promotes trust and collaboration without introducing conflicting or chaotic political influence; 
The PJM Board and leadership structure and culture foster innovation while prioritizing reliability and affordability;
The PJM stakeholder process allows beneficial ideas to advance in a clear and streamlined way, and is less biased toward maintaining the status quo due to reforms that:
Prevent beneficial reforms from being blocked by small groups of stakeholders that wield outsize influence;
Re-examine the sector structure to ensure non-incumbent interests have a meaningful voice;
Simplify and streamline the stakeholder feedback process; and
Provide additional filing rights to PJM on transmission planning.
Each of these recommendations is discussed in more detail in the comments that follow.

COMMENTS
Advanced Energy United’s Perspective on PJM and RTO Governance

Advanced Energy United is a national trade association that represents a wide range of market participants in the PJM region and beyond. United itself is an “Associate” member of PJM, which means we pay a nominal annual fee and participate in PJM stakeholder meetings, but do not hold a vote or belong to a sector. Our member companies span multiple sectors of PJM membership, primarily the Generation Owner and Other Supplier sectors. United’s membership also includes companies that are not yet PJM market participants, but that aspire to be in the future, subject to either expansion of their business into the region or implementation of market reforms that remove barriers to participation. United also participates in the stakeholder process at MISO (as a member of the Environmental/Other Sector), SPP (as a voting member at the Markets and Operations Planning Committee in the Transmission Users sector), and at the New England Power Pool (“NEPOOL,” as an “Associate Non-Voting Participant,” a category we helped create). Our comments are informed by our experience in all these markets, as well as the experiences of our member companies.


Design Governance Around Desired Outcomes
An RTO’s organizational structure and culture, governance, and stakeholder process should be oriented toward delivering optimal outcomes. A well-functioning RTO with a purpose-built governance structure and balanced stakeholder process should deliver outcomes (i.e., market rule changes, planning processes, operational improvements) that:
Maintain reliability at a just and reasonable rate;
Are fair and non-discriminatory, and foster competition;
Deliver sufficient market certainty to enable infrastructure investment, yet;
Welcome innovation, beneficial change, and entry of new resource types;
Are responsive to changing market and economic conditions, new technologies, and other exogenous factors; 
Respect and accommodate the (different) policy preferences of constituent states; and
Are clear with respect to responsibilities or reform needs that lie outside the RTO’s jurisdiction, such that other entities (e.g., states) can take appropriate action.

An independently-operated RTO remains the best available framework to deliver these outcomes. Yet looking at the results across the PJM region over the past few years, it is clear they do not align with these ideals. Reliability is at risk and prices have shot up. Open access has been compromised in the name of adding supply as quickly as possible, and market certainty has likewise been undermined by rapid swings in market rules, including but not limited to price caps put in place to protect consumers from skyrocketing electric bills. The region has not embraced new technologies, such as battery storage, that have helped other regions, like ERCOT and California, maintain reliability and keep costs low. The response to changing market conditions has been slow—although the pace of those changes has also been unprecedented. PJM’s market rules have at times been hostile toward state policy preferences, preventing those policies from working harmoniously with its markets. And instead of a clear mutual understanding of the roles and responsibilities of different entities, we have seen more finger-pointing than constructive collaboration, until relatively recently.
In large part, the challenges PJM faces stem from the unprecedented speed of forecasted data center load growth, and the unfortunate timing of PJM’s pause in processing new generator interconnection requests. PJM is now working rapidly to enact solutions and deserves credit for conducting a thoughtful and iterative stakeholder process throughout which it has been navigating conflicting input from stakeholders, states, FERC, and other entities. But, with the unfair advantage of hindsight, it is also clear that more could have been done, sooner, to mitigate the scope of the challenges PJM now faces—not only by PJM, but by the states it serves, by FERC as its regulator, and by the stakeholders and market participants impacted by its actions. PJM’s governance structure is not solely to blame for the slow response to PJM’s dire prognosis of an impending reliability crisis. And indeed, governance reform is not necessary to change course, as evidenced by course-correction that is already well underway. But the fact that all parties did not take immediate and decisive action points to lack of incentive and accountability to do so, and that is a failure of governance that must be addressed. 

Reform Needs Related to Panel One
The goal of governance reform should be for PJM to maintain its role as the regional transmission organization across its current footprint.

The PJM states are clearly dissatisfied with their current role within the PJM governance structure and decision-making process, and PJM and its stakeholders for their part are left sorting through multiple conflicting priorities and positions from across and within the PJM states. It is evident that the status quo has become untenable. Advanced Energy United discourages any efforts to split or shrink the PJM footprint and, instead, supports reforms to governance that maintain and strengthen the cohesion of the PJM region by bolstering and clarifying the role of states. As an independently-governed RTO, PJM remains the best available framework for effective competition in the region it serves. With reform, this structure can deliver reliability and affordability as it has done in the past.
States must have a clear and meaningful role in PJM’s governance process that promotes trust and collaboration without introducing undue or chaotic political influence.

As noted above, Advanced Energy United supports an expanded role for states, provided that this role is clearly articulated, and implemented such that it reduces, rather than amplifies, the potential for divergent and overtly political influences on outcomes at PJM. We anticipate that the PJM states will advance ideas and requests as part of this technical conference ranging from granting of filing rights to representation on or input into selection of the PJM Board, and we look forward to reviewing and reacting to those asks. In these pre-conference comments, we offer recommendations that apply regardless of the specific governance reforms that are pursued.
First, the ability of states to come to constructive consensus positions will be the biggest determinant of their success in shaping outcomes at PJM. We have seen in PJM (with the Statement of Principles[fn:2] earlier this year) and in ISO-NE (with the New England States’ 2020 Vision Document[fn:3]) that when states can come together around a set of shared priorities, real progress can be made. State cohesion is not contingent upon governance reform, but reforms should aim to facilitate state alignment.
Second, to the extent possible, input or positions of states should be delivered through a single channel, such that PJM receives a consensus view from the states when consensus is achieved. The Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”) has historically held that role, but in recent years it is clear that OPSI does not fully capture the input of the states with respect to PJM.[fn:4] The role of OPSI and the expertise of state Public Utility Commissioners remains critical and should not be stamped out or diminished. But Governors’ offices also have a clear interest and perspective in decisions and outcomes at PJM, and these perspectives should be coordinated and ideally consolidated. The structure of the New England States Committee on Electricity (“NESCOE”) offers one potential model to reconcile these critical perspectives by allowing each Governor to appoint one or more NESCOE manager while limiting each state to a single vote; currently, multiple states have both a utility commissioner and an energy office appointee serving as NESCOE managers.[fn:5] NESCOE also invites other state government officials and agency experts to participate as relevant. This more dynamic approach may serve the PJM region better. Under any structure, as today, individual states and agencies within states would maintain their ability to express divergent opinions, conduct their own outreach and engagement with PJM, and seek recourse before FERC.
Third, lack of state consensus should not be permitted to stall progress. It is likely that states will often not agree with each other, but that fact does not eliminate the benefits of state participation. If states are unable to produce a consensus, as long as they cannot hold up the process, no harm is done. Furthermore, “consensus” should not be defined as full unanimity from states across the PJM footprint with different political perspectives, economic priorities, and resource availability. Instead, decisions should be made based at least in part on a load weighted voting approach (rather than OPSI’s current 1 state / 1 vote approach)[fn:6] that requires a certain threshold (e.g., simple majority) for issues to pass. 
Fourth, states must have sufficient resources to facilitate meaningful engagement. This may require additional funding through the PJM tariff to ensure that OPSI (in its current or in a revised form) has sufficient staff and outside resources to keep the states informed, support their efforts to coalesce around shared priorities and positions, and advance proposals where relevant. OPSI plays a critical and beneficial role, achieving a lot with just four permanent staff. But given the multitude of complex market, planning, and operational issues facing PJM; the sheer volume of meeting hours to cover all relevant PJM committees and workshops; the number and diversity of the PJM states; and the organizational and operational responsibilities of running OPSI itself, four people cannot be reasonably expected to support well-informed, well-coordinated state engagement while driving toward meaningful consensus. Investment in a better-resourced state entity that serves to improve state cohesion and thereby enhance market and regulatory certainty will be repaid many times over. If the PJM region comes to FERC with a significantly expanded proposed budget for the regional state entity, the Commission should not balk at it.[fn:7]
Finally, as part of accepting and exercising a larger role within PJM, states should also resolve to take all possible action within their purview to address the current reliability and affordability crisis, including but not limited to:
Unlocking additional demand response and Distributed Energy Resource (“DER”) participation, both at the state level and through PJM’s markets, by “freeing the meters” to facilitate data access;
Pursuing retail Virtual Power Plants (“VPPs”) that are compatible with participation in PJM’s wholesale markets;
Ensuring optimal use of existing infrastructure by prioritizing consideration of surplus interconnection opportunities, demand flexibility, Non-Wires Alternatives (“NWA”), Grid Enhancing Technologies (“GETs”), and Alternative Transmission Technologies (“ATTs”);
Pursuing procurement and correct valuation of battery storage assets to bring additional flexible resources to the PJM region;
Speeding siting and permitting process for distribution- and transmission-connected resources, to help accelerate the path from generator interconnection agreement (“GIA”) to commercial operation, and to facilitate faster adoption of distribution-connected resources;
Adopting policies such as hedging requirements to protect consumers from sudden wholesale market price spikes;
Adopting large load tariffs and create incentive mechanisms to encourage new large loads to supply their own affordable clean energy assets;

The PJM Board and leadership structure and culture should strive to embrace innovation while still prioritizing reliability.

The PJM Board, leadership, and staff are overwhelmingly comprised of talented and dedicated professionals committed to the mission of maintaining reliability at just and reasonable rates. The focus on reliability is critical and must not be sacrificed, but can manifest as a status quo bias that ultimately undermines reliability and affordability over time if new tools and technologies are not adopted as they become available. To be clear, PJM has shown a willingness to adopt new tools, as evidenced by the partnership with Alphabet’s Tapestry. And PJM’s recent initiation of a wide-ranging discussion of holistic market reform challenges to which it does not itself have the answers shows a promising openness to collaboration and fresh ideas.[fn:8] But PJM, like most of the RTOs, is running on outdated software and has seen insufficient uptake of new, proven technologies like ATTs and battery storage. This is not the fault of individuals doing their jobs well, but rather emblematic of a risk-averse culture. Governance changes could help to promote measured risk taking and innovation that enhances, rather than undermines, reliability.
Specifically, more interaction between stakeholders and the PJM Board would help to increase trust and expose Board members to commercial realities, emerging challenges, and new technologies and solutions. By relaxing ex parte rules without compromising independence, and by creating more opportunities for stakeholders and the public to hear from and engage with the Board, the region could promote greater understanding and trust. Advanced Energy United notes that board members in SPP are particularly accessible to stakeholders and hands-on in the stakeholder process, yet still maintain their independence.
Other measures to increase accountability would also promote optimal long-term outcomes, which inherently require an openness to new technology and innovation. Specifically, these include (1) posting all ethics documents publicly, as is done in ERCOT; and (2) clarifying fiduciary responsibilities of the board with respect to reliability, per the Transmission Owners Agreement and other governing documents, and to just and reasonable rates and non-discriminatory service, per the Federal Power Act. Relatedly, it would also be appropriate for PJM to obtain additional filing rights with respect to transmission planning, to better reflect PJM’s ultimate responsibility for grid reliability across the region.
Outside of specific governance reforms, leadership can foster a culture of innovation. Advanced Energy United notes that the SPP region has been particularly successful at developing and adopting creative new tools and solutions. The region has been open to conducting brainstorm-style discussions with its stakeholders; for example, the recently-approved Consolidated Planning Process was developed in large part by a small but diverse technical task force working in close collaboration with staff before going through the wider stakeholder process. While SPP’s stakeholder process and representation of stakeholders among working group voting seats can quash reform efforts, especially those that would enhance competition or benefit non-incumbent interests, it is notable that SPP has historically fostered highly collaborative efforts and has more recently initiated a discussion of potential stakeholder and governance reforms. Similarly, SPP has been open to consideration of new technologies and tools, from its early adoption of automation tools in the generator interconnection process to its burgeoning Intermediate Planning Process, which will aim to identify solutions, such as GETs and non-wires solutions, to bridge near-term operational needs while long-term infrastructure upgrades and planning improvements are made.
The Commission has also played an important role in encouraging innovation and adoption of best available tools and practices by convening technical conferences, asking questions of RTOs/ISOs, and uplifting promising solutions, and should continue to do so.

Reforms Related to Panel Two
The PJM stakeholder process should allow beneficial ideas to advance in a clear and streamlined way and avoid bias toward maintaining the status quo.

In response to Panel II questions 1, 5, 6, 7, and 8, Advanced Energy United offers the following observations and recommendations on the PJM stakeholder process, with the goal of driving beneficial outcomes for the region.
The role of the stakeholder process in PJM is critical and should be maintained—but must be improved. PJM market participants have commercial, operational, and practical knowledge and experience that informs their input and positions on proposed reforms. Without this feedback, RTOs risk putting forward proposals that are economically logical and technically sound but practically or commercially infeasible. 
PJM currently has a track record of listening to stakeholder feedback, and this should be maintained and strengthened. In regions where stakeholders hold real power, RTOs are more likely to listen, and compromise and consensus are possible. For example, in ISO-NE, where NEPOOL holds jump ball filing rights, stakeholder amendments that achieve majority stakeholder approval have sometimes been absorbed into ISO-NE’s own proposal; when they are not, the Commission has the benefit of assessing both proposals in parallel. In contrast, where stakeholder feedback is ignored, filings at FERC are often heavily protested and sometimes rejected for reasons that could have been avoided upfront.
At the same time, individual stakeholders act on behalf of their own company’s self-interest, and the power of individual companies and stakeholder blocks must be balanced carefully. Currently it is too easy for a single block of stakeholders to stall proposals that would increase competition and benefit the region overall because they might reduce revenues for those stakeholders. This creates an incumbency advantage and status quo bias that can undermine beneficial reform and undercut disruptive improvements to the market. The purpose of PJM governance is not to protect stakeholder business models; it is to produce reliable power at just and reasonable rates. Governance should recognize future system needs, not solely the interests of existing asset owners. Moreover, governance should support resource entry, not just resource retention. To that end, Advanced Energy United recommends consideration of the following reforms to PJM’s stakeholder process:
Adjust the voting process to reduce the ability of a small set of market participants to block beneficial proposals, and to prevent proposals from being blocked in lower committees;
Re-examine the existing sector structure and consider reforms to ensure that non-incumbent interests have a meaningful voice, as NEPOOL did with the creation of the Alternative Resources sector;
Simplify and streamline the stakeholder feedback process by eliminating the complex “solutions matrix” that requires stakeholders to put forth a complete package, and consider a simple “amendment” process as used in ISO-NE; and
Provide additional filing rights to PJM on transmission planning to reduce stakeholder gridlock and reflect PJM’s ultimate responsibility for regional reliability (as mentioned above).

An additional measure worth considering is endowing the stakeholder process with its own professional entity and legal expertise, following the example of NEPOOL in New England. NEPOOL exists as a formalized stakeholder body, funded primarily by NEPOOL members, and it benefits from representation and support from outside legal counsel that directly serves NEPOOL. Similar to a well-funded regional state entity, this structure and associated outside support promotes organized, thoughtful, and consensus-driven stakeholder participation.

CONCLUSION
Advanced Energy United appreciates the opportunity to provide input to the Commission, the PJM states, PJM stakeholders, and PJM itself in advance of the July 23 technical conference. We look forward to a robust and productive discussion of reforms that will deliver a more affordable, reliable, fair, and competitive PJM.
