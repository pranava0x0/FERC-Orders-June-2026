II. The Commission Should Clarify that PJM Must Describe the General Reliability Circumstances Pursuant to Which Interim NITS, Non-Firm and Firm Contract Demand Services May Each Be Curtailed.

The June 18 Order “disagree[s] with commenters that PJM must provide specific details in its Tariff, such as the trigger and procedures for curtailment of Interim NITS and FCD or NFCD transmission services, or how PJM will enforce its curtailment procedures.”2 It finds that “requiring PJM to specify the curtailment priority of [the] new transmission services in its Tariff, while leaving specific implementation details in its manuals and individual service agreements, provides transmission customers sufficient certainty as to their priority relative to other

transmission services and ensures that PJM has sufficient flexibility to preserve system reliability in varying operating conditions.”3

As a general matter, Eolian believes that a unified, specific approach to reliability triggers for non-firm service adopted across grid operators and based on existing reliability frameworks would provide clear and consistent rules of the road to developers of load and generation resources, thereby reducing transaction costs, and facilitating the scale-up of efficient, cost- effective business models for the development of load and supply designed to take advantage of non-firm service. And by basing such triggers on transparent, actionable, and already-established reliability criteria—such as limiting resource adequacy-based curtailment to NERC energy emergency alert thresholds of EOP-011, and limiting transmission security-based curtailment based on market-based dispatch of a dispatchable load bidding at the cap—a clear and consistent trigger across regions would provide grid operators controls within the existing reliability framework and procedures that ensure adequate system reliability.4 And by basing such triggers on the existing reliability framework and procedures, regions can avoid needless disputes about application of business practices. While we urge the Commission to consider providing all regions with direction or other encouragement to adopt such criteria, we recognize that this proceeding may not be the appropriate place for the Commission to establish such a specific, nationwide rule.

Under City of Cleveland v. III. The Commission Should Clarify that PJM Must Allow Load Taking Interim NITS to Rely on Any Resource Not Obligated to Deliver to PJM or Another Entity

The Commission’s Order addresses the question whether an Interim NITS customer may access a Co-Located Generator during curtailments (i.e., the transmission customer’s curtailment obligation is reduced according to the output of its Co-Located Generator). It finds that “it is just and reasonable to prohibit the Eligible Customer taking Interim NITS on behalf of Co-Located Load from drawing energy from the Co-Located Generator when the Eligible Customer is curtailed, if the Co-Located Generator is a capacity resource.”23 At the same time, however, the Commission identifies two specific circumstances where “such a restriction would not be applicable”24: (1) “if the associated Co-Located Generator does not yet have any interconnection service or has only provisional interconnection service”; or (2) “if the associated Co-Located Generator is seeking capacity resource status, but [has] not yet obtained such status.”25

The reasonable implication of these findings is that a co-located resource may be relied upon where it is not a Generation Capacity Resource under the PJM tariff or is not otherwise obligated to deliver its services to PJM or another entity apart from the relevant transmission customer taking Interim NITS. Eolian agrees that it is reasonable to bar resources from delivering solely to the Interim NITS customer when they are obligated to serve Network Load.26 However, clarification is needed to confirm that all resources not obligated to deliver to PJM or another entity are eligible to support Interim NITS customers, because the Commission explicitly requires tariff modification only with regard to the specific circumstances it discusses27—leaving out additional circumstances for which a co-located resource could provide capacity to its co-located load without being a Generation Capacity Resource. To more completely effectuate the principle that the Commission is reasonably aiming at, it should make clear that PJM must allow a transmission customer taking Interim NITS to rely on any resource whenever it is not obligated to deliver to PJM or another entity apart from the relevant load taking Interim NITS.

The key challenge which the Commission’s ordering language does not necessarily overcome is that for Interim NITS to be commercially workable, most loads must be able to rely upon a co-located or electrically proximate resource until the load’s network upgrades are completed. Where the load is developed in conjunction with a new generation resource, the load’s network upgrades may not be completed on the same timeline as network upgrades required to interconnect the storage or generation capacity resource. If the interconnection of non-provisional CIRs proceeds faster than that of firm service to load, the Interim NITS

5

customers.

IV.C.
Principles for Curtailment Triggers: Preserving Reliability
1
While Avoiding Excess Mandatory Curtailment
2
We outline potential principles for curtailment triggers to maintain reliability and meet the 3

Commission’s other objectives, including maximizing the benefits of non-firm transmission 4

services. Triggers apply to two categories of reliability: (1) resource adequacy (ensuring 5

sufficient supply) and (2) transmission security (keeping power flows within line limits). The 6

impact these triggers are likely to have on the durability and adoption of the transmission service, 7

described below, strongly indicates that these provisions materially affect rates, charges, and 8

terms of these transmission services.
9
In the first instance, triggers need to be able to maintain reliability so that the co-location 10

arrangement has a net zero impact on resource adequacy and on transmission security. Second, 11

they should feature comparable treatment to similarly situated participants. Third, they should be 12

reasonably well specified so that participants can assess curtailment risk without unnecessary 13

uncertainty. Fourth, they should not require more curtailment than necessary for meeting the 14

other principles.
15
In general, these principles can be adhered to by mandating net withdrawal reductions only after 16

ordinary economic actions have been exhausted, with such mandates grouped together with other 17

comparable curtailments. Indeed, PJM themselves have stated this principle, stating that: “…all 18

generating resources that are available (i.e., not on outage or otherwise unable to be brought on- 19

line in a timely manner) will be utilized to serve the Interim NITS customer. The Interim NITS 20

customer would only be curtailed after these actions are taken and if the system is in an 21

unreliable state due to transmission system violations or capacity/reserve shortages.”16 To avoid 22

unnecessary uncertainty in curtailment volumes that significantly impacts commercial viability 23

of the non-firm large load transmission services, PJM need only file this principle in its Tariff. 24

IV.C.1.a. Resource Adequacy Triggers
1
An RTO or other grid operator is “resource adequate” when it has sufficient generation to serve 2

load except under the most severe operating conditions. If a system does not satisfy resource 3

adequacy criteria, operators may need to rely on excessive curtailments of firm load (rotating 4

brownouts) to keep supply and demand in balance. For a paired generator and load arrangement, 5

the fundamental resource adequacy curtailment trigger is a systemwide shortage in maintaining 6

the supply-demand balance.
7
Such shortages are commonly identified through a NERC-defined energy emergency alert 8

(EEA).17 EEA Level 1 indicates that all available generation is in use; EEA Level 2 indicates that 9

load management procedures are in effect; and EEA Level 3 indicates that firm load interruption, 10

i.e., a rolling brownout, is imminent or already underway. Comparable emergency actions could 11

occur after the issuance of EEA Level 1 (prior to pre-emergency DR, with among the largest 12

curtailment exposure), during the dispatch of pre-emergency DR (prior to EEA Level 2), or 13

during PJM Emergency Procedures Step 2 (which includes issuance of EEA Level 2 and 14

simultaneous dispatch of emergency DR, the least frequent trigger that would meet definitional 15

reliability need and maximize commercial viability).18
16
This choice of trigger would have significant impacts on curtailment volumes: over the last 24 17

months, PJM has had zero EEA 3 events, two emergency demand response deployment events 18

(amounting to EEA 2 events) of 4 and 7.5 hours, and twenty-two pre-emergency demand 19

response deployment events lasting between 4.5 and 10 hours, with a median of 7.5 hours 20

duration.19 This directional trend in curtailment frequency and duration of the EEA levels is a 21

feature of their design, but the absolute value may change as supply conditions continue to 22

tighten, while weather may revert to the mean relative to the unusually high maximum 23

temperatures in PJM during the 2025 and 2026 summers.
24

IV.C.1.b. Transmission Security Triggers
16
Transmission security is required to keep power flows within the operating limits of transmission 17

facilities and maintain other reliability parameters, such as voltage and stability, within 18

acceptable bounds. Because grid conditions can change instantly, transmission facilities must 19

remain within their limits even after a single contingency, such as the loss of a generating unit or 20

transmission line. For transmission security, we outline two possible categories of triggers: 1) 21

pre-contingency actions, such as operator-directed dispatch and curtailment, and 2) post- 22

contingency actions, made available by automated remedial action schemes (RAS). To preserve 23

transmission security, a load with co-located generation (or paired generation in an electrically- 24

equivalent location) must reduce net grid withdrawals to zero when the grid operator (or an 25

automated RAS) determines that doing so is necessary.20
26

For transmission security events, PJM can determine the need for curtailment through Security 1

Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED), Security Constrained Unit Commitment (SCUC), or 2

other operational planning tools. Because reducing net withdrawals generally requires curtailing 3

high-value load, the efficient approach is to treat that curtailment as a high-cost reliability action 4

and use it only after ordinary economic alternatives, including generator redispatch, have been 5

exhausted. This can be achieved by configuring the load as a dispatchable asset with a high 6

willingness to pay reflected in a bid price at some cap (such as $2,000/MWh, the offer cap for 7

setting price as a generator, or $3,700/MWh, the bid cap for price-sensitive demand in the PJM 8

day-ahead market).21
9
A SCED-based approach would likely set nodal market clearing prices for energy at the bid price 10

during curtailment. A SCUC-based approach may be less direct, especially because it can 11

include other values such as shut-down cost and no-load cost that would not be reflected in 12

prices even after being dispatched to reduce net withdrawals. Either way, the dispatch signal 13

coming from the PJM market engine can serve as the mandatory directive to reduce non-firm 14

load or net withdrawals at a co-location arrangement. The ability to bid at the price cap serves to 15

meet the principle of exhausting economic actions prior to relying on the withdrawal reduction to 16

maintain transmission security, thereby avoiding inefficient curtailment when not strictly needed. 17

This approach has the virtue of using the same mechanism by which co-location arrangements 18

can voluntarily reduce net withdrawals economically in response to market prices. This would be 19

expected when the marginal cost of the co-located generation is lower than market prices, or 20

when the site has been enabled with low-cost load flexibility.
