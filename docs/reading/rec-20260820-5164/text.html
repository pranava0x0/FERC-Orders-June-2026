Ceres’ Comments RE: PJM Governance 
Thank you for convening the recent technical conference to examine governance and 
stakeholder reforms at PJM Interconnection. I submit these comments on behalf of Ceres, 
a nonprofit advocacy organization dedicated to ensuring a sustainable economy. 
Below are our suggestions for reforms to PJM’s governance structures to ensure energy 
reliability, affordability, and sustainability throughout the region:  
1. Reflect affordability and the public interest in PJM’s mission and governing 
documents. Decisions made at PJM impact 67 million ratepayers across 13 states 
and the District of Columbia. At the same time, PJM has no formalized commitment 
to serve the public interest or to consider electric affordability on the same footing 
as electric reliability. If the PJM Board is to have its independence and power 
expanded, this should only happen if steps are taken to meaningfully commit PJM to 
serve the public, and not just its members, such as by explicitly including a public 
interest mandate and affordability in the PJM mission and governing documents. 
 
2. Grant OPSI 205 jump ball filing rights. Currently, the states do not have a strong 
enough voice in PJM’s decision-making process. One way to strengthen the voice of 
the states is to grant the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (OPSI) 205 “jump ball” 
filing rights with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), so that they can 
advance proposals to be considered on even footing with those from PJM. This is 
already the case for other regional transmission organizations (RTOs) and PJM 
should be brought into alignment with this standard. Additionally, 205 filing rights 
for OPSI can serve as an oversight mechanism for a public interest mandate, as the 
states will have a formal route to intervene. 
 
3. Formalize Board consultation with OPSI. Under the status quo, the PJM Board does 
not sufficiently consult OPSI and the states prior to making major filings with FERC. 
Poor communication increases the likelihood that the states and the PJM Board will 
be at odds on proposals, especially those that would increase costs substantially 
for ratepayers. While granting OPSI 205 filing rights provides a backstop, increased 
consultation between the PJM Board and the states - such as getting feedback on 
draft filings - would foster trust, credibility, and avoid litigation. 
 
4. Require cost impact information. To evaluate whether PJM is serving the public 
interest on the basis of affordability, it is necessary for states, advocates, 
stakeholders, and the public to have access to accurate cost impact information.

PJM should analyze all proposals for cost impact and make the results widely and 
publicly available so that interested parties can make informed decisions. Such 
transparency will also serve to improve PJM’s accountability to the public.  
5.  
Appoint an Independent Transmission Monitor. For over 25 years, an Independent 
Market Monitor has provided indispensable oversight of PJM’s power markets, 
performing cost impact analysis and identifying structural problems. Just like the 
energy and capacity markets, transmission planning at PJM has a significant impact 
on ratepayer costs. During a time when transmission costs are consistently rising, 
creating an Independent Transmission Monitor would provide necessary oversight 
of PJM’s approach to planning. 
 
6. Expand the Nominating Committee to include state representatives. If the PJM 
Board is to better engage with the states, it would help to have more formalized 
involvement of the states in the nominating process for Board directors. Currently, 
the Nominating Committee does not consistently include a consumer advocate, 
and the states do not get to appoint representatives. Along with reserving a 
permanent seat for a consumer advocate, the Nominating Committee should also 
allow for one appointee from OPSI and another from a governors’/legislators’ 
organization - as recommended by the Governors’ Collaborative. 
 
7. Expand the Board expertise requirements to include state and ratepayer 
experience. Expertise requirements are an important guardrail to ensure the PJM 
Board has the collective experience and insight necessary to do its job effectively. 
However, the current requirements do not include expertise in state regulatory 
work, consumer advocacy, or clean energy. To have a Board that is equipped to 
understand how their decisions impact electric affordability for ratepayers, it is 
critical to require that at least one Board member brings direct experience from 
each of these areas. Moreover, expanding requirements in this way would better 
equip PJM to adhere to a public interest and affordability mandate in its governing 
documents. 
 
8. Open Board meetings and publish Board votes. Perhaps the most straightforward 
reforms PJM can choose to make would be to open all Board meetings to the public 
and publish the results of individual Board votes. By increasing the transparency of 
Board proceedings, PJM can concretely demonstrate accountability to its mission - 
which should include an explicit public interest and affordability mandate - rather 
than merely asking PJM members and the public to trust the process. Moreover,

with published votes, all interested parties will be equipped to evaluate the 
performance of individual Board directors. 
 
9. Expand sector-weighted voting to all Standing Committees and Senior Task Forces. 
At higher committee levels in PJM the practice of sector-weighted voting helps 
ensure that no individual sector overwhelms the others. However, this voting 
method is not employed in lower level standing committees and senior task forces, 
which leads to outsize influence by supply-side interests and their many affiliate 
members. Applying sector-weighted voting at all committee levels would ensure the 
voice of end-use customers, including representatives of residential ratepayer 
interests, are not drowned out. 
 
10. Require transparency of individual firm votes across all committee levels. In a body 
like PJM, whose decisions ultimately impact the affordability of electricity for 67 
million people, there is no justification for keeping the votes of PJM members under 
wraps. Just as votes by the Board should be made public, votes at all committee 
levels of PJM should be transparent and accessible to oversight. If states are to 
conduct meaningful oversight of the utilities within their jurisdiction, PJM should not 
serve as an obstacle to that function by allowing member votes to remain obscured. 
In the same spirit of transparency, all PJM meetings should be recorded and made 
publicly available. 
 
Sincerely, 
Jeff Mauk 
Director, State Policy, East
