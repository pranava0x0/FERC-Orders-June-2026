I. INTRODUCTION AND INTEREST OF PERSISTENCE ANALYTICS GROUP LLC
Persistence Analytics Group LLC (PAG) respectfully submits these post-technical conference comments in 
response to the Commission's examination of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. governance and stakeholder processes in 
Docket No. AD26-7-000. PAG is an independent infrastructure-risk and decision-assurance firm focused on large-
load integration, demand durability, grid stress, load integrity, transmission cost causation, and implementation 
verification.
The Commission's July 23, 2026 technical conference was directed toward concrete, actionable reforms that can 
improve PJM's ability to address operational and market needs in a timely and efficient manner. The Commission's 
subsequent process also identifies Board independence, section 205 filing rights, more timely and transparent 
stakeholder decision-making, and meaningful structured participation by state entities as major reform themes.
PAG's central recommendation is straightforward: governance reform should include an explicit decision-
assurance layer. The stakeholder process should not end when a proposal receives the votes, approvals, or filings 
necessary to move forward. For decisions with material reliability, transmission, cost-allocation, large-load, or 
capital consequences, the process should identify the assumptions supporting the decision, the evidence supporting 
those assumptions, the conditions requiring revalidation, and the evidence showing whether the expected result was 
ultimately realized.
A stakeholder process can be procedurally complete while the assumptions underneath the 
resulting decision remain unverified.
These comments address governance principles only. PAG does not address the merits of any specific contested 
proceeding pending before the Commission.
II. THE GOVERNANCE PROBLEM IS ALSO AN EVIDENCE PROBLEM
PJM is operating in an environment in which changes in large-load development, generation availability, 
transmission needs, equipment lead times, financing conditions, siting, permitting, and state policy can materially 
change the assumptions supporting major system decisions. The governance challenge is therefore not simply speed. 
A faster process that relies on assumptions that are incomplete, stale, internally inconsistent, or insufficiently tested 
can accelerate the wrong decision.
The inverse is also true. A process that repeatedly relitigates settled questions because participants lack a 
common evidentiary record can delay decisions even when the underlying need is real. Better governance should 
therefore create a disciplined way to distinguish policy disagreement from factual uncertainty, and to determine 
which assumptions require verification before a decision becomes difficult to reverse.
PAG recommends that PJM distinguish among at least four categories of information: (1) announced or forecast 
conditions; (2) contractually supported conditions; (3) independently supportable or executable conditions; and (4) 
realized operating outcomes. These categories are not interchangeable. For example, announced load is not the same 
as durable operating demand, and financial security is not the same as proof that every critical infrastructure 
dependency can be delivered on the required timetable.

III. A PJM ACCOUNTABLE DECISION ARCHITECTURE
PAG recommends that the Commission encourage PJM and its stakeholders to develop an Accountable 
Decision Architecture for material governance decisions. The architecture need not prescribe substantive outcomes. 
Its function is to create a repeatable evidentiary record around consequential decisions.

1. What is being assumed? Identify the material load, timing, reliability, cost, resource, transmission, 
implementation, and market assumptions that make the proposed decision reasonable.
2. What evidence supports each assumption? Separate forecasts, representations, contracts, engineering 
evidence, operating data, and other forms of support so decision-makers can see the quality and limitations of the 
record.
3. Who is affected if an assumption is wrong? Identify states, customers, market participants, transmission 
owners, generators, and other stakeholders that bear material consequences if an assumption fails.
4. What event requires revalidation? Define objective triggers for reconsideration when material facts change, 
including delays, downsizing, cancellation, changes in load, changed infrastructure dependencies, or other 
deviations from the record supporting the decision.
5. Who owns the exception? Assign responsibility for identifying, escalating, correcting, and closing material 
exceptions rather than allowing them to persist across committees or planning cycles.
6. What evidence proves the outcome? After implementation, compare the realized condition against the 
assumptions that justified the decision and make the result available to improve future governance.
IV. FIVE GOVERNANCE REFORMS THE COMMISSION SHOULD ENCOURAGE
1. Material-Assumption Disclosure. For consequential decisions, PJM should identify the material 
assumptions that drive the recommendation before the relevant stakeholder or Board action. The objective is not 
disclosure of every model input. It is a concise decision-level record showing which assumptions are outcome-
determinative and the evidence category supporting each one.
2. Structured State Evidentiary Access. State entities should have a defined path to the evidence underlying 
decisions that materially affect customers in their jurisdictions. Meaningful state participation requires more than 
an opportunity to comment after an allocation or planning conclusion has largely hardened. It requires timely 
access to the assumptions and evidence that drive the decision.
3. Defined Revalidation Triggers. PJM should identify circumstances that require a material assumption to be 
revisited before the next irreversible implementation or capital step. Revalidation should be targeted, not an 
invitation to reopen every prior decision. The trigger should be tied to a change in a fact that was material to the 
original decision.
4. Decision and Exception Ownership. Material assumptions, unresolved exceptions, and corrective actions 
should have identifiable institutional owners. Governance slows when responsibility is diffuse. A transparent 
owner-and-deadline structure can improve accountability without changing voting rights or substantive market 
rules.
5. Post-Decision Outcome Verification. For selected high-consequence decisions, PJM should compare actual 
outcomes with the assumptions that supported approval. The purpose is not retrospective fault-finding. It is 
institutional learning: determine where assumptions were durable, where they failed, and which governance 
controls should change as a result.
V. LARGE-LOAD INTEGRATION SHOWS WHY REVALIDATION MATTERS
Large-load integration illustrates the need for an explicit verification and revalidation discipline because a 
single project may depend on several timelines that do not move together. Customer commitment, utility 
construction, network upgrades, generation, substations, transformers, fuel supply, permitting, financing, cooling, 
and customer occupancy may each be credible individually while remaining inconsistent as a combined execution 
plan.

Financial security and minimum-payment structures can be important tools for allocating financial 
consequences. They do not, by themselves, prove that the underlying load will energize on schedule, persist at the 
assumed level, or create the precise system need attributed to it. Governance should therefore distinguish the 
question of who pays if an assumption fails from the separate question of whether the assumption should be relied 
upon in the first instance.

A governance process that explicitly records the difference between forecast, commitment, executability, and 
realized performance would improve the Commission's and states' ability to understand when a change in large-load 
conditions should affect planning, cost causation, or implementation sequencing. It would also reduce the risk that 
an assumption continues to influence later decisions after the underlying facts have materially changed.
VI. A PRACTICAL IMPLEMENTATION PATH
Phase 1 - Select. Choose a limited set of high-consequence decision types in which material assumptions are 
identifiable and changes in those assumptions can materially affect reliability, cost, or implementation.
Phase 2 - Record. Create a concise decision record listing the material assumptions, evidence category, affected 
stakeholders, accountable owner, and revalidation trigger.
Phase 3 - Revalidate. When a trigger occurs, require a focused update of the affected assumption rather than 
reopening the entire governance record.
Phase 4 - Verify. After implementation, compare actual performance with the assumptions that justified the 
decision and identify any recurring governance failure modes.
Phase 5 - Scale. Use the results to determine which elements should become standard across additional PJM 
stakeholder, planning, market, or Board processes.
This approach is compatible with different views regarding voting structures, Board independence, section 205 
filing authority, sector representation, and state participation. It provides a common evidentiary layer regardless of 
which institutional reforms the Commission and PJM ultimately pursue.
VII. CONCLUSION
The Commission has appropriately focused this proceeding on concrete and actionable reform. PAG 
respectfully submits that durable reform requires more than changing who participates, who votes, or how quickly a 
proposal advances. It also requires a disciplined record of what the decision assumes, what evidence supports those 
assumptions, what changes require revalidation, and whether the expected result was ultimately realized.
A governance process should be able to answer three questions at any consequential 
decision point: What did we assume? What evidence supported it? What happened 
afterward?
PAG therefore recommends that the Commission encourage PJM to incorporate material-assumption disclosure, 
structured state evidentiary access, revalidation triggers, decision and exception ownership, and post-decision 
outcome verification into the governance reforms developed through this docket.
