While broad agreement exists that artificial intelligence (AI) is crucial for US security, that 
consensus papers over key issues for determining how to connect and power the data centers that 
provide such AI services.1 In the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC) Order 
Instituting Proceeding Under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act and Consolidating with Other 
Proceedings (February 20, 2025),2 FERC calls for assessments of “any national security 
implications associated with co-location arrangements,” including options for behind-the-meter 
nuclear power plants that serve data centers.3 This Declaration examines the specific ways in which 
AI data centers are becoming crucial for US defense in peacetime and war, the implications for 
co-located power, and broader security requirements for FERC to consider in its proceedings.  
 
In the first round of filings, most of the PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) Transmission Owners 
argued that all load needs to be interconnected to the utility-owned transmission grid, despite the 
fact that in some areas in PJM there are lengthy delays and even moratoriums on new large load 
utility interconnections. PJM, for its part, acknowledged its preference for a connection to the 
transmission grid that it operates, but also identified a number of additional options that could be 
explored for connecting large loads such as those used to power AI services for the Department of 
Defense (DOD). It appears that a number of these options may offer advantages in terms of the 
speed with which such plants can meet surging data center needs, and the resilience of the power 
supply certain configurations may provide over traditional interconnections.  
 
This Declaration finds that given the extensive and accelerating dependence of US security on AI 
data centers, FERC must ensure that rules enable the quickest and most resilient options to connect 
and serve those data center loads, including rules that enable co-location arrangements, or other 
options to ensure a prompt connection to the transmission system, if that is what the load desires. 
The entities that own or operate the AI facilities should be given the choice to determine which 
configuration for interconnecting them is best for their needs, in close consultation with DOD and 
the other national security agencies that depend on their AI services. Neither FERC, PJM nor 
individual transmission owners should make that choice for them.

I. 
ARGUMENT SUMMARY 
 
DOD relies on AI to help execute an immense, and rapidly growing, range of military missions. 
AI services provided by private sector data centers are crucial for meeting DOD’s requirements. 
Ensuring that the energy sector can keep pace with that growth, and connect new data centers 
quickly and provide the power that data centers need to support America’s defense, is especially 
important as China competes for primacy in AI. Co-locating existing power plants with data 
centers can often provide such connections and power years faster than interconnecting data 
centers for grid-provided service. That speed advantage should constitute a key factor in assessing 
the national security implications of co-location.  
 
The resilience of power for data centers against enemy attack offers an additional security 
consideration, and one that has thus far been overlooked in proceedings on co-location. DOD uses 
AI every day to train autonomous drones and conduct other peacetime activities. But if a conflict 
breaks out in the Taiwan Strait or other region, DOD will rely on AI to support essential combat-
related functions, including wartime intelligence analysis and the command, control, and 
coordination of US forces. 
 
The distinction between data center defense support in peacetime versus war has profound 
implications for meeting their power requirements. In peacetime, any quick and reliable way to 
meet growing data center loads will do. In conflict, the US Intelligence Community assesses that 
China could conduct cyberattacks and potentially physical attacks against the US energy sector 
and other critical infrastructure systems.4 Given DOD’s dependence on AI data centers for combat 
support, those centers must have power supplies that are resilient against attacks by China or other 
potential adversaries.   
 
FERC, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC), and their industry partners 
have greatly strengthened the cyber and physical security of the Bulk Power System (BPS). Those 
efforts improve the resilience of grid-provided power for data centers. They also bolster the 
resilience of service from co-located nuclear plants, by mitigating threats to the transmission lines 
those plants need for safe operations. Especially valuable: BPS critical infrastructure protection 
(CIP) standards make it far more difficult for China to create interconnection-wide blackouts than 
would otherwise be the case.

In both storms, severe cold disrupted supplies of natural gas for power generation, requiring

power precisely when DOD and the Nation need them the most.  
 
II. 
AI AND THE TRANSFORMATION OF NATIONAL DEFENSE 
 
Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth emphasizes that AI capabilities are disruptive and vital to 
DOD’s success in the future, and is prioritizing its use for monitoring cyberspace, countering 
transnational criminal organizations, testing and evaluating weapons, and other critical missions 
and support functions.7 Under the Department’s Data, Analytics, and Artificial Intelligence 
Adoption Strategy, the range of such AI applications is rapidly expanding. The strategy calls for 
an extensive range of AI applications to “enhance the warfighting capabilities of the Joint Force,” 
across the continuum “from the boardroom to the battlefield.”8  
 
The scope and accelerating pace of DOD’s uses of AI are astonishing and little recognized beyond 
the Defense community – except, of course, by the private sector data center owners/operators 
who provide AI services to the military, including for highly classified missions. Meeting the 
power requirements for these data centers is crucial so that DOD can 1) train autonomous vehicles 
and conduct other AI functions in peacetime; 2) help DOD prevail during future conflicts; and 3) 
counter China’s drive for AI supremacy and its use of AI to help achieve military superiority over 
the United States.

A. Peacetime Defense Support 
 
AI is now crucial for DOD efforts to help America prevail in future conflicts – and, even better, 
deter Chinese and other attacks on the US and its security partners in Asia and beyond. Prime 
examples of AI applications with significant power requirements include:  
 
Autonomous system training. AI-controlled drones are not born smart. Their ability to dogfight 
and perform other missions depends on using extensive data sets (comprised of real world and 
simulated scenarios) to teach AI algorithms to autonomously navigate, identify targets, make 
tactical decisions, and react to adversary aircraft behavior. DOD and its industry partners 
continuously update these data sets to account for results of autonomous flights, evolving 
assessments of “wingman” requirements to support manned aircraft, and threat data.9 Massive AI 
training activities will also be required for the many thousands of air, sea and land-based 
autonomous vehicles that DOD will soon be deploying.10 
 
Maintenance. At the opposite end of the glamour spectrum lies maintenance – mundane yet 
absolutely vital to keep drones and all other US weapons and platforms ready for war. Data center 
owners and operators are providing AI services to the armed forces to enable predictive 
maintenance. In particular, these solutions use AI algorithms and data analytics tools to monitor 
operations, detect anomalies, and predict possible defects or breakdowns in equipment before they 
happen.11

Defense Manufacturing and the Defense Industrial Base. The examples of peacetime AI 
applications highlighted above primarily focus on DOD activities. However, the DOD makes very 
few of its own weapons. It depends on the defense industrial base (DIB) to manufacture products 
for national defense. With DOD’s support, defense companies are rapidly adopting AI tools to 
design, develop, and produce the next generation of US weaponry. Especially important, the DOD 
Office of Industrial Base Resilience is driving the integration of AI into defense systems, and is 
collaborating with the DIB to rapidly adopt AI for defense applications and maintain the Nation’s 
tactical advantage.12 The DIB’s adoption of AI drastically increases the total defense-related uses 
of data centers. More than 100,000 DIB companies and their subcontractors perform under contract 
to the DOD.13 
 
Defense Intelligence. Project Maven exemplifies the emerging value of AI for intelligence 
applications. Maven provides image-processing AI capabilities that have become some of the 
military’s most in-demand tools for intelligence and targeting. In US Central Command alone, 
13,000 Maven accounts are already in frequent use. The National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency 
(NGA) now plans to expand its total user base ten-fold. The problem, according to NGA director 
Vice Admiral Frank Whitworth: the explosive growth in computer requirements to operate 
intelligence-related AI functions.14 Of course, these computational requirements (and the 
electricity loads they require) are greater still if we include the broader intelligence community 
essential for national security, including the booming uses of AI by the Central Intelligence 
Agency.15

Command and Control. Autonomous vehicles again serve as the poster child for AI. The US Air 
Force is planning to deploy a fleet of more than 1,000 unmanned warplanes, the first of them 
operating by 2028.16 With thousands more Navy, Army and Marine autonomous vehicles 
operating in all warfare domains, managing the integrated operation of all of these assets against 
enemy forces (themselves AI-enabled) will be immensely difficult.17 DOD’s Combined Joint All-
Domain Command & Control (CJADC2) initiative is structured to meet this challenge.  With the 
help of AI, the program was originally designed to produce “the warfighting capability to sense, 
make sense, and act at all levels and phases of war, across all domains, and with partners, to deliver 
information advantage at the speed of relevance.”18  
 
Decision Support. DOD’s AI strategy calls for the development of additional ways to achieve 
“decision advantage” over enemy commanders, via 1) battlespace awareness and understanding; 
2) adaptive force planning and application; 3) fast, precise, and resilient attack structures; and 4) 
other applications of AI to combat and combat support functions.19  
 
The advantages that AI offers for decision support is driving the rapid expansion of their use. A 
comprehensive analysis of these services found that “artificial intelligence promises to help 
military commanders make sense of vast amounts of data at superhuman speeds. The military has 
a strong motivation to take advantage of AI, and among the most interested within the armed forces 
are commanders charged with making operational decisions in war. These commanders must 
continuously ‘observe, orient, decide, and act’ on a fast-paced and multidimensional battlefield 
where decisions are life-and-death.”20 To meet these needs, Palantir, Scale AI, Anduril, and other 
AI companies and their associated commercial data centers are rapidly scaling up their decision 
support and situational awareness services to DOD.21

D. The Need for Speed 
 
DOD’s growing reliance on AI in peacetime and war, combined with China’s efforts to overtake 
US progress, provide compelling reasons for FERC to facilitate the rapid connection of data 
centers and to continue its efforts to ensure power sources are incented and rapidly deployed. Speed 
is of the essence for national security.    
 
President Trump has pointed the way to a promising option to achieve such speed: have data 
centers rely on power generators sitting “right next” to them.36 The President noted that co-located 
data center projects can go forward on a “very rapid basis” compared to other options.37 That speed 
offers a significant advantage over grid-connected alternatives for data center power, which can 
take as many as 7 years or longer to build the necessary transmission infrastructure and meet other 
project requirements.38 Some transmission owners argue that their preferred grid-connection 
options should be the only connection option available.39 However, limiting data center 
development to only grid-connected options may simply take too long to implement in many

private sector facilities. It would be mistaken to imagine that because AI services are so critical to 
national defense, DOD relies entirely on its own data centers sited on military bases or other 
defense installations, and that somehow the DOD powers these centers with its own generation 
resources. The opposite is true. Both DOD-owned data centers and their private sector counterparts 
that provide AI service to the DOD overwhelmingly depend on grid-provided power, co-located 
nuclear plants, or other commercial solutions. Moreover, DOD’s utilization of private sector data 
centers is rapidly growing, including highly classified AI support functions.40 Rules to facilitate 
the rapid connection of data centers must enable options that meet all such urgent requirements for 
power.  
 
III. 
POWER RESILIENCE 
 
In addition to accelerating the availability of expedient data center connection options given its 
importance to America’s defense, rules on data center connections should also enable DOD and 
its data center partners to benefit from the increased resilience that locating at or near nuclear 
plants can provide in future conflicts. 
 
Reliable electric service is necessary for day-to-day operations of data centers in peacetime. 
However, given the importance of data centers to support US warfighting, and the risk that China 
will attack the grid and natural gas infrastructure in future conflicts, connections to data centers 
must also be resilient: that is, be able to “prepare for and adapt to changing conditions and 
recover rapidly from operational disruptions,” including from “deliberate attacks, accidents, or 
naturally occurring threats or incidents.”41

A. Intensifying Threats and US Resilience Initiatives 
 
The March 2025 Annual Threat Assessment of the US Intelligence Community states that “China 
has demonstrated the ability to compromise U.S. infrastructure through formidable cyber 
capabilities that it could employ during a conflict with the United States.”42 In particular, “if 
Beijing believed that a major conflict with Washington was imminent, it could consider aggressive 
cyber operations against U.S. critical infrastructure and military assets. Such strikes would be 
designed to deter U.S. military action by impeding U.S. decision-making, inducing societal panic, 
and interfering with the deployment of U.S. forces.”43 
 
This US Intelligence Community assessment provides additional context for responding to 
FERC’s February 2025 request for analysis of the national security implications associated with 
co-location arrangements.44 As noted in Section II.B above, AI data center services play a vital 
and rapidly growing role in supporting US combat decision-making, command and control, and 
other missions essential for deploying and operating US forces. Given the US Intelligence 
Community’s assessment of Chinese conflict objectives, strengthening the resilience of power 
flows to data centers is crucial to help counter those goals and reinforce deterrence against Chinese 
attacks.  
 
Ongoing improvements to the cybersecurity of the BPS help strengthen that resilience. FERC, 
NERC, and BPS entities deserve enormous credit for developing and implementing CIP standards 
that strengthen the grid’s resilience against cyber and physical threats. These ongoing security 
improvements to the BPS strengthen the resilience of grid-provided power for data centers. Those 
improvements also benefit co-located nuclear plants by mitigating threats to the transmission lines 
that plants need to safely operate.  
 
Natural gas pipeline systems and other gas infrastructure owners and operators are also taking 
significant, urgently needed steps to strengthen the cyber resilience of their systems. The US 
Transportation Security Administration (TSA) has issued a series of performance-based 
cybersecurity mandates for these systems, including that owners/operators establish and 
implement TSA-approved cybersecurity implementation and incident response plans.45 On a

Accordingly, from a national security perspective, it makes sense for FERC rules to enable data 
center operators to choose from a diverse set of resilient power options. Some operators (and their 
DOD customers) may decide that grid-connected power best meets their needs. Others may want 
to prioritize resilience against attacks on natural gas systems designed to disrupt the grid, and 
decide instead to rely on co-located or proximate generation. The section that follows highlights 
the potential advantages of co-location.  
 
B. Uri, Elliott, and Power Resilience for Defense-Critical Data Centers 
 
Extreme weather events provide “real world” examples of how disruptions of natural gas supplies 
for generation can degrade electric service, even when transmission systems are largely unaffected. 
Winter Storms Elliott and Uri offer prime examples. In both storms, severe cold disrupted supplies 
of natural gas for power generation. The resulting generation outages, de-rates, and failures to start 
required grid operators to conduct large scale load shedding operations to maintain the grid’s 
reliability – including shedding firm load.49

Moreover, even as severe cold disrupted gas generation and electric service to key customers, 
nuclear power plants kept providing much-needed power. During Winter Storm Elliott, 31 nuclear 
reactors in operation across PJM’s service territory produced more than 96 percent of their 
potential power output during the storm.50 A key enabler: unlike gas generators, nuclear power

by severe weather or adversary attacks.  
 
Of course, the ability of nuclear plants to sustain their operations in severe weather events depends 
on the continued availability of transmission system ties to enable their safe operation. The same 
will be true in future conflicts. It is prudent to assume that, to disrupt US military operations, China 
will attack both natural gas infrastructure and the BPS on which data centers and other defense-
critical assets depend. Thanks to the security initiatives taken by FERC and its partners, US 
transmission systems may be able to keep operating in the face of such attacks, just as they did in 
Uri and Elliott. That survivability could help nuclear power plants sustain service to co-located 
data centers even if large scale load-shedding is underway in the surrounding grid. And of course, 
the resilience of nuclear-provided power is further enhanced by their compliance with the 
stringent, continuously upgraded cyber and physical security standards established by the US 
Nuclear Regulatory Commission.51  
 
IV. 
CONCLUSION: ALTERNATIVE PATHS TO POWER RESILIENCE 
 
Not all data center owners and operators will assess their needs for resilient power in the same 
way. And over time, new technologies may become available to provide resilient power beyond 
those that exist today, including the deployment of small modular reactors. As FERC assesses the 
security considerations involving co-location arrangements it should consider the following: 
 
• Data center owners and operators should have the latitude to select power sources and 
interconnection configurations that best meet the requirements of their particular 
applications, including power resilience for military and other mission-critical use cases. 
One size will not fit all. Rules affecting large load interconnections should preserve that 
latitude.  
 
• That said, accelerating the pace with which new sources of power can meet data center 
loads will remain absolutely vital to national security. The advantages of alternatives to 
traditional utility grid connections in this regard are compelling, and should be weighted 
accordingly by FERC as it moves forward in this proceeding.

initiatives on continuity planning and antiterrorism/force protection.

recovery criteria in Resilience for Black Sky Days (2014). His recent studies on the electric system
