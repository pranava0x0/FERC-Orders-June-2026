BEFORE THE FEDERAL ENERGY REGULATORY COMMISSION
PJM GOVERNANCE AND STAKEHOLDER REFORMS

Docket No. AD26-7-000
Opening Statement of Abraham Silverman
I appreciate the invitation to speak to the Commission.  My name is Abe Silverman, and I am a research scholar at Johns Hopkins University and principal of SilverGreen Energy Consulting, where I write on data centers, interconnection, interregional transmission planning, supply chain constraints, and PJM issues, including governance.  I recently authored a working paper on “ISO-RTO Governance in the 21st Century,” which goes into these matters in more detail.

Introduction:
The last 12 months have exposed the structural limitations in PJM’s existing governance and stakeholder processes, particularly as the speed and size of large load growth overwhelmed the ability of PJM and its stakeholders to respond.  The result has been a fractured response that has increased consumer costs and reduced reliability, while, paradoxically, creating a challenging environment for investors.

PJM’s inability to successfully navigate the large-load integration process, along with its inability to clear the interconnection backlog or conduct auctions on a predictable schedule and with consistent market rules, has put the organization at risk of losing the confidence of political actors in the PJM region.  The loss of political support leads directly to reduced investor confidence, as investors recognize that the market structure they rely on to send price signals is at risk of disintegration.

The result is that suppliers are increasingly demanding long-term contracts with significant risk premiums, even as the supply-demand fundamentals have never looked better for merchant investment in PJM, even considering higher capital costs for new generation.  The erosion in political confidence also opens the door to calls to move back to re-regulation and an increased role for monopoly utilities, which would be a disaster for PJM customers on par with the cost overruns and inefficiencies of 1980s markets that gave rise to restructuring in the first place.

Recommendations:
To begin the process of restoring political (and therefore investor) confidence in PJM markets, state actors – including public utility commissions, governors’ offices and state legislators – need not only to be given a seat at the table, but also to be treated as full partners in solving the complex challenges facing PJM today.  Successfully doing so means

1) Immediately Form, Fund, and Fully Empower a State-led “States Committee”. PJM is a significant outlier in the role it provides its state partners.  Bringing PJM state into parity with states in other regional markets should be the starting point for any reform efforts.  An immediate “no-regrets” approach would be for the PJM Board of Managers to invite states to form a “PJM States Committee” to comprehensively represent the interests of states.

The New England States Committee On Electricity (“NESCOE”) group provides an excellent model for how to do this, even in a complicated multi-jurisdictional environment where states are not always unified in their policy vision.3  The PJM region would benefit enormously from its own states developing a document modeled off of NESCOE’s 2020 “New England States’ Vision for a Clean, Affordable, and Reliable 21st Century Regional Electric Grid,”4 which laid out a comprehensive vision for the ISO New England grid. In addition to forming the organization, PJM also needs to fund and fully empower it.

• In terms of funding, NESCOE’s 2026 budget is $2.7 million for six states with ~1/6 of the peak load of PJM.  Costs are collected as part of ISO New England’s network transmission charges.  NESCOE’s five employees and consulting budget allows the states to access data, conduct modeling and interpret the outcomes.

• Fully empowering the PJM States Committee means giving the new organization co- filing rights over, at minimum, the resource adequacy portions of the PJM tariff, direct involvement in selecting the PJM Board of Managers, and a mandatory consultation relationship with the Board and PJM Management on all topics not covered by filing rights.

• Representatives of the new organization must also be given equivalent participatory rights, without requiring sector membership or payment of duplicative membership

A key benefit of a States Committee-type structure is that it brings both political and regulatory actors to the stakeholder table, which is necessary if we are to restore state confidence in PJM.  The appropriate role is for public service commissions to keep participating in the day-to-day stakeholder discussions, and to provide Governors the opportunity to create a more policy-oriented body to sit alongside the PJM Board of Managers to guide PJM’s strategic direction.

My observation is that PJM significantly underappreciates the different roles of governor’s offices, legislative bodies and state public service commissions.  State commissions – represented by the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”) – contribute technical and ratemaking expertise, while governors supply long-term vision, political accountability and authority to resolve cross-cutting policy tradeoffs.  State statutes typically designate public service commissions as quasi-judicial actors, making policy development driven by state commissions evolutionary, rather than revolutionary.  And as creatures of statute, state commissions often need legislative guidance on how to deal with fast-evolving issues, such as the AI revolution.  Governors and legislators, however, can act more quickly, and are the appropriate entities to chart a holistic state response to key PJM decisions. Finally, it is critical to note that a PJM States Committee would be a complement to, and not a replacement of, OPSI.  I would expect OPSI to continue to be a vibrant participant in PJM policy.  But for PJM to find its way out of the wilderness, it needs to increase buy-in from all sides of state government by means of a more comprehensive representation of states in the PJM decision-making processes.

2) Incorporate a Commitment to Affordability, the Public Interest, and Achievement of State Policy into PJM’s Foundational Documents.

Long-time FERC observers have noted that ISOs and RTOs were initially imagined as mini- FERCs that would independently call balls and strikes, informed by the “just and reasonable” requirements of the Federal Power Act.  Today’s PJM, however, tends to view its role largely as representing the interests of its members – not the public interest. This broken relationship traces back to PJM’s governing documents, which do not include an affirmative obligation to consider the public interest, affordability, or support for state policies.  As a Delaware limited liability corporation, PJM has explained that its Board of Managers owe their fiduciary duty to the organization, rather than to the public interest. Thus, the “scoring rubric” that PJM and its members use to judge their success does not

Again, PJM is out of step with the national consensus on the proper role of an ISO Board.5 There is no better illustration of PJM’s organizational misalignment with states that the issue of large load growth.  There is a startling bipartisan, national, consensus among governors and other elected officials that PJM must act promptly to prevent cost shifting associated with large load interconnection.  Politically-oriented policy makers6 have been exceptionally clear about the need for PJM action in addressing what they characterize as a data center-driven affordability crisis.  PJM, however, has been reluctant to engage in affordability measures, instead focusing on system reliability (including adding billions in Regional Transmission Plan spending to serve new large loads) and incremental changes to its market design.7

To fix this lack of alignment between PJM’s internal rubric and state interests, we need to change PJM’s corporate governance documents.  I would recommend three concrete changes:

(1) explicitly incorporate affordability and service to the public interest as core priorities in PJM’s foundational documents;

(2) clarify that the Board of Managers owes its fiduciary duty to the public interest, rather than PJM or its members; and

(3) incorporate support for state policy achievement into its governing objectives. PJM often deems affordability as primarily the province of state regulators.8  With no formal voice, states are dependent on the organizational priorities set by PJM’s voting members and the Board of Managers.  This leaves states with the impossible mission to adapt their energy laws and policies without clear wholesale market rules.  This process is less efficient and less cost-effective for customers a more effective process would incorporate state policy upfront.

I note that acknowledging affordability as a key PJM metric does not mean sacrificing economic principles or bending to ever-shifting political winds in a way that prioritizes short-term rate cuts over long-term customer interests.  Integrating state priorities into PJM’s decision-making process aligns investor expectations with politically supportable cost-reliability tradeoffs, resulting in stronger, more durable, market outcomes.  Utilities are voluntary participants in the ISO/RTO markets and continuation of regional markets should not be assumed – particularly if PJM continues to ignore the customer affordability issues core to durable regional market structures.  Without affordability, continued state political and regulatory support for competitive markets evaporates.

3) Embrace a “Cooperative Federalism” Approach to Solving the Tough Issues Facing PJM.

PJM should be working directly with state leaders to address key policy challenges at the intersection of traditional wholesale-retail boundaries, such as resource adequacy and data center load growth.  If there was ever a time when federal and state regulators needed to sing from the same hymnal, it is today, as data center load growth threatens to shatter our existing electricity market structures.

This is not a new idea.  The 1935 Federal Power Act explicitly recognized the need for shared federal-state jurisdiction over the energy sector and this, at times uneasy, relationship between federal and state regulation has existed since the first days of the electric industry.10

Section 209 of the Federal Power Act provides a formal process for the Commission, state commissions, and affected public utilities (including PJM) to work through tough issues at the edge of federal-state jurisdiction.11  But of course, nothing stops PJM from initiating similar action, outside of Section 209.
