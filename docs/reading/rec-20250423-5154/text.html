COMMENTS OF BUCKEYE POWER, INC.
Pursuant to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or “Commission”) February 20, 2025 Order establishing this show cause proceeding under section 206 of the Federal Power Act,1 Buckeye Power, Inc. (“Buckeye”) respectfully files these comments in support of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) response to the show cause order.2

I. BACKGROUND
New large loads, primarily comprised of data centers, are creating a strain on the electric grid nationwide, and particularly in the PJM region, putting front and center issues of reliability,

resource adequacy, and just and reasonable rates.  Buckeye supports co-location of large loads that are visible to the network.  This includes generation arrangements where the co-located load pays for its share of transmission costs and ensures the grid can continue to rely on existing generation resources.  Buckeye supports PJM’s position that the existing Open Access Transmission Tariff, the Amended and Restated Operating Agreement of PJM, and the Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities in the PJM Region (collectively, the “Tariff”) remains just and reasonable.  Buckeye also supports the additional options PJM has proposed to facilitate front-of-the-meter co-location arrangements where the co-located load is Network Load and resource adequacy is maintained.

While front-of-the-meter, Network Load co-location arrangements are a better alternative for reliability and resource adequacy than non-Network Load behind-the-generator arrangements, even these front-of-the-meter, Network Load arrangements do not resolve all of the significant concerns with the influx of large loads in the PJM footprint.  The risk of significant stranded transmission investment is another critical issue that must be considered, regardless of whether the generation is co-located with load or not.  If the demand of large loads is as expected, that could benefit existing customers by lowering transmission rates.  But there is serious concern that these large loads could drive significant transmission investment and then not show up to pay for it on a sustained basis, shifting costs to everyone else that relies on the expanded grid.  Buckeye supports policies that ensure large loads pay for the costs and impact to the grid they cause.

III. COMMENTS
A. Large Load Co-located with Generation Should be Network Load in Front of the Meter Buckeye supports co-location arrangements where the large loads are Network Load and in “front of the meter” because these arrangements will ensure visibility to the grid operator, allow PJM to conduct appropriate planning to ensure grid reliability, ensure the load pays for its share of grid costs, and ensure generation resources remain available for the entire grid.  Conversely, co- located large load arrangements where the load is located “behind the meter” of the generator, and is not Network Load, may create significant impacts to the grid, increase costs, and decrease reliability for everyone else that relies on the grid for affordable and reliable electricity.  While certain parties have advocated that these so-called “fully isolated” arrangements will avoid

PDF page 6

- 6 -
transmission investments, those potential cost savings are outweighed by significant concerns about the impacts to reliability, resource adequacy, and just and reasonable rates.5 Front-of-the-meter arrangements where the load is Network Load avoids many of the concerns associated with “behind-the-generator” co-location arrangements that are not Network Load:

Improved Grid Reliability.  When load is located in front of the generation meter rather than behind the generation meter, the generation resource is available for full grid operation, rather than having all or a portion of the generator’s output dedicated to a specific load.  This arrangement provides significant reliability benefits by avoiding reliance on protective relays to operate (which can fail) in the event of a generator outage, provides enhanced ability for PJM to manage which load to curtail and prioritize in response to emergency conditions, and enhances PJM’s visibility and control over the co-located load.  As a result, front-of-the-meter, Network Load arrangements will improve system planning and operation and will have less negative impacts on resource adequacy (particularly if the arrangement is consistent with PJM Options 6, 7, and/or 8).6 In contrast, so-called “fully isolated” co-location arrangements would essentially remove the associated generation (assuming it is existing generation) off the grid so that it can no longer be relied upon by everyone else.  This is particularly problematic because co-location arrangements often seek to use large baseload generation that is needed for grid reliability. Although the ultimate balance of demand and supply is arguably the same whether the load is co- located with the generation and behind the transmission meter, rather than in front of the

Removing generation that is associated with a “fully-isolated” co-location arrangement (i.e., a behind-the-generator, non-Network Load arrangement) from the rest of the grid is inconsistent with the grid’s planning and operational foundations and thus threatens to alter flows in a manner that could require additional transmission upgrades and impair the operational flexibility that is essential to reliability.  Finally, the abrupt removal of large baseload resources from the system may have additional impacts to grid operation and costs that are not immediately apparent.

Fair Cost Allocation.  Front-of-the meter arrangements also ensure that the co-located load will pay its fair share of transmission costs as Network Load.  Under behind-the-generator arrangements, co-located load will not be subject to any transmission costs, even though the load will be both causing costs and receiving the benefits of the grid (including, at a minimum, ancillary services and black start capabilities).  Indeed, there seems to be no question that these loads will create costs and will receive benefits from the grid, but as non-Network Load will pay for neither. Avoids Conflicts with State Law. C. Transmission Cost Impacts of Large Load
The Commission has raised concerns with issues of transmission cost allocation and
forecasting relating to co-location arrangements.15  PJM recognizes in its response that inaccurate forecasting can result in overbuilt transmission infrastructure, and that this risk is true with respect to all large load additions—not just those co-located with generation.16  PJM notes that for both transmission and capacity costs, “[t]o the extent the forecasted load additions do not actually materialize, these higher capacity [and transmission] costs would be allocated to all other load on the system.”17  Buckeye agrees that forecasting and cost allocation issues associated with large loads are a significant concern and one that the Commission should address, regardless of whether that load is co-located with generation or not.

To address these cost-shifting concerns caused by new large loads, the Commission should consider the following solutions, as more fully addressed in Buckeye’s Comments filed on December 9, 2024 in Docket AD24-11:

LSE Responsibility.  The Commission should consider requiring the LSE that is driving the transmission investment (i.e., the LSE in whose territory the retail large load is locating) to be responsible for any cost-shifting caused by large loads that do not show up as expected, thereby covering the costs of transmission investments caused by their retail load.  The LSE with the large new load should be the entity that receives this price signal, because it has a retail relationship with the large load that creates the potential for stranded transmission investment and is therefore able to pass the stranded cost responsibility to that retail load.  For instance, the LSE could enter into retail-level agreements with the large loads driving such transmission investments that address cost responsibility in the event that load does not materialize as anticipated (e.g., through a minimum demand charge, and minimum contract term, backed by appropriate credit support requirements). Importantly, any costs collected under such retail arrangements should be credited to the wholesale transmission revenue requirement to ensure that any stranded transmission costs associated with such large loads are not shifted to other LSEs in the zone.23
