COMMENTS OF BUCKEYE POWER, INC.
Pursuant to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or “Commission”) 
February 20, 2025 Order establishing this show cause proceeding under section 206 of the Federal 
Power Act,1 Buckeye Power, Inc. (“Buckeye”) respectfully files these comments in support of 
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) response to the show cause order.2   
I.
BACKGROUND
New large loads, primarily comprised of data centers, are creating a strain on the electric 
grid nationwide, and particularly in the PJM region, putting front and center issues of reliability,

resource adequacy, and just and reasonable rates.  Buckeye supports co-location of large loads that 
are visible to the network.  This includes generation arrangements where the co-located load pays 
for its share of transmission costs and ensures the grid can continue to rely on existing generation 
resources.  Buckeye supports PJM’s position that the existing Open Access Transmission Tariff, 
the Amended and Restated Operating Agreement of PJM, and the Reliability Assurance 
Agreement Among Load Serving Entities in the PJM Region (collectively, the “Tariff”) remains 
just and reasonable.  Buckeye also supports the additional options PJM has proposed to facilitate 
front-of-the-meter co-location arrangements where the co-located load is Network Load and 
resource adequacy is maintained.  
While front-of-the-meter, Network Load co-location arrangements are a better alternative 
for reliability and resource adequacy than non-Network Load behind-the-generator arrangements, 
even these front-of-the-meter, Network Load arrangements do not resolve all of the significant 
concerns with the influx of large loads in the PJM footprint.  The risk of significant stranded 
transmission investment is another critical issue that must be considered, regardless of whether the 
generation is co-located with load or not.  If the demand of large loads is as expected, that could 
benefit existing customers by lowering transmission rates.  But there is serious concern that these 
large loads could drive significant transmission investment and then not show up to pay for it on a 
sustained basis, shifting costs to everyone else that relies on the expanded grid.  Buckeye supports 
policies that ensure large loads pay for the costs and impact to the grid they cause.

COMMENTS
A. Large Load Co-located with Generation Should be Network Load in Front of the Meter
Buckeye supports co-location arrangements where the large loads are Network Load and 
in “front of the meter” because these arrangements will ensure visibility to the grid operator, allow 
PJM to conduct appropriate planning to ensure grid reliability, ensure the load pays for its share 
of grid costs, and ensure generation resources remain available for the entire grid.  Conversely, co-
located large load arrangements where the load is located “behind the meter” of the generator, and 
is not Network Load, may create significant impacts to the grid, increase costs, and decrease 
reliability for everyone else that relies on the grid for affordable and reliable electricity.  While 
certain parties have advocated that these so-called “fully isolated” arrangements will avoid

PDF page 6

- 6 -
transmission investments, those potential cost savings are outweighed by significant concerns 
about the impacts to reliability, resource adequacy, and just and reasonable rates.5   
Front-of-the-meter arrangements where the load is Network Load avoids many of the 
concerns associated with “behind-the-generator” co-location arrangements that are not Network 
Load:  
Improved Grid Reliability.  When load is located in front of the generation meter rather 
than behind the generation meter, the generation resource is available for full grid operation, rather 
than having all or a portion of the generator’s output dedicated to a specific load.  This arrangement 
provides significant reliability benefits by avoiding reliance on protective relays to operate (which 
can fail) in the event of a generator outage, provides enhanced ability for PJM to manage which 
load to curtail and prioritize in response to emergency conditions, and enhances PJM’s visibility 
and control over the co-located load.  As a result, front-of-the-meter, Network Load arrangements 
will improve system planning and operation and will have less negative impacts on resource 
adequacy (particularly if the arrangement is consistent with PJM Options 6, 7, and/or 8).6  
In contrast, so-called “fully isolated” co-location arrangements would essentially remove 
the associated generation (assuming it is existing generation) off the grid so that it can no longer 
be relied upon by everyone else.  This is particularly problematic because co-location 
arrangements often seek to use large baseload generation that is needed for grid reliability.  
Although the ultimate balance of demand and supply is arguably the same whether the load is co-
located with the generation and behind the transmission meter, rather than in front of the

transmission meter, a behind the transmission meter, non-Network Load co-location arrangement 
has the potential to accelerate the pace with which the incremental demand arrives on the system, 
potentially without sufficient, if any, additional incremental supply—exacerbating resource 
adequacy concerns.7  In addition, the existing transmission system was (and continues to be) 
planned and built based on studies that consider the locations of loads and generation across the 
system.  Removing generation that is associated with a “fully-isolated” co-location arrangement 
(i.e., a behind-the-generator, non-Network Load arrangement) from the rest of the grid is 
inconsistent with the grid’s planning and operational foundations and thus threatens to alter flows 
in a manner that could require additional transmission upgrades and impair the operational 
flexibility that is essential to reliability.  Finally, the abrupt removal of large baseload resources 
from the system may have additional impacts to grid operation and costs that are not immediately 
apparent.  
Fair Cost Allocation.  Front-of-the meter arrangements also ensure that the co-located load 
will pay its fair share of transmission costs as Network Load.  Under behind-the-generator 
arrangements, co-located load will not be subject to any transmission costs, even though the load 
will be both causing costs and receiving the benefits of the grid (including, at a minimum, ancillary 
services and black start capabilities).  Indeed, there seems to be no question that these loads will 
create costs and will receive benefits from the grid, but as non-Network Load will pay for neither. 
Avoids Conflicts with State Law.  As PJM noted in its answer, non-Network Load, “behind-
the-generator” co-location arrangements may not be permissible in all states within the PJM 
footprint based on whether the state is deregulated for generation supply and the state’s laws on

requiring the franchised utility to be the sole provider of generation supply to the load.8  The front-
of-the-meter, Network Load arrangements currently permitted by the Tariff and as proposed in 
PJM’s preferred options respect those state laws while providing for consistent application of the 
Tariff across the PJM footprint.  To the extent a patchwork approach were adopted, with different 
arrangements possible in certain states but not others, that would only add further complexity and 
challenges to the important reliability, system planning, and rate issues discussed above.  
Ultimately, the advantages of front-of-the-meter, Network Load arrangements far 
outweigh these numerous concerns with behind-the-generator, non-Network Load arrangements.  
Moreover, the alleged potential benefits of the non-Network Load, behind-the-generator 
arrangements rely largely on the fiction that the arrangement is “fully isolated” from the grid.  As 
noted by PJM’s answer, this is not accurate from either an engineering or economic standpoint 
because the large co-located load receives services from the grid (e.g., ancillary services), but does 
not pay for it, and because the “full isolation” is dependent on a protection system that could fail 
(and has in some circumstances) dumping significant load onto the grid with little notice.9  If these 
large loads have the potential to appear on the system, even for a short time, this could have a 
significant impact on the grid.

Buckeye agrees with PJM that the existing Tariff is just and reasonable because it “permits 
co-located loads to interconnect through existing load interconnection processes,” and the fact that 
“certain entities may prefer, for their own business reasons, alternative pathways to co-location,”

does not render the existing Tariff invalid.10  Accordingly, Options 1 and 2 described by PJM are 
just and reasonable and currently accommodated by the existing Tariff.11 
Buckeye supports additional flexibility for large load co-location as spelled out in Options 
6, 7, and 8, as each of these options provide flexibility to the load while supporting resource 
adequacy.  
•
Option 6: supports co-located load that elects to be Network Load and bring its own 
generation, whether that is new generation or generation expecting to be retired and 
provides incentives for faster interconnection processing of the associated generation.12 
•
Option 7: allows co-located load to be classified as “non-capacity backed load” for a 
transitional period until the appropriate resource adequacy balance is obtained.  This 
would allow the large load to avoid capacity costs during the transitional period and 
allow PJM to curtail the large load in emergencies.  This option may allow large loads 
to be interconnected to the grid more quickly than traditional interconnection 
processes.13   
•
Option 8: co-located load may participate as demand response with potential 
expansions on the use of diesel generators as back-up generation.14  
Buckeye supports the Commission providing additional clarity or guidance to support these 
options which provide flexibility for the co-located load, while also addressing resource adequacy 
issues.

C. Transmission Cost Impacts of Large Load
The Commission has raised concerns with issues of transmission cost allocation and 
forecasting relating to co-location arrangements.15  PJM recognizes in its response that inaccurate 
forecasting can result in overbuilt transmission infrastructure, and that this risk is true with respect 
to all large load additions—not just those co-located with generation.16  PJM notes that for both 
transmission and capacity costs, “[t]o the extent the forecasted load additions do not actually 
materialize, these higher capacity [and transmission] costs would be allocated to all other load on 
the system.”17  Buckeye agrees that forecasting and cost allocation issues associated with large 
loads are a significant concern and one that the Commission should address, regardless of whether 
that load is co-located with generation or not.  
Load growth of approximately 10,000 MW driven by data centers in the Dominion Zone 
resulted in approximately $13 billion in transmission investment to accommodate this load.18  
Central Ohio is also experiencing massive load increases that will require significant transmission 
investment, impacting the transmission rates for everyone in the AEP Zone, including Buckeye 
and its members.  PJM’s load forecast analysis that drives transmission investment anticipates

If the load shows up as expected, the massive investments in new transmission should not 
result in cost increases to wholesale transmission customers, and ultimately retail ratepayers.  The 
expansion in the load paying for the increased rate base should maintain or decrease the rates for 
existing transmission customers.  However, other customers in the applicable zone could face 
significant cost increases if massive transmission costs are incurred, and the load does not show 
up as planned for in the transmission expansion, is or becomes significantly lower than the original 
request, or has the ability to bypass the transmission cost allocation mechanism.22

the AEP Zone of PJM, transmission costs are allocated to wholesale transmission customers based 
on an annual single coincident peak (1 CP) – meaning that transmission costs are allocated to 
wholesale transmission customers/LSEs based on their usage during a single hour of the entire 
year.  Because the massive transmission investments that are driven by these large loads are sunk 
costs that are recovered over time through transmission rates, rather than directly assigned to the

To address these cost-shifting concerns caused by new large loads, the Commission should 
consider the following solutions, as more fully addressed in Buckeye’s Comments filed on 
December 9, 2024 in Docket AD24-11:
LSE Responsibility.  The Commission should consider requiring the LSE that is driving the 
transmission investment (i.e., the LSE in whose territory the retail large load is locating) to be 
responsible for any cost-shifting caused by large loads that do not show up as expected, thereby 
covering the costs of transmission investments caused by their retail load.  The LSE with the large 
new load should be the entity that receives this price signal, because it has a retail relationship with 
the large load that creates the potential for stranded transmission investment and is therefore able 
to pass the stranded cost responsibility to that retail load.  For instance, the LSE could enter into 
retail-level agreements with the large loads driving such transmission investments that address cost 
responsibility in the event that load does not materialize as anticipated (e.g., through a minimum 
demand charge, and minimum contract term, backed by appropriate credit support requirements). 
Importantly, any costs collected under such retail arrangements should be credited to the wholesale 
transmission revenue requirement to ensure that any stranded transmission costs associated with 
such large loads are not shifted to other LSEs in the zone.23
