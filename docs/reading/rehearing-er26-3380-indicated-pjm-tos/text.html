I. BACKGROUND
On July 31, 2026, after a truncated CIFP review process and without proper notice to the TOs, PJM filed its RBP proposal with the Commission under FPA section 205, with a proposed effective date of September 29, 2026.  The RBP proposal included an Exit Amendment that would establish new prerequisites to a TO exiting PJM and impose new fees on exiting TOs.  The Indicated PJM Transmission Owners submitted a Protest opposing this Exit Amendment.  This Protest was not brought on by any present desire to exit PJM; rather,

B. Argument
1. The Exit Amendment Violates Cost-Causation Principles
The Order’s preliminary conclusion that the Exit Amendment may violate cost- causation principles should have been stated finally and definitively, instead of being left open for decision after a paper hearing.  Under cost-causation principles, rates must “reflect to some degree the costs actually caused by the customer who must pay for them,” and costs must be

6
allocated in a manner at least roughly commensurate with benefits.15  As the Order recognized, PJM proposes to procure RBP capacity “on behalf of LSEs,” and “the beneficiaries of the RBP are the LSEs on whose behalf PJM will procure capacity through the RBP.”16  TOs “are not the buyers for which PJM procures capacity under the RBP,” and PJM “fails to explain why” they “should be responsible for RBP Charges.”17  The Commission’s precedent points the same way: the Commission has found it just and reasonable to impose generation capacity costs on LSEs located within an exiting TO’s zone, not on the TO as such.18

Although the Commission expressed preliminary agreement with the argument that the Exit Amendment violates cost-causation principles, the Commission should not have left the issue open, but instead should have definitively and finally ruled that the Exit Amendment violates cost-causation principles.  The Commission should grant rehearing and issue that final ruling now.

2. PJM Lacked Authority To File The Exit Amendment
The Order summarized the Indicated PJM Transmission Owners’ filing-rights challenge, and PJM’s brief response, but the Order did not resolve this issue.19  The Indicated PJM Transmission Owners respectfully request that the Commission address that challenge now and sustain it.  PJM lacked filing authority for three independent reasons.

First, the Exit Amendment amends the CTOA, which already addresses an exiting TO’s obligations,20 and which may be amended only with the supporting vote of the TOs.21 The Exit Amendment adds new obligations to exit that have no counterpart in PJM’s governing documents, which “do not contain an exit fee.”22  And the Exit Amendment makes those obligations preconditions to exit: each payment obligation must be satisfied “prior to” the TO’s exit from PJM.23  Thus, the Exit Amendment constitutes an amendment of the CTOA—the existing version of which permits an exit only once the preconditions in sections 3.2 and 3.4 have been met.24  PJM cannot avoid the CTOA’s amendment procedures by casting a change to the core terms of the CTOA as an amendment to the OATT, rather than to the CTOA. Second, to the extent the Exit Amendment imposes costs on TOs in their capacity as TOs, this is a matter of recovery of transmission-related costs—which falls within the TOs’ exclusive filing rights.25

Third, even if the Exit Amendment somehow does not concern recovery of
transmission-related costs, at a minimum it “relates to or affects” provisions governing recovery of transmission-related costs, and PJM therefore was required to provide the TOs with 45 days’ notice before filing the Exit Amendment.26  PJM did not do so, opting instead for a truncated CIFP review process that provided a constantly moving target and no adequate notice.27

Because PJM failed to fulfill any of these procedural prerequisites, PJM exceeded its authority in unilaterally submitting the Exit Amendment as a section 205 filing.  The sole mechanism by which PJM could have filed this proposal would have been pursuant to section 206—but PJM failed to do so, or to carry the associated burden of showing that the current exit provisions are unjust and unreasonable.

The Order’s suggestion that exit obligations might instead be placed on LSEs28 does not moot this challenge.  The Order did not adopt that modification; rather, it accepted the Exit Amendment as filed, anticipated that modifications may be directed after the paper hearing, and encouraged PJM to submit a new filing.29  The Commission should grant rehearing and hold that PJM lacked authority under section 205 to file the Exit Amendment.
