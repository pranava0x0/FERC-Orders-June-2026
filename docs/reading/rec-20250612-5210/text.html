Second, the Indicated Transmission Owners belatedly trot out the “major questions 
doctrine” to argue that the Commission does “not have any authority to require a new type of 
transmission service” and “cannot order any of the replacement rates contemplated by the 
parties.”15  This smacks of desperation.  In at least six rounds of prior filings in these proceedings, 
the Indicated Transmission Owners never previously raised this argument.16  And it is no wonder 
why: it is meritless.  Confirming that existing behind-the-meter generation rules in PJM apply to 
large loads, and adopting generator-contingent or interruptible forms of transmission service with 
accompanying rates, is well within the Commission’s statutory authority to ensure just and 
reasonable and non-discriminatory rates for transmission service, and is far narrower in its scope 
than, for example, the Commission’s recent transmission reforms in Order No. 1920, let alone 
Order No. 888.17  Ironically, if any party’s position implicates the “major questions doctrine,” it is 
the Indicated Transmission Owners’:  Congress expressly left to the states jurisdiction over retail 
power sales, yet the Indicated Transmission Owners ask the Commission to override a state’s 
choice to allow co-location and direct retail service by mandating that all large loads must be 
wholesale Network Integrated Transmission Service customers and pay on a gross basis.18
