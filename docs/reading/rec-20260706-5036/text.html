Commission-Led Technical Conference on PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms 
Docket No. AD26-7-000 
 
By ABDUR RAHMAN 
PJM Governance Mechanics, Board Authority, States' Role, Filing Rights, and Document 
Architecture 
July 2026 
 
Question 1. The PJM Board's role in ensuring that PJM is sufficiently tackling the region's 
operational and market challenges; how the Board evaluates its effectiveness in that role; and 
how often, and under what circumstances, the Board acts unilaterally, and whether this should 
change. 
The Board's highest function in the current environment is that of execution steward: the body 
accountable not for the content of every solution but for the fact that identified needs reach a 
decision on a defined schedule. In large institutional transitions, the governing body that performs 
best is rarely the one that decides the most; it is the one that guarantees that decisions are made, 
on time, by the actor assigned to make them. That reframing matters because it gives the Board a 
role that is both stronger and more disciplined than episodic intervention: the Board becomes the 
owner of the machinery rather than an occasional substitute for it. 
Three practices would operationalize that role. First, the Board should adopt and publish an annual 
governance execution review reporting, at minimum, the median and ninetieth-percentile elapsed 
time from issue identification to terminal decision by issue category, the inventory of open issues 
by age band, the number of filings made, and the number of matters resolved by default-outcome 
provisions. What is measured is managed, and today the performance of the governance system 
itself is largely unmeasured in any public, standardized form, which means the Board's 
effectiveness in its stewardship role cannot currently be evaluated by the Board, the states, or the 
Commission except by anecdote. Second, the Board should maintain a public decision log

recording each significant Board action or deliberate inaction, the rationale, and the positions 
received, converting diffuse institutional memory into an auditable record against which 
effectiveness can be judged over time. 
Third, on unilateral action: it should be rare, but it should be rule-bound rather than ad hoc. The 
Board should publish ex ante the criteria under which it will act without a supporting stakeholder 
vote, such as a reliability need identified by PJM operations or planning staff, a binding compliance 
deadline, or a demonstrated market integrity concern, together with the commitment that any such 
action carries a published rationale and attaches all dissenting positions. The question worth 
answering is therefore not how often the Board should act unilaterally but under what published 
rules. Rule-bound authority is more legitimate, and therefore more usable, than discretionary 
authority, and the existence of a credible, criteria-based backstop changes bargaining behavior 
upstream long before it is ever invoked, because participants negotiate more seriously against a 
known alternative than against an indefinite continuation of talks. 
Question 2. How states, through the Organization of PJM States, Inc. or other structures, should 
interface with the Board to enable the region to meet operational and market challenges while 
respecting PJM's independence. 
The design principle is structured voice without veto. State input is most valuable, and least 
disruptive to independence, when it arrives at defined stages, through defined channels, on a 
defined clock. Episodic or improvised access serves neither side: states cannot plan their 
participation, and PJM cannot demonstrate that participation was fairly provided. 
These comments recommend that every issue charter, as described under Question 3, include two 
state consultation windows: one at charter initiation, when scope and decision path are set and 
input has the greatest leverage, and one before any resulting filing is finalized, when the practical 
consequences for retail customers are clearest. Each window should have a fixed duration, a 
designated PJM owner responsible for receiving and responding to state input, and a requirement 
that a written disposition of state comments accompany the record. Joint technical sessions 
between PJM staff and OPSI on high-impact matters, held early rather than after positions harden, 
serve the same function at lower formality. Structure protects both sides: states receive a 
guaranteed, timely, documented channel rather than episodic access, and PJM's independence is 
protected precisely because the channel is advisory, bounded, and recorded rather than open-
ended. Over time, the written dispositions also create a factual record of how state input shaped 
outcomes, which is the most durable answer to any perception that consultation is ornamental.

Question 3. The governance reforms that would best improve accountability and responsiveness. 
Two reforms do the most work, and a third makes their effects visible. The first is single-owner 
accountability: every issue admitted into the governance process should have one named PJM 
executive sponsor accountable for driving it to terminal decision. Diffuse ownership is the most 
common failure mode in large-scale institutional execution; where everyone is responsible, no one 
is, and the record of stalled issues in any large organization is usually a map of unassigned 
ownership rather than a map of hard problems. 
The second is the mandatory issue charter. At the moment an issue enters the process, a short 
public charter should fix five elements: the scope of the issue, the accountable owner, the clock to 
terminal decision, the decision path through the committee structure stated in advance, and the 
default outcome if the clock expires without the required support. The default outcome is the load-
bearing element. A process with a defined default converts delay from a costless strategy into a 
choice with known consequences, which changes participant behavior at every stage before the 
default is ever reached. Charters also create the unit of account for governance metrics: without a 
defined start, clock, and terminus, cycle time cannot even be computed. 
The third is transparency instrumentation: the public decision log and the cycle-time reporting 
described under Question 1, published quarterly by issue category. Accountability requires 
observability. A governance system whose throughput is visible tends to improve for the same 
reason that any measured operational process tends to improve, and visible metrics allow the 
Board, OPSI, and the Commission to distinguish between a system that is slow because its 
problems are hard and a system that is slow because its design permits it. 
Question 4. How the division of Federal Power Act section 205 filing rights among PJM, the 
Transmission Owners, and the Members Committee affects PJM's ability to act effectively and 
quickly, when warranted, to address operational and market needs. 
Divided filing rights create two predictable execution problems regardless of how any particular 
allocation is drawn. The first is negative space: issues that fall between rights-holders, where each 
actor plausibly believes initiation belongs to another, and which therefore belong to no one. The 
second is incentive misalignment: the actor holding the right to initiate a change is not always the 
actor with the incentive to initiate it, so needed reforms can lack a motivated sponsor even when 
few participants oppose them on the merits. Both problems are properties of the architecture, not 
of the participants, and both are treatable without reallocating any underlying legal right. 
Three responses follow. First, publish a filing-rights map that states, for each recurring category 
of tariff and agreement provision, which entity holds initiation rights, under which instrument, and

through which amendment path, so that the negative space becomes visible and assignable rather 
than discovered issue by issue. Second, adopt an escalation protocol: where the Board designates 
a matter as a regional priority and the relevant rights-holder does not initiate within a defined 
period, the matter automatically proceeds to a defined alternative path, with the rights-holder's 
stated reasons attached to the record. Third, where rights are shared or contested, adopt tie-break 
mechanics built on the principle that disagreement should change the venue of decision rather than 
prevent a decision; the New England arrangements discussed under Question 6 illustrate the 
principle. The author takes no position here on the legal reallocation of any existing right. The 
recommendations concern the operational machinery that surrounds whatever allocation exists, 
which is where most of the delay actually accrues. 
Question 5. The extent to which provisions in PJM's Operating Agreement, Transmission 
Owners Agreement, Open Access Transmission Tariff, Reliability Assurance Agreement, or 
Manuals impede timely decision-making by PJM. 
The impediment is less any single provision than the architecture: five instruments, drafted and 
amended over decades, in which related provisions are distributed without a master index of who 
decides what through which path. Fragmented document architecture imposes a search cost on 
every issue before deliberation even begins, and search costs compound because they are paid by 
every participant separately. In large corporate transitions this is a familiar condition, and the 
remedies are familiar as well. 
First, PJM should produce and maintain a public cross-instrument index of decision rights and 
amendment paths. This is a navigational document rather than a legal amendment; it can be 
completed quickly, requires no vote to be useful, and pays immediate dividends by shortening the 
search phase of every subsequent issue. Second, PJM and its members should adopt boundary 
discipline between tariff-level and Manual-level content: durable rights and obligations belong in 
the filed instruments, while operational detail belongs in Manuals governed by a defined, time-
bound change process, so that routine operational updates do not carry tariff-amendment overhead 
and tariff amendments are not delayed by disputes that are operational in character. Third, a 
standing sunset review should identify provisions that require sequential approval by multiple 
bodies and evaluate, category by category, whether sequential approval still serves a purpose that 
parallel or single-body review cannot, since each additional sequential gate compounds cycle time 
multiplicatively rather than additively.

Question 6. Lessons from other regional transmission organization and independent system 
operator governance structures that PJM could reasonably adopt, recognizing regional 
differences. 
Two design lessons travel well. In MISO and CAISO, stakeholder processes are structured as 
advisory inputs to an independent board that retains both the authority and the obligation to act; 
those regions demonstrate that robust multisector voice and retained filing authority can coexist. 
New England offers a complementary lesson through a different mechanism: its governance 
arrangements provide a defined path by which a stakeholder-supported alternative can travel to the 
Commission alongside the regional transmission organization's own proposal, so that a failure of 
consensus changes the forum of decision rather than producing no decision at all. 
The transferable element is not any single structure, and these comments do not suggest that PJM 
abandon its multisector model, which reflects the region's history and gives its process legitimacy. 
The transferable element is the design principle common to both examples: every issue has a 
terminal decision point, the path to that point is defined in advance, and disagreement is carried 
forward in the record rather than allowed to arrest the process. PJM can adopt that principle within 
its existing sector architecture through the charter, escalation, and rule-bound backstop 
mechanisms described in these responses. Regional difference is a reason to adapt the machinery, 
not a reason to forgo the principle.
