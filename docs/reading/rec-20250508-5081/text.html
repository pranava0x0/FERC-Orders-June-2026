B. Generation Asset Owners Should Not Be Prohibited from Maximizing the Use of 
Their Assets 
NOVEC argued that nuclear power plants should not be permitted to serve new large loads 
because they were “largely constructed by or for vertically integrated utilities who funded the 
projects from cost-of-service rates collected by captive retail customers.”33  NOVEC states that it 
“agrees in principle that owners of generation assets should be able to seek to maximize the 
beneficial ownership of those assets,” but asserts that the Commission should reject the co-location 
of large loads at these generating facilities “because the Federal Power Act is a consumer-
protection statute.”34  
Vistra agrees with the principle that owners of generation assets should be able to seek to 
maximize the beneficial ownership of those assets.  PJM may not prohibit sellers from seeking 
beneficial bilateral arrangements in an effort to commandeer the sellers’ resources for the PJM 
capacity market.35  Attempts to do so may even rise to a regulatory taking.36  Moreover, NOVEC’s

C. Reforms to Load Forecasting Are Warranted, But Should Occur Through a 
Standardized Load Interconnection Process 
ACEG proposed improvements to PJM’s load forecasting process.  Specifically, ACEG 
proposed to require load-serving entities and electric distribution companies that request load 
adjustments demonstrate that any new large loads interconnections are backed by stringent 
financial commitments.  ACEG asserts that the additional financial commitments would not 
impose a new FERC-jurisdictional rate.  Rather, ACEG’s proposal contemplates that these reforms 
occurring primarily through retail tariffs, with “[m]inimal changes to BPM Manual 19.”43   
Vistra agrees that requiring such financial commitments would help improve the accuracy 
of PJM’s load forecasting by disincentivizing load customers from submitting multiple duplicative 
interconnection requests.  However, while Vistra agrees with the overall proposal, Vistra submits 
that these reforms would constitute a FERC-jurisdictional rate—not a retail rate—and, under the 
Commission’s rule of reason, should be placed in the PJM tariff rather than a business practice

III. 
Conclusion 
Responses to PJM’s Answer to the Show Cause Order provide a strong record for the 
Commission to act quickly to implement a replacement rate that ensures PJM accommodates 
behind-the-meter co-located load in a just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory manner.  
There is no valid basis to discriminate against behind-the-meter co-located load given that the 
reliability issues of new large loads apply regardless of the specific configuration in which they 
are added to the system.  Based on Dr. Shanker’s analysis of behind-the-meter co-located loads 
against a proper comparison case,48 and Mr. Herling’s conclusions that the relevant reliability 
concerns are equally or even more applicable to non-co-located large loads,49 it is clear that co-

II. 
DESCRIPTION OF CO-LOCATION PROJECT OPTIONS 
 
7. 
There are a number of potential options for the interconnection of co-location projects to the 
grid, involving different physical configurations and different means by which the co-located load 
will interact with the grid.  In its March 24, 2025 Answer in the above-captioned docket, PJM 
presents eight such options.  PJM Option 1 is not, in fact, a co-location configuration, but 
contemplates load connected to the grid at a separate point of interconnection from any generator. 
8. 
The remaining PJM options all involve load co-located with generation at the same point of 
interconnection.  Two of the options include relaying that will prevent delivery of energy from the 
grid following the loss of the co-located generating resource.  For example, Option 4 relies on a direct 
connection to co-located generation facility for the delivery of energy without reliance on grid 
transmission facilities.  Some of the PJM options also contemplate the ability of the co-located load to 
take interruptible service or participate as a Demand Response resource, thereby serving as a tool for 
operators to manage operational emergencies.

10.

IV. 
OBSERVATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS 
17. 
PJM and the PJM transmission owners currently perform a wide range of analyses to establish 
compliance of the grid with all NERC, PJM, and transmission owner planning criteria.  These studies 
span the entire range of power flow, short circuit, and dynamics analyses and have been performed, 
collaboratively, for decades, since the inception of PJM as an Independent System Operator.   
18. 
Through the Necessary Study process described above, PJM has the ability to evaluate all 
aspects of a co-location project to ensure that it is designed such that the grid remains reliable, 
consistent with all applicable criteria.  The Necessary Study relies on the same transmission system, 
generation, and load models that PJM uses throughout all aspects of the RTEP process.  It utilizes all 
of the same analytical tools and evaluates all of the same contingency sets to test compliance against 
all of the same reliability criteria. 
19. 
The modeling elements required to perform power flow, short circuit, and dynamics analyses 
for the various co-location options, proposed by PJM, are all knowable and any operational actions 
included in PJM’s proposed options are readily modeled.  PJM and the PJM transmission owners 
have the ability to collaborate to perform all of the analyses required to ensure the continued 
reliability of the PJM grid.   
20. 
With respect to PJM’s concerns about potential failure of a special protective system, it is 
worth noting that PJM plans, in its RTEP analyses, for N-1 conditions followed by a single protection 
system failure, such as a stuck breaker.  In this instance, a stuck breaker becomes a second 
contingency.  Based on my experience with the RTEP, the solution to stuck breaker criteria violations

is to add a redundant series breaker, making the failure of both breakers following an N-1 event a

21. 
It is also important to note that the voltage-sensitivity issue underlying the 1,500 MW load 
loss event cited by NERC is not a co-location issue.  Nothing in the NERC Comments or the attached 
materials suggests that any of the data centers involved in the event were co-located with generating 
resources.  The voltage sensitivity issue at the core of the discussion is equally, or perhaps even more 
applicable to front-of-meter interconnections of large data centers, such as contemplated in PJM’s 
Option 1.  Co-located generators are, themselves, a voltage source with automatic voltage regulators, 
reducing the likelihood that grid disturbances will exceed the threshold associated with voltage-
sensitive data center loads.  The loss occurred because the loads transferred from the grid to their own 
uninterruptible power supply (“UPS”) systems in order to protect data center cooling equipment, not 
because of any interaction with co-located generation.11  
22.

is sensible.  Coordination of study efforts between PJM and the PJM transmission owners has always 
been a foundational element of the PJM planning process and must continue for the grid to remain 
reliable and for customer needs to be met in a timely, fair and non-discriminatory manner.  Consistent 
with NERC’s stated concerns, PJM should have access to the data and information required to 
support operational actions and the performance of reliability analyses.  PJM as the planner and
