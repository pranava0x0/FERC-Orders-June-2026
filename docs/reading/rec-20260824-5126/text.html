I.​
Introduction 
 
Google, LLC ("Google") appreciates the leadership of the Federal Energy Regulatory 
Commission (“FERC” or “Commission”) to address Governance and Stakeholder Reforms in 
PJM, and submits these post-technical conference comments in response to the Commission's 
July 30, 2026, Notice Requesting Post-Conference Comments.1  Brian D. George serves as 
Senior Lead, U.S. Energy Markets for Google, establishing and executing the company's energy 
strategy to ensure access to adequate power supply and infrastructure required to serve data 
center facilities across RTO and ISO regions, including PJM.  Mr. George participated as a 
panelist during Panel 2 of the Commission's July 23, 2026, Technical Conference in this docket. 
Although Google is not a PJM member and does not hold voting rights in PJM's 
stakeholder process, Google was deeply engaged in both of PJM's recent Critical Issue Fast Path 
("CIFP") stakeholder processes: Large Load Additions ("CIFP-LLA") and the Reliability 
Backstop Procurement and Connect-and-Manage reforms ("CIFP-RBP/C&M"). Google 
participated in nearly every meeting and worked actively to form cross-sector coalitions, 
including one with four state governors (Pennsylvania, Maryland, New Jersey, and Virginia), and

The timeliness of FERC’s technical conference was framed by Chairman Laura Swett's

Google's perspectives are informed by its significant investment in the region and the 
principles of the Ratepayer Protection Pledge (“Pledge”)—which Google and six other leading 
technology companies signed in March 2026.3  The Pledge reinforces the fundamental principle 
that the costs and risks of new generation and regional network expansions required to serve 
large loads should be appropriately borne by those loads themselves, and not passed on to other 
consumers.  To achieve this, Google continues to advocate for a workable "Bring Your Own New 
Capacity" ("BYONC") framework to incentivize large loads to bilaterally contract for and 
finance new clean generation, thereby alleviating PJM's severe supply deficit and protecting 
captive retail ratepayers.

Based on Google's extensive participation, the pre-filed comments of other stakeholders, 
and technical conference discussions, three core structural principles should guide the 
Commission's evaluation of PJM governance reforms: 
1.​ Valuing Stakeholder Consensus Alongside the Need for Decisive Action 
PJM’s stakeholder process is an essential, highly valuable open forum for debate and 
collaboration. However, with more than 1,100 members representing naturally conflicting 
commercial interests, full consensus on foundational market and operational issues is simply not 
always attainable. 
The recent CIFP-LLA process illustrates this reality: While none of the twelve 
stakeholder packages achieved the required two-thirds sector-weighted vote, they were not 
twelve completely divergent paths. In fact, most proposals were conceptually aligned around key 
themes—specifically load forecasting, demand response, and BYONG incentives. Where broad 
agreement falls short of formal voting thresholds, PJM Executive Staff and the Board of 
Managers must step in to synthesize these shared themes, establish clear directives, and advance 
necessary regulatory filings that reflect stakeholder priorities while moving the market forward. 
2.​ Building on Stakeholder Input Through Deliberate Process Discipline   
Encouraging PJM leadership to step forward when consensus is difficult to reach is not a 
call to bypass the stakeholder community through unilateral Section 205 filing rights. The 
collaborative nature of PJM’s membership is one of its greatest strengths; setting it aside risks 
creating new regulatory friction and unnecessary uncertainty before FERC. 
The most effective path forward is to support and refine the stakeholder framework rather 
than replace it. PJM can achieve this by establishing clearer issue scoping upfront and keeping

discussions anchored to reasonable timelines. When a stakeholder process concludes, the Board 
of Managers is best positioned to synthesize the valuable insights and common ground members 
have developed, translating shared ideas into timely, well-supported regulatory solutions. 
3.​ Structured State Collaboration is Vital, But Must Avoid Gridlock 
Compared with other RTOs and ISOs, PJM is an outlier in its limited institutional role for 
state regulators and policymakers. Google supports a more robust role for states, particularly the 
13 governors and utility commissions within the footprint who are ultimately accountable for the 
retail rates customers pay and the siting of the infrastructure needed to maintain reliability.  
However, as noted by Commissioner Judy Chang and other conference participants, 
securing a single "states' view" is incredibly challenging and potentially unrealistic given the 
regional diversity across PJM's 13 states and the District of Columbia. Google supports a 
structure that would fund and empower states to coordinate their technical positions, bringing 
unified regional public-policy priorities directly into PJM's strategic planning and board selection 
processes without bogging down the day-to-day wholesale market operations.

Comments: Reducing the cooling-off period to one year is a pragmatic reform. It expands the candidate pool

5) 
Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable more 
communication between the Board and stakeholders? 
x 
 
 
 
Comments: The current ex parte communication limits isolate the Board from stakeholders, preventing direct 
and productive dialogue. During the CIFP-LLA process, PJM’s positions and acceptable pathways forward 
were not consistently known to stakeholders, creating a disjointed process. Google agrees with conference 
feedback that Board members should have regular, structured, and transparent contact with states and 
stakeholders early in the policy development process to build alignment.  
6) 
In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the 
Board be required to explain its reasoning?  
x 
 
 
 
Comments: Google strongly supports requiring the Board to publicly defend its decision-making rationale, 
especially when it deviates from stakeholder or state consensus on high-stakes processes, such as the recently 
concluded CIFP-LLA process. Market participants and long-term energy investors make multi-billion dollar 
investment plans based on these proceedings and sudden, unexplained late-stage shifts in proposal directions 
(like those seen in connect-and-manage, now IRAS, and RBP in 2026) inject severe commercial uncertainty 
and disrupt contracting for new generation.

16) 
Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered from 
two-thirds sector-weighted voting? 
 
 
x 
 
Comments: Lowering the voting threshold from 2/3 sector-weighted voting (as discussed by several states 
and panelists during the technical conference) could reduce gridlock. However, the mathematical threshold 
itself is not the primary bottleneck. The core issue is the lack of early executive direction, scoping, and 
decision discipline from PJM leadership. If the stakeholder process is made advisory and the Board is 
granted conditional independent Section 205 filing rights, the 2/3 voting threshold becomes a useful metric 
of stakeholder consensus rather than a barrier to regulatory filing. 
17) 
Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing 
committees, also apply to lower-level committees? 
x 
 
 
 
Comments: Google supports eliminating affiliate voting at lower-level committees and task forces. Under the 
current "status quo" rules, large market participants can leverage multiple registered affiliates to dominate 
lower-committee processes and "sector- block" proposals. Extending sector-weighted voting or Member-only 
voting to lower committees would prevent these coalitions from stifling innovative ideas and non-traditional 
proposals before they can even reach senior committees.

IV.​
Conclusion  
Ensuring a reliable, affordable, and clean grid in PJM will require moving beyond the 
pursuit of absolute stakeholder consensus toward more decisive, transparent, and collaborative 
leadership. By instilling greater process discipline, engaging states and stakeholders earlier, and 
empowering the Board to provide clear and proactive direction, PJM can deliver the regulatory 
certainty needed to support the next generation of energy investment. Governance reform should 
focus on strengthening these mechanisms rather than shifting or fragmenting filing rights
