Harvard Electricity Law Initiative Response to 
Questions 12 and 13 in the Notice Requesting  
Post-Conference Comments1 
Questions 12 and 13 in the Appendix to the Notice Requesting Post-
Conference Comments ask whether PJM should have filing rights over energy and 
ancillary services markets and regional planning. These issues are currently in the 
PJM Operating Agreement (OA), and any section 205 filing amending the energy 
market rules or planning protocols must be approved by the Members Committee. 
Moving either Schedule 1 (energy market) or Schedule 6 (regional planning) from 
the OA to the Tariff would allow the PJM Board to file under section 205 without 
the Members Committee’s approval.  This comment anticipatorily rebuts arguments 
that transmission owners hold any control, beyond their share of the Members-
Committee vote, over filing rights for matters housed in the OA. 
Two years ago, both the transmission owners and PJM asserted that 
transmission-owner consent, expressed through amendments to the Consolidated 
Transmission Owners’ Agreement (CTOA) filed by the transmission owners under 
section 205, was necessary to move Schedule 6 of the OA to the Tariff. The 
Commission denied PJM’s proposal to relocate Schedule 6 only because PJM

The OA Does Not Require Transmission Owner Consent to Move 
Schedules from the Operating Agreement to the Tariff 
When it interprets an RTO governing document to determine which entity 
has authority over a particular issue, the Commisison follows a two-step approach 
rooted in caselaw. Applying this framework, the OA cannot be read to empower the 
transmission owners with special authority over how PJM transfers its filing rights. 
The Commission summarized its approach to interpreting RTO governing 
documents in a 2020 proceeding about whether the CTOA delegated to PJM 
consideration of end-of-life transmission projects.12 The Commission explained that 
to determine whether the PJM transmission owners delegated authority over a 
particular issue, it first considers whether the relevant contract “unambiguously 
addresses the matter at issue.”13  
The Operating Agreement is unambiguous about the Members Committee’s 
exclusive role in authorizing section 205 filings that amend the OA. Section 8.8 
specifies that to amend the OA the Members Committee must act in accordance

The Commission Should Reject Arguments that the CTOA 
Clandestinely Requires Transmission Owner Consent to Transfer 
Schedules from the OA to the Tariff 
PJM and the transmission owners floated several theories about the 
transmission owners’ apparent authority over the Operating Agreement and 
regional planning. We briefly recount and rebut the arguments they filed in 2024. 
Filing Rights Do Not “Ultimately Reside” with the Transmission Owners 
In 2024, PJM claimed that “the rights to plan the PJM Transmission Owners’ 
facilities, and any accompanying filing rights, ultimately reside with the PJM 
Transmission Owners and require PJM to obtain the Transmission Owners’ 
agreement to transfer.”23 As discussed, the filing rights currently reside with the 
Members Committee, and the utilities did not retain any filing rights over the 
regional planning protocol.  
Filing rights “ultimately reside” with the utilities in a limited sense that has 
no relevance here. Commission approval of a utility’s withdrawal from PJM would 
terminate various contractual commitments. Assuming the withdrawing utility is 
not joining a different RTO, it would be out of compliance with the Commission’s 
Open Access transmission standards. The utility would likely have the right to file a 
new transmission tariff that meets Commission requirements, including about 
regional planning. In that sense, filing rights over matters that had been handled 
by the RTO would return to the transmission owner. That hypothetical would not be 
at issue if the Commission orders PJM to move schedules from the OA to the Tariff. 
So long as a utility remains a CTOA signatory, filing rights over regional planning 
do not “reside” with the utility.

The “Scope and Configuration” of Planning Rights Do Not Encompass Filing 
Rights over the Planning Protocol 
In 2024, PJM claimed that the CTOA “specifies the scope and configuration” 
of the right to plan regional transmission expansion.24 PJM pointed to CTOA 
provisions that delegate to PJM the responsibility to plan regional transmission 
expansion. PJM then presumed that control over regional-planning filing rights are 
hidden in these CTOA provisions. PJM’s conclusion cannot be squared with the 
Commission’s approach to contract interpretation. 
As summarized above, to determine whether the PJM transmission owners 
delegated authority over a particular issue, the Commission first considers whether 
the relevant contract “unambiguously addresses the matter at issue.”25 PJM did not 
claim that the CTOA unambiguously allows the transmission owners to retain 
authority over regional planning filing rights. To the contrary, PJM claimed that 
the transmission owners’ rights are implied by the regional planning’s unambiguous 
delegation of planning rights.  
Reading the “entire agreement” does not support PJM’s hidden-rights 
theory.26 In CTOA Article 7, the parties used specific and unambiguous language to 
transfer and reserve filing rights over various issues.27 It is implausible the parties 
would have been so specific about transferring filing rights in Article 7 while also 
implying that utilities retain a veto over filing rights transfers in the “scope and 
configuration” of the regional planning delegation.

The CTOA Does Not “Define the Location” of the Planning Protocol 
PJM claimed that the CTOA’s definition of the planning protocol demands 
transmission-owner consent to any transfer of Schedule 6 to the Tariff.28 The 
Commission should not infer any substantive meaning from this cross-reference. 
Article I of the CTOA defines the document’s key terms, including the 
“Regional Transmission Expansion Planning Protocol,” which “shall mean Schedule 
6 of the Operating Agreement, or any successor thereto.” That definition, according 
to PJM: 1) conditions the initial filing-rights transfer over regional planning from 
the transmission owners in 1997 on the precise location of the planning protocol; 
and 2) provides the transmission owners with a right to veto any relocation of the 
planning provision. 
That’s a lot of weight for this definition to bear. PJM attached far more 
significance to that single cross-reference in the CTOA than it did to dozens of cross-
references in its Tariff. To transfer of Schedule 6 into the Tariff, PJM proposed 
revisions to approximately fifty tariff definitions or sections that would replace 
references to Schedule 6 of the Operating Agreement with references to the new 
location of the planning protocol in the Tariff.29 In its section 205 filing to 
implement those changes, PJM “emphasize[d]” that these modifications were not 
“substantive.”30 PJM does not explain why an identical reference to Operating 
Agreement Schedule 6 in a single CTOA definition creates a legal right, while 
dozens of references in the Tariff are trivial.

D.C. Circuit Decisions about Filing Rights Do Not Prevent the Commission 
from Ordering the Changes Proposed in Questions 12 and 13 
In 2024, the utilities warned that if the Commission transfers authority over 
the planning protocol to the PJM Board without utility consent it would be 
“exceed[ing] its jurisdiction under FPA section 206.”31 The legal problem, according 
to the utilities, is that transferring the Members Committee’s filing authority 
without utility consent would “require the Transmission Owners to cede their filing 
rights over planning in a manner other than what they voluntarily agreed to.”32 No 
court decision or Commission order suggests that utilities may so restrain the 
Commission’s section 206 authority. 
Under the Atlantic City cases, the Commission “has no jurisdiction to . . . 
requir[e] utilities to surrender their rights under § 205 of the FPA to make filings to 
initiate rate changes.”33  The D.C. Circuit’s limited holding does not award the 
utilities control over filing rights that they voluntarily gave up by contract. In 2003, 
following the second Atlantic City decision, the Commission understood that it could 
act under section 206 to modify a settlement agreement between PJM and the 
utilities that allocates filing rights, and that it could even grant a party additional 
filing rights without transmission-owner consent.34 A section 206 order transferring 
a schedule from the OA to the Tariff is well within the Commission’s authority.

CONCLUSION 
Control over the OA’s schedules is a matter between the Members 
Committee, the PJM Board, and the Commission. The utilities’ endorsement via 
CTOA amendments of any transfer from the OA to the Tariff is legally irrelevant.
