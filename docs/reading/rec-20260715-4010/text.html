Pre-filed Comments of Maria Gulluni, ISO New England Inc.
Panel 1: PJM Governance Mechanics, Board Authority, States’ Role, Filing Rights,
and Document Architecture

I respectfully submit these comments in advance of the technical conference convened by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (Commission) on July 23, 2026 to discuss the governance and stakeholder processes of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM).  I appreciate the invitation to join you on Panel 1.

New England, of course, is a smaller and more defined region than PJM’s control area, and its states’ energy policies and regulatory structure are generally more aligned than in PJM (although not perfectly so).  Given these characteristics, ISO New England Inc. (ISO-NE) may have fewer challenges than PJM in working with its states and market participants on regional energy issues.  Nonetheless, to the extent it is helpful, I will describe our governance structure in this document.

I outline below the governance mechanics cited in the description of the first panel, including Board authority, the states’ role, filing rights, and document architecture.  I will focus on the ways, to my knowledge, that ISO-NE’s mechanics differ from that of PJM.1

States’ Role.  The New England states are represented by two organizations.  The New England Conference of Public Utilities Commissioners (NECPUC) “provides regional regulatory assistance” to New England’s public utilities commissions on topics including water, gas, electricity, and telecommunications.6  New England’s Regional State Committee, the New England States Committee on Electricity (NESCOE), was formed later than NECPUC and is more narrowly focused on electricity issues.7  NESCOE is directed by a committee composed of representatives appointed by each of the six New England governors, in his or her discretion; in practice, these appointees generally include energy policy officials as well as public utilities commissioners.8

In fact, NESCOE has committed to work within that process to present policy proposals for stakeholder consideration, and is required to collaborate with stakeholders to reach negotiated solutions where possible.12

NESCOE has committed to “make every effort to avoid duplication of efforts or conflicting policy positions” with NECPUC.13  NESCOE achieves this objective by meeting jointly with NECPUC, ISO-NE and stakeholders, and formally coordinating with NECPUC. While this coordination is required at least once annually to allocate lead responsibility for regional electric policy issues to “avoid conflicting recommendations and to make efficient use of available staff,”14 in practice it occurs much more frequently.

ISO-NE also works with the states’ consumer advocates through the NEPOOL process, as many consumer advocates are members of NEPOOL’s end user sector.  Consumers and consumer advocates also interact with ISO-NE through the Consumer Liaison Group, which was formed as part of the region’s compliance with the Commission’s Order No. 719.15 Board Authority.  Unlike PJM, ISO-NE is a non-stock, not-for-profit corporation organized pursuant to Delaware law.  Its Board of Directors is composed of nine independent members and the CEO, who is a non-voting member.  The Board of Directors also serves as the members of the Company, filling the role that shareholders play in a for-profit company.16  The Board does much of its work through its standing committees, which include Audit and Finance; Compensation and Human Resources; Nominating and Governance; Information Technology and Cyber Security; Markets; and System Planning and Reliability.17

The Committee seeks to reach consensus on new candidates.20  Following nomination by the Committee, NEPOOL votes on the slate, with a vote of 70% required to endorse the slate.21  Finally, the Board completes the process with a vote to elect.22 Board members must have a cross-section of skills, such as Commission electric regulatory affairs, energy industry management, corporate finance, bulk power systems, power pool operations, public policy, distributed generation or demand response technologies, renewable energy, consumer advocacy, environmental affairs, business management and information technologies.  At least three directors must have electric industry experience, and preference is given to New Englanders.23  Directors are subject to term limits and an age limit to facilitate turnover.24

 The Board interacts regularly with NEPOOL and the states (both NECPUC and
NESCOE).  Per the Participants Agreement, Board members are invited to NEPOOL meetings, which they regularly attend, and additionally meet with NEPOOL at least twice per year.25 These twice-yearly meetings occur between members of the Board and the individual sectors of NEPOOL, which are Transmission, Generation, Suppliers, Publicly-Owned Entities, Alternative Resources, and End Users.26

By practice, the Board also meets with representatives of both NECPUC and NESCOE twice per year, and has developed a “state liaison” program.  Per that program, the Board assigns two directors to each New England state public utilities commission.  These liaisons are available for the commissions to contact at any time, and the liaisons meet with their assigned commissions at least annually.  Each state commission usually invites the state’s governor- appointed NESCOE manager to these meetings.

ISO-NE has contractually shared its Section 205 rights with NEPOOL via a “jump ball;” pursuant to the relevant provision of the Participants Agreement, if NEPOOL approves an alternative to an ISO-NE market rule proposal, ISO-NE must file that alternative with the Commission pursuant to Section 205 on equal footing with ISO-NE’s own proposal.28 This “jump ball” right extends to a proposal made by NESCOE.  As noted above, NESCOE has committed to work within the participant process and present its policy proposals through that process.29  If NEPOOL supports an alternative market rule change proposed by NESCOE, the “jump ball” provision applies to that proposal.30  The states have other rights pursuant to the Tariff, as well, including decision-making on certain transmission planning projects through the Longer-Term Transmission Planning process.31  States can also provide scenarios to analyze through ISO-NE’s economic studies32 and Probabilistic Energy Adequacy Tool.

The transmission owners also granted NESCOE a “jump ball” regarding proposals for the allocation of the costs of generation and transmission interconnection upgrades.  Pursuant to that provision of the Transmission Operating Agreement, if NESCOE opposes the transmission owners’ cost allocation proposal for interconnection costs, NESCOE may provide the transmission owners with an alternative proposal that must be included in the transmission owners’ filing with the Commission on equal footing.33
