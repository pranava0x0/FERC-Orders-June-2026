On August 13, 2026, PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM’s”) filed an application in this 
case to implement a new Interim Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”). PJM requests an 
October 12, 2026 effective data with the IRAS beginning in the 2027/2028 capacity auction 
Delivery Year.   
OCC supports PJM’s proposal to interrupt New Large Loads that connect to the grid 
without sufficient supporting capacity before curtailing residential and other existing consumers.   
However, that proposal does not guarantee that it is the New Large Loads that will be curtailed, 
leaving other consumers at risk of bearing the burden of those curtailments.  Additionally, OCC 
does not support PJM’s proposal to compensate those New Large Loads for interruptions that are 
an inherent condition of their receipt of service without providing adequate capacity for their 
obligations. While the IRAS proposal would establish curtailment conditions for new data 
centers and large loads (at least 50 MW) that lack sufficient capacity, it fails to ensure that 
residential consumers will be protected from the costs or operational risks of IRAS.   
PJM’s proposal will impose excessive rates and curtailments of service on existing 
consumers in Ohio—consumers who are not causing the incurrence of these costs and operating

BACKGROUND 
PJM proposes to implement the IRAS proposal starting with the June 1, 2027 Reliability 
Pricing Model (“RPM”) Delivery Year.3 The filing proposes to require New Large Loads to be 
automatically enrolled for IRAS if they fail to bring sufficient capacity to satisfy their capacity 
obligations.4 PJM offers no proposal to address existing large loads that affected the capacity 
auctions run prior to the June 1, 2027 cutoff.   
PJM represents in the filing that it “hosts the largest concentration of data center load in the 
world.”5  It states that its load forecast “predicts peak load growth of 32 gigawatts [(“GW”)] from 
2024 to 2030, 30 GW of which is projected to be from data centers.”6 PJM explains that the 
2027/2028 RPM base residual auction (“BRA”) cleared over 6,600 MW short of the region’s 
reliability requirement, and that the 2028/2029 RPM capacity auction cleared over 6,800 MW short 
of that requirement.7 In other words, PJM represents that the addition of new supply has not kept 
pace with rapid large load demand growth, thus giving rise to the significant tightening of the 
capacity market in recent years and subsequent resource adequacy shortfalls.8  To mitigate the 
potential reliability risks associated with resource adequacy shortfalls, PJM proposes the IRAS “as a

II.
PROTEST 
The PJM grid must be maintained to ensure reliable and adequate generation and 
transmission services.  FERC has an obligation to ensure that this objective is satisfied in a 
manner that ensures just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential rates for 
services.  In fact, FERC’s primary obligation is to provide consumers a “complete, permanent 
and effective bond of protection from excessive rates and charges.”30
PJM seeks in this filing to ensure that New Large Loads that do not bring sufficient 
generating capacity to the system to satisfy their capacity obligations are placed at risk of 
curtailment when capacity is insufficient to satisfy their needs. This objective is laudable. It  is 
intended to protect existing consumers from the resource scarcity that could result from the

A.
PJM’s IRAS proposal to compensate IRAS load for curtailments is patently 
unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory or preferential and fails to 
protect existing consumers.  
There is no justification for compensating New Large Loads for curtailment during 
periods of resource shortages when those loads have failed to bring sufficient capacity to the 
system. PJM’s resource adequacy rules encompassed in the PJM Reliability Assurance 
Agreement Among Load Serving Entities are designed to obligate LSEs to bring sufficient 
capacity to the grid to meet the capacity needs of the consumers they serve.  PJM’s IRAS 
proposal would allow New Large Load customers to connect to the system without an obligation 
to bring sufficient capacity to satisfy their needs.  Treating the removal of the risk New Lare 
Load customers have caused as a separately compensable service and assigning the costs to 
existing consumers improperly shifts to other consumers costs attributable to the conditional 
service furnished to the New Large Load.  In that sense, PJM’s proposal is fundamentally

Although the intent of PJM’s IRAS proposal is to target the New Large Loads with the 
curtailment orders, that intent will not necessarily be achieved.  By design, PJM is proposing 
merely to direct the Electric Distribution in whose zone the New Large Load is located to curtail 
energy usage, and leaves the question of which end use customers will have to curtail to the 
states. But as discussed later in section II.D of the Protest, not all states in PJM have laws 
regarding large load-related curtailments.  Nor do they have laws regarding who pays for the 
compensation that will be required under PJM’s FERC-jurisdictional tariff.  Ohio is one such 
state.  Consequently, OCC protests PJM’s proposal to provide compensation to New Large 
Loads in the event they are curtailed under the new program. At a minimum, if compensation is 
to occur (and it should not), compensation and administrative costs of the IRAS should be 
directly assigned to the participating New Large Load or waived, with no recovery from 
residential consumers. 
The only possible explanation PJM offers for its IRAS proposal is its inapposite reference 
to its non-comparable program for compensating Pre-Emergency Load Response participants.34
This program allows PJM to call upon Pre-Emergency Load Response participants to curtail 
their loads in response to PJM’s issuance of a pre-emergency call for load reductions due to 
growing near-term generating capacity scarcity. Under this program PJM charges the participants 
that underperform or pays those who overperform 50% of the capacity Non-Performance Charge 
rate.35 PJM argues that “[f]rom a customer perspective, the non-performance rate appropriately 
reflects that load paid for capacity but did not get the benefit of the Pre-Emergency Load

But there is a big difference between the two programs that makes compensating New

participants that are compensated for curtailment during pre-emergency conditions are existing

Instead, the IRAS program will rely on curtailment options when generating capacity shortages 
exist.

To illustrate the unreasonableness of PJM’s proposal, consider the following example. 
The Governor of Ohio is holding a meeting to discuss the importance of affordable electricity in 
the state..  The Governor has invited entities that represent transmission owners and consumers 
to this meeting and tells everyone participating to bring their own lunch. A new entity finds out 
about the meeting, and the day before the meeting informs the Governor that it too is a consumer 
and plans to attend the meeting.  Only this new entity brings 25,000 to 50,000 people with it to 
the meeting.  None of these people bring their own lunch. The Ohio Governor has not included 
these entities in its plans for this meeting given the short one-day notice and does not have 
enough food to serve lunch to all these unanticipated meeting participants.  As a result, the 
Governor must tell the late-notice guests that they cannot share the food of those who were 
invited and who brought their own lunches. Under the PJM IRAS model, the Ohio Governor 
would have to compensate the uninvited guests for going without food during lunch, potentially 
at the expense of the other participants.   
This example is not outrageously exaggerated, nor is it unreasonable. PJM’s filing states 
that the peak loads of 50-MW New Large Load customers are roughly equivalent to the electricity

PJM also attempts to justify its proposed compensation scheme by arguing that “[u]nder 
conditions of tightening supply and demand, it is a measured and well-considered response to 
permit the new demand causing the shortfall on the system to interconnect, subject to load reduction 
under limited emergency conditions, but receive compensation for that load reduction.” 44  In 
support, PJM cites to an order on rehearing in a generator interconnection complaint case:   
Tenaska Clear Creek Wind, LLC v. Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 183 FERC ¶ 61,043 at P 24 (2023) 
(“Tenaska II”).  
 That case does not support PJM’s premise that interconnections allowed under limited 
emergency conditions may receive compensation for that curtailment.  In Tenaska II, FERC 
dismissed a complaint in which the generator argued that its facility had been unjustly

Simply put, the IRAS program does not satisfy the all encompassing objectives of the White 
House Ratepayer Protection Pledge – that existing consumers be truly held harmless from the 
risks and costs associated with both existing Large Loads and New Large Loads.  
 Only direct and complete assignment of these costs and risks to the Large Load 
customers will fulfill this promise. Consequently, FERC should direct PJM to submit a 
compliance filing that requires direct assignment to New Large Loads taking IRAS of 
curtailment directives, related penalties and associated costs as a condition to interconnection 
without bringing adequate generating capacity as required under the Reliability Assurance 
Agreement capacity obligation rules.
