On August 13, 2026, PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM’s”) filed an application in this case to implement a new Interim Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”). PJM requests an October 12, 2026 effective data with the IRAS beginning in the 2027/2028 capacity auction Delivery Year.

OCC supports PJM’s proposal to interrupt New Large Loads that connect to the grid without sufficient supporting capacity before curtailing residential and other existing consumers. However, that proposal does not guarantee that it is the New Large Loads that will be curtailed, leaving other consumers at risk of bearing the burden of those curtailments.  Additionally, OCC does not support PJM’s proposal to compensate those New Large Loads for interruptions that are an inherent condition of their receipt of service without providing adequate capacity for their obligations. While the IRAS proposal would establish curtailment conditions for new data centers and large loads (at least 50 MW) that lack sufficient capacity, it fails to ensure that residential consumers will be protected from the costs or operational risks of IRAS. PJM’s proposal will impose excessive rates and curtailments of service on existing consumers in Ohio—consumers who are not causing the incurrence of these costs and operating

I. BACKGROUND
PJM proposes to implement the IRAS proposal starting with the June 1, 2027 Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) Delivery Year.3 The filing proposes to require New Large Loads to be automatically enrolled for IRAS if they fail to bring sufficient capacity to satisfy their capacity obligations.4 PJM offers no proposal to address existing large loads that affected the capacity auctions run prior to the June 1, 2027 cutoff.

PJM represents in the filing that it “hosts the largest concentration of data center load in the world.”5  It states that its load forecast “predicts peak load growth of 32 gigawatts [(“GW”)] from 2024 to 2030, 30 GW of which is projected to be from data centers.”6 PJM explains that the 2027/2028 RPM base residual auction (“BRA”) cleared over 6,600 MW short of the region’s reliability requirement, and that the 2028/2029 RPM capacity auction cleared over 6,800 MW short of that requirement.7 In other words, PJM represents that the addition of new supply has not kept pace with rapid large load demand growth, thus giving rise to the significant tightening of the capacity market in recent years and subsequent resource adequacy shortfalls.8  To mitigate the potential reliability risks associated with resource adequacy shortfalls, PJM proposes the IRAS “as a

II. PROTEST
The PJM grid must be maintained to ensure reliable and adequate generation and transmission services.  FERC has an obligation to ensure that this objective is satisfied in a manner that ensures just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential rates for services.  In fact, FERC’s primary obligation is to provide consumers a “complete, permanent and effective bond of protection from excessive rates and charges.”30

PJM seeks in this filing to ensure that New Large Loads that do not bring sufficient generating capacity to the system to satisfy their capacity obligations are placed at risk of curtailment when capacity is insufficient to satisfy their needs. This objective is laudable. It  is intended to protect existing consumers from the resource scarcity that could result from the

A. PJM’s IRAS proposal to compensate IRAS load for curtailments is patently unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory or preferential and fails to protect existing consumers.

There is no justification for compensating New Large Loads for curtailment during periods of resource shortages when those loads have failed to bring sufficient capacity to the system. PJM’s resource adequacy rules encompassed in the PJM Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities are designed to obligate LSEs to bring sufficient capacity to the grid to meet the capacity needs of the consumers they serve.  PJM’s IRAS proposal would allow New Large Load customers to connect to the system without an obligation to bring sufficient capacity to satisfy their needs.  Treating the removal of the risk New Lare Load customers have caused as a separately compensable service and assigning the costs to existing consumers improperly shifts to other consumers costs attributable to the conditional service furnished to the New Large Load.  In that sense, PJM’s proposal is fundamentally

That Pledge, now signed by over 300 organizations including many of the large data centers and electric utilities in the nation,32 commits in relevant part that Large Loads will

1. build, bring, or buy the new generation resources and electricity needed to satisfy their new energy demands, paying the full cost of those resources whether by building, or buying from, new or otherwise additive power plants;

The Governor has invited entities that represent transmission owners and consumers to this meeting and tells everyone participating to bring their own lunch. A new entity finds out about the meeting, and the day before the meeting informs the Governor that it too is a consumer and plans to attend the meeting.  Only this new entity brings 25,000 to 50,000 people with it to the meeting.  None of these people bring their own lunch. The Ohio Governor has not included these entities in its plans for this meeting given the short one-day notice and does not have enough food to serve lunch to all these unanticipated meeting participants.  As a result, the Governor must tell the late-notice guests that they cannot share the food of those who were invited and who brought their own lunches. PJM also attempts to justify its proposed compensation scheme by arguing that “[u]nder conditions of tightening supply and demand, it is a measured and well-considered response to permit the new demand causing the shortfall on the system to interconnect, subject to load reduction under limited emergency conditions, but receive compensation for that load reduction.” 44  In support, PJM cites to an order on rehearing in a generator interconnection complaint case: Tenaska Clear Creek Wind, LLC v. FERC found in Tenaska II that “[s]ubjecting the interconnection customer that causes the need for upgrades in this manner ensures that the interconnection customer, bears the system- reliability burdens that flow from its decision to operate prior to completion of the required network upgrades.”48 FERC further found that curtailment in Tenaska II was appropriate because the curtailment occurred “on the basis that it [the generator], as an interconnecting facility, was subject to limited operation.”49 Like the generator in Tensaka I and Tenaska II, curtailment without compensation of a load interconnection customer is appropriate where the customer is being interconnected and begins taking service before sufficient generating facilities have been constructed to provide reliable service.

As FERC found in Tenaska II, this process allows the interconnection customer “to decide whether to operate its generating facility under limited operation pending availability of full interconnection capacity.”50  FERC properly concluded that “[t]he alternative is that the generating facility does not operate until full interconnection capacity is available.”51
