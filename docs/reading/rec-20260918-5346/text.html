EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Large Energy Users appreciate the collaboration resulting from the Large Load
Working Group and MISO's willingness to listen to stakeholder concerns. These Comments focus
on two topics where the Large Energy Users submit that clarifications are necessary to ensure just
and reasonable practices affecting rates and to avoid adverse economic consequences and
unnecessary regulatory burdens to large loads, especially existing manufacturing and institutional
loads that have not triggered the regulatory and legislative efforts responding to concerns associated
with high-impact, large computational loads. First, the proposed tariff language in Section 37A
II.2(a) lacks an explicit exemption for existing loads in service that experience incremental growth
of less than 25 MW in total. Second, the proposed tariff language in Section 37A.4 is ambiguous
and, if not clarified, creates the possibility of burdensome real-time forecasting requirements for all
Large Loads including conforming or non-computational loads' where the terms of such real-time
forecasting requirements are not specified in the tariff, contrary to the Commission's Rule of
Reason requiring that practices that significantly affect rates must be clearly delineated in the legally
binding tariff on file with the Commission and not left to implement through non-binding guidance
at MISO's discretion (i.e., business practice manuals). The Large Energy Users emphasize that,
because current reliability efforts undertaken by the North American Electric Reliability
Corporation ("NERC") and federal legislation in the Ratepayer Protection Act focus on addressing

In City of Cleveland v. FERC, 249 U.S. App. D.C. 162, 773 F.2d 1368 (D.C. Cir. 1985),
the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals held that utilities must file, "only those practices that affect rates
and service significantly, that are realistically susceptible of specification, and that are not so
generally understood in any contractual arrangement as to render recitation superfluous."12 This
doctrine, known as the Rule of Reason, requires utilities to file with FERC practices that
significantly affect rates and service, and are easily reduced to writing. While the Rule of Reason
recognizes that there is an infinitude of practices affecting rates and services, 13 and affords the
Commission ample discretion to apply the rule as it sees fit, this discretion is not unlimited. In
Keyspan-Ravenswood, LLC V. FERC, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals found that The New York
Independent System Operator's ("NYISO) method for translating installed capacity into unforced
capacity should have been filed with FERC because the translation methodology reduced the
internal reserve margin by nearly a third which significantly affected its compliance with reliability
rules set by the New York State Reliability Council. 4
As discussed herein, a five-minute interval real time forecasting requirement is a costly
and burdensome proposal that would substantially affect rates and services and specifically the
operational costs of manufacturers across the Midwest. Similarly to NYISO's conversion
methodology at issue in Keyspan-Ravenswood, LLC v. FERC, the real time forecasting specifics
proposed in MISO's Tariff should be filed with FERC. As such, the details regarding the real time
forecasting requirement should be detailed in the Tariff, not in the Business Practices Manual.
And when the details are filed with FERC, new forecasting requirements must be just and
reasonable practices affecting rates. Taken together with undue burden that would be imposed on

Regarding
LLWG: Large Load Operational Forecast Requirements (LLWG-2026-4A)
September 10, 2026
ABATE, IIEC, LEUG, TIEC, CMTC, and MLEC as representatives of the End-Use Customer Sector, and IBEC
and NLCG as representatives of organizations advocating for large end-users, appreciate this
opportunity to provide these comments to MISO.
During the June 29, 2026 and August 27, 2026 meetings of the Large Load Working Group ('LLWG'),
MISO proposed details of the requirements for the Large Load forecast submissions in hourly and five-
minute cadences and notice that it plans to monitor forecast accuracy including the potential for
penalties? Specifically, due to concerns that large load additions could add substantial volatile demand
that MISO's current weather and calendar driven forecasting cannot capture and that FERC raised within
its recent Show Cause order, MISO proposes to require the following of all Large Loads, regardless of
whether they are Non-Computational or Computational:
• A forecast of five-minute integrated demand for the next five-minute period and the proceeding
71 five-minute periods, updated every five-minutes; and
• A potential monitoring period to measure Large Load forecast to actual error rates with
tolerance bands that could be applied to different types of Large Loads, and the potential to
assign torecast-related penalty costs to the Large Load errors that create them.
While we understand and agree with the need to address the possibility of large load additions
introducing substantial volatile demand that MISO's weather and calendar driven forecasting cannot
currently capture, MISO has not demonstrated: (i) a reasonable need for the five-minute integrated
demand portion of its proposal for Non-Computational Loads, or (ii) a need to design penalty charges at
this time.

Regarding five-minute interval load forecasts, the following are important considerations:
• MISO has not provided any evidence showing the lack of individual load five-minute demand
forecasts for existing 50 MW and larger Non-Computational Loads has resulted in either
significant reliability or cost subsidy issues within MISO over the last decades which have
included a very large number of 50 MW and larger Non-Computational Loads.
• It is the possibility of additional, very large amounts of new Computational Load not operated at
a very high, steady load factor that can be quickly dispatched up or down based on market
conditions which introduces a potential significant challenge to MISO's current weather and
calendar driven forecasting, not Non-Computational Loads.
• Putting aside demand response participation, that has its own Tariff and Business Practice
Manual requirements, MISO currently settles all load in the real-time market on an hourly
integrated demand basis, and not on a five-minute integrated demand basis. As a result, there
are no market signals that would cause loads that can quickly change their demand, specifically,
Computational Loads, to make abrupt five-minute changes in their demand.
• Requiring five-minute forecasts for a six-hour basis, updated every five-minutes, would impose a
substantial continuous operational, analytical, and costly burden for Large Loads to provide
without clear evidence that it will improve the risks of inaccuracies within MISO's Reliability
Assessment Commitment, Look Ahead Commitment, or real time Security Constrained
Economic Dispatch processes.
Given the above we urge that hourly forecast data provided day-ahead for Non-Computational Loads,
when combined with the newly required real-time telemetry for Large Loads, is sufficient for not only
day-ahead needs but also for real-time needs, until proven otherwise with actual data-driven
experience. There is no FERC requirement for real-time five-minute forecasts from Large Loads nor has
there been a showing of MISO reliability problems given existing Non-Computational Loads that
necessitates a new burden for customers. The FERC only noted a concern that "MISO's Tariff does not
require transmission customers to provide MISO hourly forecasts, telemetry data, and other data to
ensure sufficient visibility...'
As for MISO's proposed creation of incentives/penalties for Large Load forecast errors:
• MISO has not provided any evidence showing that participant submitted load forecast errors
have been significant and have resulted in reliability or cost subsidy concerns over the last
decades which have included a very large number of 50 MW and larger Non-Computational
• For many Non-Computational Loads, due to the nature of their processes, it is not possible for
them to forecast demand within a narrow band of accuracy on an hourly integrated demand
basis, never mind a five-minute integrated demand. As such, applying penalties to them for
falling outside a narrow band accuracy would not be just and reasonable as any lack of accuracy
forecast would not be a behavioral issue but rather related to the nature of the processes
associated with these loads.

• Without evidence of actual significant forecast errors in aggregate, as individual errors could
balance each other out due to their diversity, causing significant dispatch cost impacts there is
no basis for a new allocation of existing commitment, reserve, congestion, and/or shortage
pricing to loads based on the theoretical concept of uninstructed deviation or failure to follow
dispatch instructions for load as MISO suggests within its August 27, 2026 presentation.
• If penalties were applied to a Transmission Customer for forecast deviations without a
mechanism to separate errors from updated forecast deviations, and without mechanisms to
allocate charges appropriately between the Transmission Customer(s), Load Balancing Areas,
and retail Large Loads, then the resulting charges have the propensity to become a zero sum
incentive if they end up blindly being assigned to all load within a Load Balancing Area.
Given this, if it were demonstrated forecast inaccuracy for Non-Computational Loads introduced
significant reliability or cost subsidy issues that need to be addressed, which MISO has not shown to be
the case, the appropriate action would be to first, monitor such deviations and study their reasons, and
then secondly explore potential changes to the cost allocation for ancillary services, not the immediate
application of penalties to these loads without a full examination of actual occurrences and conditions.
Collected data can be monitored for potential impacts and accuracy incentives or penalties considered
later, if needed, around tolerance bands that are within the Load's control and based on actual
experience. 5
For the foregoing reasons, MISO should limit the application of its proposed real-time five-minute
forecast requirement to Computational Loads.
Also for the above reasons, MISO should not pursue forecast deviation incentives or penalties at this
time. An actual data-driven and risk-informed framework applied proportionally to demonstrated
reliability needs, system conditions, and facility characteristics makes the most sense at this time.
