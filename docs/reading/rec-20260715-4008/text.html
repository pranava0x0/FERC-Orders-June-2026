I. Executive Summary
PJM’s governance model is broken. As Chairman Laura Swett stated at the May 2026 PJM Annual Meeting, the “current stakeholder process is slow where it must be fast, opaque where it must be transparent, and vulnerable to vetoes and agenda control exactly when the region needs immediate action.”3  While it may seem counterintuitive to tackle PJM governance while PJM and its stakeholders are simultaneously seeking to solve the substantive issues of large load integration and resource adequacy, the reality is that the flawed governance structure has (i) played a role in causing these problems and (ii) will prevent PJM and its stakeholders from developing effective, long-term solutions. PJM is fundamentally different from other RTOs and ISOs. ISO-NE, for example, consists of six states that have relatively homogenous state policies. MISO and SPP, while representing large footprints, consist primarily of vertically integrated states. New York and California are single state ISOs, minimizing regional policy friction that affects multi-state markets. PJM’s vast size, broad scope and regional diversity exacerbate its governance challenges. The fundamental flaws in PJM’s governance structure were largely masked over the past two decades because the relevant externalities were significantly different. During most of that time, PJM was in a position of excess supply and demand was manageable and somewhat predictable, with largely flat or declining load growth. Under surplus capacity conditions PJM’s governance was not truly stress-tested. With economics and policy causing the retirement of a significant amount of capacity in the region4 and the recent rapid demand growth driven by the integration of large loads,5 the externalities have now radically changed, and PJM’s governance problems have been fully exposed.

II. The CTOA and the OA must be amended to empower PJM’s regional transmission planning and enhance its accountability to asset owners.

1. What is the status quo under the current CTOA paradigm?
The CTOA is the foundational agreement by which the TOs collectively contracted with PJM to transfer control of over $80 billion of transmission assets to PJM. Under the CTOA, PJM is responsible for: (1) providing open-access transmission service, including processing transmission service and interconnection requests; (2) directing the operation of the TOs’ transmission facilities and monitoring/operating the bulk power system; (3) preparing the RTEP and the load forecast used as an input to the RTEP; and (4) conducting all planning of “expansions” and “enhancements” to the regional transmission grid.9 The TOs retain responsibility to: (1) build, finance, own, acquire, sell, or otherwise transfer assets, including terminating the member’s relationship with PJM; (2) maintain, repair and replace transmission assets; (3) identify and plan supplemental projects to address local reliability needs; and (4) seek FERC approval for new rules or changes to existing rules regarding the recovery of transmission revenue requirements, rate design and cost allocation.10

The CTOA is unique among the PJM governing documents in establishing a demarcated and fundamental split of responsibilities between PJM and its TOs. Yet, the agreement is often ignored in discussions about PJM governance. The legal and practical reality is that it cannot be ignored. From the TO perspective, discussions about the future of PJM governance must start with the CTOA.

 One fundamental flaw with the CTOA is that it memorializes a structure whereby the regional planning protocol establishing rules for regional transmission planning resides in the PJM OA instead of the Tariff. Pursuant to the CTOA, “Each party shall transfer to PJM, pursuant to this Agreement and in accordance with the Operating Agreement, the responsibility to prepare a Regional Transmission Expansion Plan and to provide information reasonably requested by PJM to prepare the Regional Transmission Expansion Plan...”11 And under the OA, PJM is required to obtain the approval of the PJM Members Committee prior to

PJM’s senior standing committees – the Markets and Reliability Committee and the Members Committee - use sector-weighted voting, with members categorized into one of five sectors with equal voting strength. Each of the five sectors has 20% of the vote and a proposal must achieve 2/3 approval from the sectors to pass.  PJM’s sector voting structure was designed to ensure that no one group could “disproportionately dominate” the stakeholder decision-making and voting processes. Yet the PJM voting structure when applied to today’s membership composition no longer works to produce effective outcomes.

Below are two real-world examples of anomalies and inefficiencies in the PJM voting process that impede resolution of critical issues:

• Critical Issue Fast Path (“CIFP”) process for Large Loads: This stakeholder process was initiated by PJM to develop rules around the important issue of the integration of large loads. Yet, when votes on proposals were taken in November 2025, only 118 of 520 eligible Members participated, reflecting 24% of PJM’s total voting population. The Generation Owner and Other Supplier sectors had less than half of their membership in attendance (29% and 7% respectively), but those two sectors combined for 40% of the sector-weighting under PJM’s voting protocol.20 The ability of two sectors to join together to “sector block” a proposal from being endorsed is magnified with limited

2. What changes should be made to PJM’s current voting rules and how can these changes be effectuated?

We present two types of potential solutions to revise the current PJM voting structure. The first category would involve implementing a different weighting so that votes are based on a member’s actual level of interest in PJM and its stake in PJM outcomes. The second category would simply make all voting advisory. In PSEG’s view, this second approach will not be as effective if pursued in isolation and, given the increased authority accorded to PJM under this scenario, should be accompanied by meaningful changes to enhance PJM’s accountability and clearly delineate responsibilities.

IV. The PJM stakeholder process should be reformed to accelerate the speed of decision- making.

We agree with FERC that the current PJM stakeholder process is too slow and subject to the obstructive agendas of various stakeholders. The inability to coalesce on issues in the PJM stakeholder process has resulted in tremendous inefficiencies and in some cases a complete failure to act at all. One such example, referenced above, is the CIFP process to develop large load integration rules, which occurred between August 2025 and November 2025. Despite 11 stakeholder meetings, no proposal was endorsed by the members for consideration by the PJM Board. This lack of action in turn resulted in direct federal and state intervention in PJM decision-making, specifically the January 2026 National Energy Dominance Council and the thirteen PJM state agreement memorializing the “Statement of Principles Regarding PJM.30 Modest changes can be implemented in the short term that will expedite decision-making in the stakeholder process, and we offer a few for consideration:

• Upfront identification of purpose and system benefits of all stakeholder proposals (would require revisions to PJM Manual 34): Solution proposals offered by stakeholders should contain a clear purpose - tied to the defined scope of the stakeholder process at issue - and should explain the measurable benefits that will result if the proposal is endorsed. If the proposal is lacking in any of these respects, PJM should require that the proposal, either be removed from consideration or changed to satisfy these criteria before a vote is taken.

• Member-only proposal submissions (would require revisions to PJM Manual 34): PJM should strongly consider limiting stakeholder proposal submission to members or requiring that at least one member be a co-sponsor to a proposal. Non-member stakeholders may continue to participate and be heard in stakeholder meetings - and can seek to influence the content of proposals - but should not be permitted to sponsor proposals without at least one member also attached to the proposal. Otherwise, non- member stakeholders are allowed a significant benefit of membership without bearing

V. Reforms are critical to enhance PJM’s accountability and ensure defensible, durable outcomes.  Specific to resource adequacy, additional clarity is needed to delineate responsibilities.

Accountability
PJM’s 2026 Whitepaper on potential market design reforms captures the gravity of the resource adequacy problem at hand. At the same time, the whitepaper fails to identify concrete, timely and actionable solutions. Rather, the white paper merely notes that relevant decisions on future market design “belong to the people and institutions with democratic accountability for their consequences – to state regulators and legislatures, to FERC, to consumers and the advocates who represent them.”32 Given PJM’s status as the independent FERC-approved entity responsible for the reliability of a grid that serves over 65 million customers, its choice to refrain from making a recommendation on a path forward feels inadequate.

Order No. 719 requires each RTO or ISO to demonstrate “that it has in place, or will adopt, practices and procedures to ensure that its board of directors is responsive to customers and other stakeholders.”33 In compliance with this requirement, PJM must make difficult, and at times unpopular, decisions and then explain those decisions to its stakeholders and stand by those decisions.

Defined Roles for Resource Adequacy

Enhancing PJM’s accountability will restore confidence in its decision-making at this critical time where it must work with its stakeholders and the PJM states to tackle resource adequacy and the rapid integration of large load customers. PJM has indirect responsibility for resource adequacy through its use of market pricing signals to incent the addition of new generation and to retain existing resources. However, PJM is not an integrated resource planner and therefore does not plan generation.

By contrast, the PJM states do have authority to address resource adequacy through the planning of generation and a vested interest in ensuring that, to the extent they continue to rely on PJM’s markets, rules for those markets are transparent and will procure sufficient supply to maintain reliability. There is a pressing need for clarity on the respective responsibilities between PJM and the states on resource adequacy, and discussions should occur as to how PJM and the states – with support from asset owners with responsibility for reliability - will work together to obtain this clarity. Those discussions need to happen now.

PJM, its states and the TOs have recently collaborated on load forecasting improvements, which will involve, among other things, use of a third party consultant to verify large load forecast data, review by the states of such data and formalized involvement by the states in the overall load forecasting process to ensure state policies are accurately reflected in the forecast.38 Appropriately involving the states in a fulsome way in load forecasting will help to ensure a more accurate and defensible work product. PJM and its states could adopt a similarly productive approach to resource adequacy, and one way to advance progress would be through the use of a properly-bounded state advisory committee, such as exists in SPP and the ISO-NE, with clearly- defined roles and responsibilities.

VI. Conclusion
Getting the rules right on PJM governance is critical and the time for action is now. Reforms must be focused on changing and improving PJM’s decision-making and providing PJM with the tools to effectuate sustainable outcomes that will ensure long-term system reliability. PSEG’s proposed solutions – providing PJM with filing rights over transmission planning rules, changing voting in a meaningful way, making stakeholder processes more efficient and enhancing PJM accountability for its decisions – are intended to satisfy these objectives.
