I. 
Executive Summary 
PJM’s governance model is broken. As Chairman Laura Swett stated at the May 2026 PJM 
Annual Meeting, the “current stakeholder process is slow where it must be fast, opaque where it 
must be transparent, and vulnerable to vetoes and agenda control exactly when the region needs 
immediate action.”3  While it may seem counterintuitive to tackle PJM governance while PJM 
and its stakeholders are simultaneously seeking to solve the substantive issues of large load 
integration and resource adequacy, the reality is that the flawed governance structure has (i) 
played a role in causing these problems and (ii) will prevent PJM and its stakeholders from 
developing effective, long-term solutions. The current process is locked in an endless cycle of 
finger pointing, lack of defined accountabilities, flawed voting and contentious stakeholder 
processes -- with no resolution in sight.

II. 
The CTOA and the OA must be amended to empower PJM’s regional transmission 
planning and enhance its accountability to asset owners. 
1. What is the status quo under the current CTOA paradigm? 
The CTOA is the foundational agreement by which the TOs collectively contracted with 
PJM to transfer control of over $80 billion of transmission assets to PJM. Under the CTOA, 
PJM is responsible for: (1) providing open-access transmission service, including processing 
transmission service and interconnection requests; (2) directing the operation of the TOs’ 
transmission facilities and monitoring/operating the bulk power system; (3) preparing the RTEP 
and the load forecast used as an input to the RTEP; and (4) conducting all planning of 
“expansions” and “enhancements” to the regional transmission grid.9 The TOs retain 
responsibility to: (1) build, finance, own, acquire, sell, or otherwise transfer assets, 
including terminating the member’s relationship with PJM; (2) maintain, repair and replace 
transmission assets; (3) identify and plan supplemental projects to address local reliability needs; 
and (4) seek FERC approval for new rules or changes to existing rules regarding the recovery of 
transmission revenue requirements, rate design and cost allocation.10   
The CTOA is unique among the PJM governing documents in establishing a demarcated and 
fundamental split of responsibilities between PJM and its TOs. Yet, the agreement is often 
ignored in discussions about PJM governance. The legal and practical reality is that it cannot be 
ignored. From the TO perspective, discussions about the future of PJM governance must start 
with the CTOA.  
 One fundamental flaw with the CTOA is that it memorializes a structure whereby the 
regional planning protocol establishing rules for regional transmission planning resides in the 
PJM OA instead of the Tariff. Pursuant to the CTOA, “Each party shall transfer to PJM, 
pursuant to this Agreement and in accordance with the Operating Agreement, the 
responsibility to prepare a Regional Transmission Expansion Plan and to provide information 
reasonably requested by PJM to prepare the Regional Transmission Expansion Plan...”11 And 
under the OA, PJM is required to obtain the approval of the PJM Members Committee prior to

PJM’s senior standing committees – the Markets and Reliability Committee and the Members 
Committee - use sector-weighted voting, with members categorized into one of five sectors with 
equal voting strength. Each of the five sectors has 20% of the vote and a proposal must achieve 
2/3 approval from the sectors to pass.  PJM’s sector voting structure was designed to ensure that 
no one group could “disproportionately dominate” the stakeholder decision-making and voting 
processes. Yet the PJM voting structure when applied to today’s membership composition no 
longer works to produce effective outcomes.

Generation Owner and Other Supplier sectors had less than half of their membership in 
attendance (29% and 7% respectively), but those two sectors combined for 40% of the

2. What changes should be made to PJM’s current voting rules and how can these changes 
be effectuated? 
We present two types of potential solutions to revise the current PJM voting structure.  
The first category would involve implementing a different weighting so that votes are based 
on a member’s actual level of interest in PJM and its stake in PJM outcomes. The second 
category would simply make all voting advisory. In PSEG’s view, this second approach will 
not be as effective if pursued in isolation and, given the increased authority accorded to PJM 
under this scenario, should be accompanied by meaningful changes to enhance PJM’s 
accountability and clearly delineate responsibilities.

b.  Alternatively, PJM could consider adopting advisory stakeholder voting 
  
We recognize that other RTOs/ISOs employ full stakeholder advisory voting and that 
PJM already uses it for certain matters falling within the PJM Tariff such as RPM capacity 
market rules. For example, MISO operates under an advisory stakeholder process “that provides 
input to MISO on the regional system plan and a range of market- and tariff-related issues and 
policy initiatives.”28 Similarly, voting in the New England Power Pool (“NEPOOL”) stakeholder 
process for ISO New England (“ISO-NE”) is advisory; however, if an alternative NEPOOL 
proposal meets a particular voting threshold, “then ISO-NE must include in its Section 205 filing 
a description of the alternative proposal” under the “jump ball” provision.29 If advisory voting 
were to be implemented in PJM, it would shift the ultimate responsibility for decision-making 
onto PJM and create a structure whereby PJM could independently design and propose Tariff 
revisions to the FERC on all issues under its purview. While one may legitimately question 
whether this type of voting has worked with respect to the RPM capacity market rules, we 
understand that, at least in theory, full advisory voting could help to embolden PJM to act 
independently and more decisively. Because this approach would provide more authority to PJM 
it must be accompanied by enhanced accountability, including for the PJM Board, so that 
members have confidence in the decisions PJM is making.

IV. 
The PJM stakeholder process should be reformed to accelerate the speed of decision-
making.    
We agree with FERC that the current PJM stakeholder process is too slow and subject to the 
obstructive agendas of various stakeholders. The inability to coalesce on issues in the PJM 
stakeholder process has resulted in tremendous inefficiencies and in some cases a complete 
failure to act at all. One such example, referenced above, is the CIFP process to develop large 
load integration rules, which occurred between August 2025 and November 2025. Despite 11 
stakeholder meetings, no proposal was endorsed by the members for consideration by the PJM 
Board. This lack of action in turn resulted in direct federal and state intervention in PJM 
decision-making, specifically the January 2026 National Energy Dominance Council and the 
thirteen PJM state agreement memorializing the “Statement of Principles Regarding PJM.30  
Modest changes can be implemented in the short term that will expedite decision-making in 
the stakeholder process, and we offer a few for consideration: 
• Upfront identification of purpose and system benefits of all stakeholder proposals (would 
require revisions to PJM Manual 34): Solution proposals offered by stakeholders should 
contain a clear purpose - tied to the defined scope of the stakeholder process at issue - 
and should explain the measurable benefits that will result if the proposal is endorsed. If 
the proposal is lacking in any of these respects, PJM should require that the proposal, 
either be removed from consideration or changed to satisfy these criteria before a vote is 
taken.     
• Member-only proposal submissions (would require revisions to PJM Manual 34):  
PJM should strongly consider limiting stakeholder proposal submission to members or 
requiring that at least one member be a co-sponsor to a proposal. Non-member 
stakeholders may continue to participate and be heard in stakeholder meetings - and can 
seek to influence the content of proposals - but should not be permitted to sponsor 
proposals without at least one member also attached to the proposal. Otherwise, non-
member stakeholders are allowed a significant benefit of membership without bearing

V. 
Reforms are critical to enhance PJM’s accountability and ensure defensible, durable 
outcomes.  Specific to resource adequacy, additional clarity is needed to delineate 
responsibilities.  
Accountability 
PJM’s 2026 Whitepaper on potential market design reforms captures the gravity of the 
resource adequacy problem at hand. At the same time, the whitepaper fails to identify concrete, 
timely and actionable solutions. Rather, the white paper merely notes that relevant decisions on 
future market design “belong to the people and institutions with democratic accountability for 
their consequences – to state regulators and legislatures, to FERC, to consumers and the 
advocates who represent them.”32 Given PJM’s status as the independent FERC-approved entity 
responsible for the reliability of a grid that serves over 65 million customers, its choice to refrain 
from making a recommendation on a path forward feels inadequate.   
Order No. 719 requires each RTO or ISO to demonstrate “that it has in place, or will adopt, 
practices and procedures to ensure that its board of directors is responsive to customers and other 
stakeholders.”33 In compliance with this requirement, PJM must make difficult, and at times 
unpopular, decisions and then explain those decisions to its stakeholders and stand by those 
decisions.

There are corporate governance principles that PJM could adopt to promote accountability 
and responsiveness from its leadership to its stakeholders. For example, PJM should utilize a 
third party to regularly evaluate the performance of its Board and its executive leadership team, 
and its Board should in turn regularly evaluate the performance and effectiveness of its senior 
leadership. There are several questions that will need to be answered to effectively evaluate 
PJM’s performance, including: (1) what are the appropriate metrics to be used to measure 
accountability and responsiveness; (2) which entity is responsible for conducting that 
measurement; (3) where should those requirements be documented; and (4) what are the 
repercussions for failing to meet those performance metrics. However, the answers to these 
questions are secondary to beginning in earnest an effort to increase organizational 
accountability.   
In its compliance filing in response to Order No. 719, PJM pointed to the existence of the 
Governance Assessment Special Team (“GAST”) stakeholder forum as a demonstration of PJM’s 
commitment to considering further enhancements (beyond the requirements of Order No. 719) to 
its stakeholder and governance processes.34 The GAST was used to examine the concerns of PJM 
membership regarding governance and stakeholder processes and to develop solutions. Under 
that process, PJM implemented both the Consensus-Based Issue Resolution (“CBIR”) process 
and the Stakeholder Process Forum to more efficiently engage stakeholders.35 GAST also 
implemented the Enhanced Liaison Committee to address important and divisive issues.36  PJM 
should employ a forum comparable to GAST to work with its stakeholders to develop 
accountability metrics. That forum could also be used to examine whether the existing 
requirements for PJM Board participation remain appropriate, including the two-year cooling-off 
period on material affiliation with a market participant and the lack of a residency or regional 
connection requirement, or whether they should instead be modified or strengthened.37

Defined Roles for Resource Adequacy 
 
Enhancing PJM’s accountability will restore confidence in its decision-making at this critical 
time where it must work with its stakeholders and the PJM states to tackle resource adequacy and 
the rapid integration of large load customers. PJM has indirect responsibility for resource 
adequacy through its use of market pricing signals to incent the addition of new generation and 
to retain existing resources. However, PJM is not an integrated resource planner and therefore 
does not plan generation.  
By contrast, the PJM states do have authority to address resource adequacy through the 
planning of generation and a vested interest in ensuring that, to the extent they continue to rely 
on PJM’s markets, rules for those markets are transparent and will procure sufficient supply to 
maintain reliability. There is a pressing need for clarity on the respective responsibilities between 
PJM and the states on resource adequacy, and discussions should occur as to how PJM and the 
states – with support from asset owners with responsibility for reliability - will work together to 
obtain this clarity. Those discussions need to happen now.   
PJM, its states and the TOs have recently collaborated on load forecasting improvements, 
which will involve, among other things, use of a third party consultant to verify large load 
forecast data, review by the states of such data and formalized involvement by the states in the 
overall load forecasting process to ensure state policies are accurately reflected in the forecast.38  
Appropriately involving the states in a fulsome way in load forecasting will help to ensure a 
more accurate and defensible work product. PJM and its states could adopt a similarly productive 
approach to resource adequacy, and one way to advance progress would be through the use of a 
properly-bounded state advisory committee, such as exists in SPP and the ISO-NE, with clearly-
defined roles and responsibilities.

VI. 
Conclusion  
Getting the rules right on PJM governance is critical and the time for action is now. Reforms 
must be focused on changing and improving PJM’s decision-making and providing PJM with the 
tools to effectuate sustainable outcomes that will ensure long-term system reliability. PSEG’s 
proposed solutions – providing PJM with filing rights over transmission planning rules, changing 
voting in a meaningful way, making stakeholder processes more efficient and enhancing PJM 
accountability for its decisions – are intended to satisfy these objectives.
