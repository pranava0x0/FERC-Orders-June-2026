COMMENTS OF THE PUBLIC UTILITIES COMMISSION OF OHIO’S 
OFFICE OF THE FEDERAL ENERGY ADVOCATE 
 
On July 31, 2026, PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) submitted, pursuant to section 205 
of the Federal Power Act, revisions to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff and the 
Reliability Assurance Agreement to establish a Reliability Backstop Procurement (RBP). In the 
filing, PJM describes the RBP as a one-time procurement of new capacity that is part of a multi-
pronged, ongoing effort to address resource adequacy challenges in the PJM region driven 
primarily by the unprecedented addition of large loads, most notably data centers.1 
Over two consecutive Base Residual Auctions (BRA) for the 2027/2028 and 2028/2029 
Delivery Years, an insufficient commitment of capacity was secured to meet the PJM Region 
Reliability Requirement. As a result, PJM proposes an RBP, to be initiated on September 30, 
2026, that aims to procure the most recently identified shortfall of approximately 6,831 
megawatts (MW) of capacity less any quantities of qualifying credits (e.g., bilateral contracts) or 
other adjustments as specified in the filing.2  
The Office of the Federal Energy Advocate (Ohio FEA) of the Public Utilities 
Commission of Ohio (PUCO), fulfilling its statutory duty to advocate on behalf of Ohio’s retail 
electricity consumers,3 offers the following comments for consideration by the Federal Energy 
Regulatory Commission (Commission).

I. 
Background 
PJM’s capacity market, called the Reliability Pricing Model (RPM), is designed to ensure 
long-term grid reliability by procuring the appropriate amount of supply needed to meet 
predicted peak load, plus an adequate reserve margin, three years in the future. A primary 
objective of the capacity market is to match (or balance) power supply with future demand, 
thereby creating long-term price signals to attract needed investments to ensure resource 
adequacy. Under the RPM, capacity resources are procured through an annual, competitive BRA. 
Starting in 2023, PJM warned of a growing imbalance between supply and demand in the 
region, driven primarily by large load growth and resource retirements outpacing the buildout of 
new generation.4 Actions such as delaying auctions or implementing price collars further 
distorted the intended design of the RPM. Over the next three years, the seriousness of the 
changing landscape became more widely understood and is now a central, dominant focus for 
PJM, state regulators, and many other stakeholders. In December 2025, the BRA conducted for 
Delivery Year 2027/2028 represented the first time in recent history that large load additions 
significantly increased PJM’s load forecast, which, in turn, significantly increased the PJM 
Region Reliability Requirement, in megawatts, PJM sought to procure in the auction. The 
auction resulted in a capacity procurement that was 6,517 MW short of the PJM Region 
Reliability Requirement and 5.6 percent below the target 20% Installed Reserve Margin (IRM).5

Similarly, in July 2026, the BRA conducted for Delivery Year 2028/2029 cleared 6,831 MW

Over this time, PJM undertook various efforts to address the increasingly urgent resource 
adequacy needs of the region, including establishing a Reliability Resource Initiative and then an 
Expedited Interconnection Track to expedite the interconnection of new capacity resources, and 
initiating multiple Critical Issue Fast Path (CIFP) stakeholder processes to address the challenge 
of maintaining reliability while efficiently integrating new large loads. In August 2025, PJM 
initiated a broad CIFP on Large Load Additions, which, among other endeavors, led to an April 
2026 CIFP on the RBP. The initial scope of the RBP CIFP was later expanded to include 
consideration of an “Interim Resource Adequacy Service” framework, which heretofore was 
ongoing in a PJM Connect and Manage Senior Task Force.  
After lengthy discussion over two months and consideration of more than a dozen RBP 
solution packages, the PJM stakeholders failed to endorse any package to the PJM Board to 
conclude the CIFP effort. As a result, on July 27, 2026, the PJM Board issued a decisional letter 
setting forth the RBP mechanism contained in the instant docket.7 The Ohio FEA supports the 
filing.

B. 
A Parallel Path Is Necessary to Ensure Reliability.  
The rate of change in demand/supply conditions in the PJM region warrants a central 
procurement to comprehensively address the more than six gigawatts of capacity shortfall seen in 
the last two BRAs. First, it is important to note that the Ohio FEA strongly supports bilateral 
contracting between data center loads and new capacity resources, as well as PJM’s ongoing 
bilateral matchmaking effort, to address resource inadequacy in the region caused by data center 
loads. Bilateral contracting directly assigns the cost of new capacity to new load, removing any 
risk of cost shifting to other customers. However, bilateral contracting takes time, which will not 
mitigate escalating reliability concerns in the near term.

To incent new resources to address the capacity shortfall, PJM proposes to procure long-
term capacity for a fixed term of up to 15 years, running from Delivery Year 2028/2029 to 
Delivery Year 2042/2043. Critically, only new megawatts (e.g., new resources, uprates to 
existing resources, capacity transfers from deactivating resources) will be eligible to participate 
in the RBP, ensuring that the effort results in new capacity and does not simply shift existing 
resources from one capacity product to another. Under the RBP, new resources must be online 
no later than June 1, 2032, assume the obligations of any PJM capacity resource, and assume a 
must-offer obligation in the RPM at $0 (price taker), along with other supply obligations 
identified in the filing. PJM proposes a pay-as-bid structure for committed resources in the RBP 
and establishes a maximum willingness to pay structure set at $555/MW-day on a MW-weighted 
average basis, which is Point 1 on the Variable Resource Requirement demand curve 
(unconstrained) for Delivery Year 2028/2029.  
A vital and non-divisive component of the RBP is who should pay the costs. While the 
allocation of any wholesale costs to retail customers is within the purview of each state, the Ohio 
FEA asserts it is widely agreed that the costs of the RBP should be paid by the data centers 
seeking new capacity during this time of regional demand/supply constraint. To accomplish this, 
PJM highlights in the filing that PJM and states must work cooperatively.9 First, PJM will 
allocate the RBP costs to each zone/area based on the megawatts of load additions each 
zone/area is forecasted to serve (i.e., pro rata share of the procurement target). The zone/area will 
then allocate costs pro rata to Load Serving Entities, which, in turn, will allocate them to retail 
customers consistent with state directives. “PJM’s proposed methodology for allocating RBP

III. 
Conclusion 
PJM’s RBP is a timely effort to address reliability concerns caused by significant data 
center load growth in the region. We urge the Commission to approve the filing to secure long-
term capacity commitments from new resources to meet escalating demand and to mitigate any 
compounding effect on PJM’s energy and capacity markets or other ratepayers.
