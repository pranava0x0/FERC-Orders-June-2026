COMMENTS OF THE 
ORGANIZATION OF PJM STATES, INC. 
 
Pursuant to the Order Instituting Proceeding Under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act 
and Consolidating with Other Proceedings1 issued by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission 
(“FERC” or “Commission”) on February 20, 2025, and in response to PJM’s Answer filed on 
March 24, 2025,2 the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”) submits the following comments.3  
OPSI appreciates the Commission’s detailed investigation to determine whether PJM’s 
current rules ensure regional resource adequacy and grid reliability, accurately assess the extent to 
which large, co-located load arrangements use grid services, fairly allocate the costs these 
arrangements may impose on existing customers, and respect existing jurisdictional boundaries. 
OPSI discusses each of these topics in more detail below.

I. 
COMMENTS 
PJM’s stated approach to large load expansion—co-located or not—focuses on: “(1) 
maintaining resource adequacy; (2) enabling reliable system operations; (3) facilitating the states’ 
and stakeholders’ efforts to timely and effectively integrate large loads in a manner that meets their 
unique needs; and (4) providing information and analysis that supports the equitable alignment of 
transmission system usage and compensation.”4 OPSI appreciates this focus, recognizing that in 
order to facilitate these arrangements quickly and efficiently, the Commission must first ensure 
that these arrangements do not negatively impact regional resource adequacy and grid reliability 
in the PJM region and that existing customers are not otherwise harmed by these arrangements. In 
its previous comments, OPSI urged FERC to require long-term scenario planning, mandate 
transparent reviews of these arrangements, establish fair cost allocation for these arrangements 
when they use grid services, and affirm state jurisdiction over co-located retail sales.5 OPSI 
reiterates these points in response to both the Show Cause Order and PJM’s Answer. 
A. 
RESOURCE ADEQUACY, RELIABILITY, AND TRANSPARENCY 
The Commission asks a series of questions about what interconnection modification and 
deactivation studies PJM should conduct in reviewing co-location arrangements to ensure resource 
adequacy and protect the reliability of the bulk power system.6 OPSI appreciates that the first 
component of PJM’s approach to the co-location of large loads is focused on maintaining resource 
adequacy and believes that PJM and the Commission should promote the development of 
comprehensive and transparent processes to evaluate the impacts, both individual and cumulative, 
of these arrangements. PJM’s position is that any portion of a generation resource intending to

The Commission also asks if the necessary study process used for generator

generating units, not the addition of large co-located loads that are sometimes as large as 
metropolitan areas. OPSI continues to call on the Commission to require that transmission 
providers conduct transparent, publicly reviewable reliability studies pursuant to a PJM tariff for 
large co-located load arrangements to preserve system reliability.

B. 
CONSUMER PROTECTION AND COST ALLOCATION 
Beyond ensuring that large, co-located load arrangements do not degrade regional resource 
adequacy or the reliability of the bulk power system for existing customers, the Commission must 
also determine the extent to which large, co-located load arrangements rely on the bulk power 
system as it determines how to allocate transmission costs appropriately and the extent to which 
these arrangements impact rates for other customers. To that end, the Commission asks how 
different co-location configurations impose costs on other customers and whether large, co-located 
load should be required to take Network Integration Transmission Service, among other things.13  
It is important that the Commission accurately determine the extent to which these 
arrangements use ancillary services. PJM objects to the concept of fully or wholly isolated load 
“as it is contrary to engineering and economic principles.”14 OPSI agrees and discourages the

Commission from accepting the premise that load can be wholly isolated from the grid without a

The Commission asks a series of questions about how co-location arrangements can create 
costs, including transmission, energy, capacity, and ancillary services charges, for all customers 
and affect the allocation of those costs when these arrangements come online before PJM’s markets 
and transmission planning processes are able to respond efficiently.15 OPSI has previously written 
that the scale of the co-location agreements being negotiated “demands that appropriate cost 
allocation tariffs be developed that recognize the benefits and services received by the generator 
and its customer in behind the meter co-location arrangements.”16 Without a framework that 
studies the individual and cumulative impacts of these arrangements on the bulk power system, it 
will be difficult to assure stakeholders that large loads co-locating with generation are being 
accurately assigned the costs of the grid services they use, recognizing these arrangements may 
also create or shift costs for existing customers.  
C. 
JURISDICTION 
In its Post Technical Conference Comments, OPSI asked that the Commission “confirm 
that the sale between co-located generation and load is a retail transaction subject to state 
jurisdiction.”17 OPSI further stated “There is no regulatory gap. Sales of electricity from a 
generator to an end use customer in intrastate commerce are subject to state jurisdiction.”18 PJM 
challenges this assertion by stating that it is unclear that this is true across the PJM region.19 OPSI’s

Member States have processes by which jurisdictional issues can be resolved. Interested parties 
can file a petition for declaratory order seeking a determination from any State Commission 
regarding the extent to which such service is state jurisdictional. State Commissions allow for 
formal due process to all affected parties. 
OPSI continues to assert that there is no regulatory gap regarding jurisdiction over retail 
sales. Section 201(b)(1) of the Federal Power Act states that “The provisions of this Part shall 
apply to the transmission of electric energy in interstate commerce and to the sale of electric energy 
at wholesale in interstate commerce.”20 However, it specifically states that it “shall not apply to 
any other sale of electric energy.”21 The Commission is correct when it states, “First and foremost, 
states retain exclusive jurisdiction over the terms of retail sales.”22 And the Commission goes on 
to assert its jurisdiction “If [the sales from generating resources used to serve co-located loads] are 
not sales for resale—that is, if they are made directly to the end-use consumer—or if they are not 
in interstate commerce then the co-located generator’s sales are under state jurisdiction.”23 OPSI 
agrees with this assertion. 
II. 
CONCLUSION 
OPSI appreciates the opportunity to comment on the many important topics the 
Commission has raised in this Show Cause proceeding.
