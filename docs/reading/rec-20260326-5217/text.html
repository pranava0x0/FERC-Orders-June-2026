COMMENTS OF THE ALLIANCE TO SAVE ENERGY 
The Alliance to Save Energy (“ASE”) appreciates the opportunity to comment in this proceeding 
following the Commission’s December 18, 2025 order on the PJM co-located load show cause 
proceeding, the related compliance process, and the paper hearing now underway. In that order, 
the Commission found PJM’s Tariff unjust and unreasonable in part, directed PJM to submit 
compliance filings, required PJM to establish clearer transmission service options for Co-
Located Load, and directed PJM to report on enhanced load forecasting and demand flexibility 
measures.  
The Alliance to Save Energy has carefully reviewed the full record developed to date, including 
the positions advanced by PJM, the Indicated PJM Transmission Owners, PJM transmission 
owners filing separately, developers and technology providers such as Bloom Energy 
Corporation, Invenergy Renewables LLC and Invenergy Thermal LLC, and Constellation Energy 
Generation, LLC, as well as trade groups including the Business Council for Sustainable Energy 
and the American Council on Renewable Energy, and industrial coalitions such as the PJM 
Industrial Customer Coalition and Industrial Energy Consumers of America. The record 
demonstrates both the urgency of large-load growth and the sharp disagreement over how 
favorable treatment should be structured.  
The Alliance to Save Energy is a leading nonprofit, bipartisan coalition of manufacturers, 
utilities, technology companies, environmental advocates, and governmental leaders committed 
to advancing the role of fuel-neutral energy efficiency as a cornerstone of U.S. energy policy and 
valuing its contribution to affordability, grid reliability, economic growth, and energy security. 
ASE’s recent filings have consistently emphasized that efficiency-first approaches and demand-
side management are among the most cost-effective tools available to improve affordability and 
reliability, and ASE has separately urged the Commission to more fully evaluate the role of 
energy efficiency in wholesale markets as a resource that should be treated comparably to other 
reliability solutions.  
The Alliance to Save Energy’s contribution here is narrower than the full tariff architecture 
debate. ASE does not purport to resolve every dispute in the record over service tiers, upgrade 
allocation, or the precise scope of co-location arrangements under state and federal law. Rather, 
ASE focuses on a question that remains underdeveloped in the record and is squarely within 
ASE’s expertise: what conditions should govern when large loads receive favorable treatment in

any interconnection framework, whether through expedited processing, flexible service, or 
reduced cost obligations? The Alliance to Save Energy believes the answer must be grounded in

load growth. Planning, interconnection, and cost-allocation decisions should increasingly reflect 
verified load, measured system performance, and enforceable commitments, ensuring that 
infrastructure investment aligns with actual system needs. 
Summary of Position 
The Alliance to Save Energy supports clear, durable rules that enable responsible large-load 
integration while preserving reliability, cost causation, and affordability. Rapid load growth from 
data centers, AI, and advanced manufacturing is real and urgent, and many parties are correct 
that the Commission should preserve innovation and speed where possible. At the same time, the 
record is equally clear that the Commission must guard against hidden reliability risks, 
unjustified cost shifts, and one-sided frameworks that either overbuild the system or let favored 
customers evade meaningful obligations.  
The Alliance to Save Energy therefore urges the Commission to center its analysis on the 
conditions for favorable treatment rather than on a binary fight over whether all co-located loads 
must be treated as full Network Load or whether broad categories of netting should be allowed. 
In The Alliance to Save Energy’s view, favorable treatment, whether in the form of expedited 
interconnection, flexible service, or lower cost responsibility, should be available only where a 
large load can demonstrate verifiable reductions in system burden through efficient design, direct 
load flexibility commitments, or independently accredited demand-side contributions. 
In January 2026, the Alliance to Save Energy released Bridging the Load Gap: A Collaborative 
Path for Utilities, Hyperscalers and Customers1, which introduces the “Bring Your Own 
Distributed Capacity” (“BYODC”)(See Exhibit A attached) concept. Under that framework, a 
large load customer funds incremental, utility-directed demand-side management investments, 
including demand response, energy efficiency, managed EV charging, storage, and similar 
measures, to create independently verified capacity headroom in constrained areas. The paper 
emphasizes four principles directly relevant here: location and timing matter more than 
systemwide averages; utilities need bridge strategies while longer-term upgrades are built; 
verification and accreditation must be robust; and customer protection and fairness are non-
negotiable.  
On pages 5–6, the report summarizes analysis showing that a curated DR/EE portfolio for a 
representative Midwestern utility could meet up to 18 percent of 2030 seasonal peak, provide 
roughly 1 GW of dependable peak reduction across critical hours, and do so at an average cost of 
approximately $33/kW-year.2 That is the kind of bridge capacity that can improve affordability,

I. Grid Reliability & Planning: Integrating Large Loads While Leveraging Energy 
Efficiency 
The Alliance to Save Energy agrees with PJM, transmission owners, and multiple state 
commenters that large-load growth raises real reliability and planning concerns. PJM’s filings 
and the Commission’s December 2025 order reflect concerns that load can arrive faster than 
transmission and generation can be developed, and that the system needs more visibility into 
how these loads will operate if planning and resource adequacy are to remain sound. The 
Commission also directed PJM to provide an informational report on enhanced load forecasting 
and demand flexibility measures, signaling that the current planning framework is incomplete.  
The Alliance to Save Energy also agrees with developers and other stakeholders that the planning 
process can be more coordinated and more efficient. Bloom, Invenergy, and others argue that 
load and generation should be studied in a more integrated manner to reduce duplicative or 
unnecessary upgrades and to align interconnection and service timelines more effectively. Those 
commenters are right that large-load growth should not be planned solely as a wires problem. 
But the answer is not simply coordinated load-and-generation studies. It is integrated planning of 
load, generation, and demand-side capacity.  
The Alliance to Save Energy therefore recommends that PJM’s final tariff framework explicitly 
incorporate verifiable energy efficiency and demand flexibility into the conditions for favorable 
treatment. If a large load seeks expedited service, conditional service, or reduced cost 
responsibility, PJM should require that the customer demonstrate measurable reductions in 
system burden through one or more of the following: direct load-management capability, 
verifiable curtailability, utility-directed demand-side capacity contributions, or other 
performance-based mechanisms that reduce the need for peak infrastructure. This would respond 
directly to the core reliability concern in the record—whether the system must be planned for a 
given load—by tying favorable treatment to demonstrable reductions in that planning obligation. 
The Alliance to Save Energy also recommends that the Commission not permit enhanced load 
forecasting and demand flexibility measures to remain only in an informational report. The 
December 2025 order properly required PJM to report on those measures, but ASE urges the 
Commission to require PJM to convert mature, administrable elements of that work into 
enforceable tariff language on an accelerated schedule. Demand flexibility that materially affects 
planning, interconnection timing, or service eligibility should not sit outside the tariff. Current 
planning processes are increasingly influenced by anticipated rather than verified load growth, 
which can overstate system needs and drive unnecessary infrastructure investment. The Alliance 
to Save Energy agrees that improving load verification, readiness criteria, and forecasting

II. Cost Allocation & Transmission Service Design: Conditions for Favorable Treatment 
The record sharply divides between parties favoring full Network Load treatment and parties 
favoring actual-use or net-impact approaches. The Alliance to Save Energy does not believe it is 
necessary, or especially useful, for ASE to take a categorical side in that broader architectural 
dispute. Instead, the Alliance to Save Energy believes the Commission should focus on what 
conditions should justify more favorable treatment within whatever service framework PJM 
ultimately adopts. 
The Alliance to Save Energy strongly supports cost-causation principles. Facilities should bear 
costs in a manner reasonably aligned with the burden they impose on the system and the benefits 
they receive from it. That principle cuts both ways. It does not support blanket gross-load 
treatment for every configuration regardless of actual performance, nor does it support allowing 
customers to receive accelerated service or sharply reduced charges without providing 
measurable system value in return.  
One live issue illustrates the point. In her concurrence to the Commission’s December 18, 2025 
order, Commissioner Chang emphasized the importance of developing a minimum charge for the 
new transmission services, warning that otherwise a co-located load that takes little or no grid 
service could pay almost nothing while still relying on the grid’s existence and PJM’s services. 
The Alliance to Save Energy agrees that the minimum-charge question is important and should 
be resolved in a way that protects other customers from unjustified cost shifts. At the same time, 
the Alliance to Save Energy takes no position on a specific dollar figure. Instead, the Alliance to 
Save Energy recommends that any minimum-charge framework include an efficiency-
performance pathway: facilities that can demonstrate verified load flexibility, efficient operation, 
and compliance with measurable service limits should be able to reflect that performance in their 
ultimate cost obligations. In short, the minimum charge should protect against free-ridership 
without flattening legitimate differences in system burden.  
Further, where large-load facilities are capable of providing additional system value, such as 
through the use of on-site assets, storage, or controllable load, the Commission should ensure 
that such contributions can be recognized and, where appropriate, compensated, consistent with 
reliability and market rules. This aligns with the broader principle that large loads, when 
designed appropriately, can function not only as demand but also as active system participants. 
The Alliance to Save Energy’s BYODC concept provides a helpful model for thinking about this 
balance. Under that concept, a large load does not simply claim favorable treatment because it is 
large or innovative. It commits capital to incremental, utility-directed, independently accredited 
demand-side measures that create real capacity headroom and improve affordability and

reliability. That is the kind of performance-based pathway the Alliance to Save Energy believes 
the Commission should preserve room for in PJM’s tariff. Favorable treatment should be earned 
through verifiable system contributions, not assumed by configuration alone.  
 
III. Flexible Load & Demand-Side Capacity: Option 7, Option 8, and the Need for Strong 
M&V 
This is where the Alliance to Save Energy believes the record most needs development. 
PJM’s March 2025 answer to the show cause order proposed eight conceptual options for co-
location, including Option 7, an interim interruptible transmission service or non-capacity-
backed service for curtailable large loads, and Option 8, participation by co-located Network 
Load as a Demand Response resource. Those two options represent the clearest demand-side 
pathways in the record. Yet the Commission’s December 2025 order ultimately directed PJM to 
propose NITS, Firm Contract Demand service, and Non-Firm Contract Demand service, and to 
proceed through compliance and paper hearing processes, without converting Option 7 and 
Option 8 themselves into concrete, enforceable tariff language. ASE recommends that PJM’s 
tariff revisions now do so.  
Specifically, the Alliance to Save Energy recommends that PJM’s final tariff revisions include 
concrete provisions that operationalize the underlying concepts of Option 7 and Option 8. For 
Option 7, that means a defined flexible or curtailable service product with clear eligibility rules, 
measurable curtailment obligations, locational specificity where appropriate, telemetry or other 
verifiable monitoring requirements, baseline methodology, settlement rules, and meaningful 
nonperformance consequences. For Option 8, that means a direct demand-response participation 
pathway for large loads that is tied to real operational obligations rather than broad assumptions 
about demand-side behavior. These provisions should not remain conceptual; they should be 
embedded in enforceable tariff text.  
The Alliance to Save Energy further believes this is the right place to be precise about 
measurement and verification (“M&V”). The Alliance to Save Energy recognizes that 
measurement and verification must strike a balance between rigor and practicality. Standards 
should be sufficiently robust to support planning-grade recognition and system operator 
confidence, while remaining administrable and scalable to avoid creating unnecessary barriers to 
participation. 
In 2024, the Independent Market Monitor for PJM challenged the way certain energy efficiency 
sellers were claiming capacity-market payments, arguing that the reports supporting those 
payments relied on retail sales data, lacked direct contracts with end users, failed to establish 
legal rights to the claimed reductions, created risks of duplicative claims, and did not 
demonstrate that the sellers’ actions actually caused the reductions. In parallel, the Market 
Monitor’s 2024 State of the Market reporting criticized the absence of a robust registration

system, clear eligibility requirements, signed customer contracts, and stronger evidentiary 
requirements. FERC later accepted PJM’s filing to remove Energy Efficiency Resource 
participation from PJM’s capacity market. ASE does not seek to relitigate those disputes, and the 
Alliance to Save Energy is not defending the legacy EE subsidy model that gave rise to them.  
That history matters here because it clarifies what standards should govern favorable treatment 
going forward. The Alliance to Save Energy agrees with the core lesson: favorable treatment 
should not rest on assumed or weakly attributed savings. It should rest on direct commitments, 
clear contractual rights, independent verification, and measurable performance. But The Alliance 
to Save Energy also believes it is important not to conflate legacy EE-in-capacity-market 
participation with demand flexibility commitments by large loads. They are different constructs 
and should be treated differently. The former involved claims to have long-duration reductions 
sold into the capacity market, often through upstream or midstream channels. The latter concerns 
operational commitments by identifiable large loads that may seek favorable service treatment in 
return for verifiable flexibility, curtailability, or direct funding of incremental demand-side 
capacity. The distinction is material. 
That distinction is also reflected in the IMM’s later treatment of large-load demand-side options. 
In October 2025 and again in December 2025, the IMM argued that demand-side options, 
including DR and PRD, are not equivalent to new generation and that any demand-side solution 
for large data center load must be premised on performance-based compensation tied to actual, 
verifiable load reductions, with availability and pricing rules that meaningfully affect real-time 
demand. The Alliance to Save Energy agrees with that core point. Demand-side flexibility should 
not be treated as a loose substitute for new generation. It should be treated as a distinct product 
with its own strong performance standard.  
For that reason, the Alliance to Save Energy recommends that any tariff provisions implementing 
Option 7, Option 8, or related flexible-service pathways include: 
• 
Direct contractual commitments from the participating large load or its designated 
counterparty, 
• 
Independent M&V of actual load reductions or service-limiting behavior, 
• 
Baseline and settlement rules designed for large-load operating profiles, 
• 
Locational specificity where flexibility is being used to support local planning or 
constrained areas, 
• 
Meaningful performance consequences for failure to deliver, 
• 
And clear rules that distinguish verified operational flexibility from generalized program 
assumptions. 
These are the conditions that make favorable treatment credible, durable, and fair.

Conclusion 
The Alliance to Save Energy appreciates the Commission’s continuing work to bring clarity to 
this important issue. The Commission’s December 18, 2025 order properly moved the 
proceeding forward, but the record still lacks sufficient development of the demand-side 
conditions that should govern favorable treatment for large loads. The Alliance to Save Energy 
believes that is where ASE can make the most constructive contribution. 
The Alliance to Save Energy therefore urges the Commission to ensure that whatever 
interconnection or service framework PJM ultimately adopts, whether for co-located load, 
flexible service, or broader large-load treatment, it includes a clear pathway for facilities to earn 
favorable treatment through verified efficiency, direct demand flexibility, and independently 
accredited demand-side contributions. PJM’s Option 7 and Option 8 concepts should be 
translated into enforceable tariff language with strong M&V requirements. The minimum-charge 
framework should protect other customers while allowing efficient, well-performing facilities to 
reflect that performance in their cost obligations. And the demand flexibility and enhanced 
forecasting work the Commission has already required from PJM should be converted, where 
mature and administrable, into tariff language rather than left as a stand-alone report.  
By taking this approach, the Commission can avoid being forced into a false choice between 
rigidity and risk. It can instead build a framework that supports affordability, reliability, and 
economic growth, and that uses the demand side as a real system tool, under standards rigorous 
enough to deserve confidence from regulators, utilities, customers, and the market alike.
