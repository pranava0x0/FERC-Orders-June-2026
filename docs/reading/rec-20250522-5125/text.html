protect other transmission customers from bearing stranded transmission costs when large loads 
fail to materialize as forecasted. To meet its Federal Power Act obligations, the Commission 
should take additional actions to fill this problematic void.

Specifically, as explained in Buckeye’s earlier comments in greater detail, wholesale 
transmission customers other than the host LSE face significant risks of stranded costs if large 
loads do not materialize as forecast.14 The costs of the significant transmission investments needed 
to serve new, large loads are shared among LSEs in the relevant PJM zone on a load-ratio share 
basis pursuant to transmission owner (“TO”) formula rates filed as Attachment H to the PJM 
OATT. If the large loads do not materialize, the host LSE’s load does not increase (or may 
subsequently decrease before the cost of transmission facilities built to serve the customer are fully 
recovered through rates, even if the large load initially went into service at planned-for levels), and 
a portion of the costs of transmission facilities built by the applicable transmission owner to serve 
the large load would thus be shifted to other LSEs in the TO Zone. Although retail “financial 
commitments” described by Vistra and ACEG may allow the host LSE to recover costs from the 
large load that failed to materialize (and potentially protect its retail customers), in the absence of 
further Commission action resulting in the crediting of the benefits of these financial commitments 
against the FERC jurisdictional wholesale transmission revenue requirement, the benefits of these 
“financial commitments” would be trapped at the host LSE’s retail level alone and would do 
nothing to protect other wholesale LSEs in the applicable TO Zone or their retail customers from 
these stranded transmission costs. 
To ensure just and reasonable wholesale transmission rates, any costs collected under such 
retail “financial commitments” would need to also be credited to the wholesale transmission 
revenue requirement for the applicable TO Zone to ensure that the costs of any transmission 
facilities built by the transmission owner to accommodate such large loads, as planned and 
contracted for, are not shifted to other LSEs in the applicable PJM TO Zone. As Buckeye has

previously explained, the different approaches taken by AES Ohio and AEP Ohio illustrates how 
“financial commitments” at the retail level with regard to new large loads are not sufficient by 
themselves to ensure that other LSEs in the TO Zone are protected against unreasonable 
transmission cost shifts.15 Thus, additional mechanisms at the FERC-jurisdictional wholesale 
level, beyond the purely retail “financial commitments” describe by Vistra (and ACEG), are 
required to ensure just and reasonable Commission-jurisdictional transmission rates. 
As noted in Buckeye’s April 2025 Comments (at 10-12), and the additional details in our 
December 2024 Comments (at 4-9), the PJM Tariff should be revised so that each LSE is 
responsible, as part of the calculation of zonal transmission rates, for its large load demand.16 Such 
LSE responsibility will ensure that any stranded transmission costs associated with such large 
loads are not shifted to other LSEs through PJM transmission rates. Indeed, this approach would 
encourage LSEs to require financial commitments of their retail large load customers in order to 
backstop their wholesale responsibility, avoiding the issue of whether PJM should require retail 
financial commitments from large loads to host LSEs (much less the issue of where such a 
requirement should be located17). Critically, only the LSE where the large load is located has the 
ability to push stranded cost responsibility and “financial commitments” to the large retail load.

Other LSEs that would be affected by material reduction in large loads through transmission cost-
shifting at the wholesale level have no ability whatsoever to mitigate such transmission cost-
shifting through the imposition of retail “financial commitments” on large loads located in the 
retail service territory of other LSEs. 
It is therefore imperative that the Commission take steps to mitigate the potential for 
significant shifting of transmission costs at the wholesale level to LSEs that do not have the ability 
to mitigate the effects of such cost-shifting through the imposition of “financial commitments” at 
the retail level on the large retail loads causing such cost-shifting.18  If the Commission does not 
address this issue in this proceeding, the Commission should open a separate Section 206 
proceeding to ensure the PJM Tariff contains sufficient provisions to mitigate transmission cost 
shifting caused by large loads.19  Regardless of the approach the Commission decides to take, these 
issues should be promptly addressed.20
