INTRODUCTION

BACKGROUND 
A. 
CAISO Is a Unique Transmission Market That Does Not Operate in 
the Same Manner as Other RTOs.  
The OSC recognizes that “the framework under which CAISO provides transmission 
service is unique among the RTOs/ISOs and that certain responsibilities fall to the Participating 
Transmission Owners.”15  Most other regions operate under the traditional framework 
established in Order No. 888.  There, the Commission required all public utilities that own, 
operate, or control interstate transmission facilities to offer network and point-to-point 
transmission services to all Eligible Customers under a pro forma open access transmission 
tariff, and established “rules for discounting practices, provisions governing priority of service 
and curtailment, and a right of first refusal for all firm transmission customers.”16  That 
framework is based on advance reservations of transmission capacity by transmission customers.  
A transmission customer, including the transmission provider, may reserve existing capacity for 
native and network load growth, and the customer may secure firm transmission service ahead of

As the OSC recognizes, the CAISO’s framework differs from this traditional framework 
in several key ways.  The CAISO’s transmission service model has no formal application process 
for transmission service, and wholesale transmission customers do not reserve transmission 
capacity in advance.19  Instead, Scheduling Coordinators—entities certified by the CAISO to 
submit balanced schedules on behalf of customers20—submit bids or self-schedules for supply 
and demand into the CAISO day-ahead and real-time markets, with equal access to all available 
transmission capacity each day.   If available transmission capacity is insufficient, losing bidders 
cannot schedule service.  If in real-time all accepted schedules cannot be met, due to unexpected 
or unplanned events, the CAISO curtails schedules based on tariff-defined scheduling 
priorities.21   
Unlike a traditional network customer, the Scheduling Coordinator does not reserve, or 
pay for, transmission service on behalf of a specific Load.  Nor does the Scheduling Coordinator 
pay an access charge for scheduling and settling Load on behalf of Eligible Customers.  Rather, 
the CAISO Tariff assesses Regional and Local Access Charges on each UDC or MSS, as 
Eligible Customers, represented by a Scheduling Coordinator to serve Load.  As a result, the 
functions of a single network customer requesting service in other RTOs are divided among, and 
coordinated between, the PTOs, Scheduling Coordinators, and UDCs and MSSs.   
Under this unique framework, the CAISO does not refer to wholesale transmission 
customers as “Eligible Customers” in the same way as the OSC.22  Rather, in the CAISO, an 
“Eligible Customer” is the UDC or MSS that procures transmission service and provides service 
to Large Load End Users.23  PG&E is therefore proposing terminology tailored to how 
transmission service is provided to the same wholesale transmission customers referenced in the

B. 
CAISO and the PTOs Each Perform Unique and Coordinated 
Functions with Respect to Requests to Interconnect to the 
Transmission System  
Participating TOs are responsible for studying requests from customers with Wholesale 
Load to interconnect to the CAISO-controlled transmission system (“CAISO Controlled Grid”)

lower, PG&E’s assumption here, reflects the fact that the utilization anticipated from Large

center operation by E3 indicates that “[o]nly two data centers had load factors above 90% of 
their reported peak loads, and fewer than half had load factors above 80%.”136  As a result, 
PG&E proposes to convert the scheduled capacity into an annual Energy quantity for calculating 
the Minimum Annual Revenue Obligation by assuming an 80% annual capacity factor.

VIII. OTHER CHANGES TO THE TO TARIFF 
In addition to adding a new Section 10A, as well as replacing the currently unused 
Appendix XI, the new Large Load processes had a ripple effect on other TO Tariff sections, 
including, most importantly, the Definitions, where many new terms were added.  The changes 
that required PG&E to add new processes for Large Load also evidenced the fact certain portions 
of the TO Tariff had not always been revised through the past nearly three decades in a manner 
that kept it congruent with the CAISO Tariff.  Thus, PG&E has revised the body of its TO Tariff 
in a variety of ways.175   
First, PG&E observed that Section 1 no longer reflected Appendix F of the CAISO Tariff 
regarding how in-BAA transmission service was paid for.  Section 1 also was the first section

rather than “High Voltage” and “Low Voltage.”  The sets of terms are identical, and the 
Definitions now indicate that they are interchangeable.  But, PG&E chose only to update the 
terms in the body of the TO Tariff (as they were relevant) and left the terms High- and Low-
Voltage in its Appendices I-X.176  As noted, many new terms had to be defined, not only to 
reflect Section 10A, but to differentiate the TCLL Interconnection process from the existing 
Wholesale Load Interconnection process.  The term “Wholesale Load Interconnection” is now 
used for this more traditional service, primarily in Sections 8 and 10.  The definition of “Gross 
Load” was updated to the CAISO definition because Gross Load is used in Appendix XI.
