Time is of the essence for the Commission to order PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) 
to file revisions to its Open Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT” or “Tariff”) to incorporate all 
eight of PJM’s proposed co-located load configurations in its Answer to the Show Cause Order 
(“Answer”).1  Talen Energy Corporation (“Talen”) largely agrees with the jurisdictional principles 
laid out by the Commission in its Show Cause Order2 and believes that specific grounds for the 
Commission’s jurisdiction over the types of large-scale co-location arrangements detailed in  
PJM’s Answer will depend on the specific facts and circumstances of each co-location 
arrangement.

I. COMMENTS 
 
A.  
FERC Has Limited Jurisdiction Over Co-Location Arrangements and Should Not 
Interfere with Innovative, State-Led Approaches to Co-Located Load. 
In broad strokes, Talen agrees with the general jurisdictional principles articulated in 
Section VI.A of the Commission’s Show Cause Order.3  It is well-settled that the Commission has 
jurisdiction over wholesale sales and transmission of electricity in interstate commerce, the 
facilities used for such sale and transmission, and rules or practices that “directly affect the 
wholesale rate.”4  States, by contrast, have exclusive jurisdiction over retail sales of electricity, 
and generation and distribution facilities.  Congress explicitly “entrusted the choice of electric[ 
energy] generation to the States,” and States—through their siting, franchise, and other police 
powers—“retain authority to choose their preferred mix of energy generation resources.”5

As the Commission’s November 2024 technical conference made clear, market

Commission use this proceeding to clarify that (1) FERC generally does have jurisdiction over the 
types of large-scale co-location arrangements discussed in PJM’s Answer to the Show Cause 
Order, and that (2) the particular basis (or bases) of the Commission’s jurisdiction will vary based 
on the specifics of the co-location arrangement. 
1.  
The Basis for the Commission’s Jurisdiction Is Fact Specific and Will Vary for 
Each Co-Located Load Arrangement.  
 
Talen agrees with PJM that “the Commission’s jurisdiction over transmission service in 
interstate commerce” is “necessarily implicated in matters relating to co-located load arrangements 
where generator interconnections are being modified” or where a generator’s interconnection 
service agreement (“ISA”) contemplates a co-located load arrangement.7  Phrased differently, the 
transmission interconnection service received by generators is itself a form of FERC-jurisdictional 
transmission service.  FERC’s jurisdiction to evaluate whether such a generator’s ISA is just and 
reasonable will also extend to the co-located load arrangement, given that (1) the co-located load 
will be enabled and facilitated by equipment and facilities described in the FERC-jurisdictional 
ISA and (2) FERC’s evaluation of the ISA may consider specific reliability concerns, which in 
turn may require consideration of the co-located load arrangement and the extent to which the 
specific behind-the-meter configuration affects system reliability.8  In this regard, FERC would

However, if the load is not using any transmission network, point-to-point, or ancillary 
services, then the basis of FERC jurisdiction is not that the co-located load itself takes a FERC-
jurisdictional form of transmission service.  Instead, in such co-location arrangements, the ISA 
governing the co-location arrangement is the only part of the arrangement that is FERC-
jurisdictional.  The sole basis for the Commission’s jurisdiction will be that the co-location 
arrangement requires an amendment to the generators’ FERC-jurisdictional ISA (or alteration 
of an RTO/ISO pro forma document).  This would occur, for example, when a generator decides 
to disconnect entirely from the grid to serve a behind-the-meter load.  When the load has no 
grid connection, there can be no jurisdictional service. 
2.  
The Utilities’ Jurisdictional Arguments Are Incorrect. 
Exelon Corporation and aligned parties (together, the “Utilities”) have a different view of 
the Commission’s jurisdiction.  In a series of related dockets, the Utilities have sought to extract 
two holdings from the Commission: (1) that co-located load always takes a form of FERC- 
jurisdictional transmission service and (2) that the interconnection of co-located load is not FERC 
jurisdictional.  In addition to being internally inconsistent, those two arguments are both wrong. 
a.  
FERC Jurisdiction Over Transmission Service Provided to Co-Located 
Load.  
 
In the Susquehanna ISA proceeding (Docket No. ER24-2172), the Utilities have insisted 
that “[a]ll co-located load is grid connected” and that the Commission has jurisdiction due to the 
transmission service that the co-located load receives.10  These Utilities’ view is essentially that

FERC cannot require transmission owners to accommodate interconnection of generators if the 
configuration involves co-located load.  If credited, that view would create a situation even worse 
than the Attleboro gap, because (in this iteration of the vacuum) the transmission owners would 
happily occupy the vacant regulatory space, and exercise unilateral power to stop generators from 
serving co-located load merely by withholding their supposedly “required” consent to an amended 
ISA, and thus profit in all instances (by, e.g., functionally forcing data centers in front of the meter 
so as to achieve their long-term financial goals).  That arrangement benefits no one except these 
Utilities. Of course, not all utilities share these views. 
The Utilities’ suggestion that co-located load always takes a form of FERC-jurisdictional 
transmission service is driven not by any coherent description of the facilities in question, but 
rather by these Utilities’ desire to remain profitable within a market in which certain of their legacy 
services are increasingly less in demand.  Their goal is to either (1) force large loads to interconnect 
with their systems in front of the meter, which in turn will enable them to build system upgrades, 
socialize the costs to their ratepayers, and collect a return on their investment; or (2) convince the 
Commission to adopt a blunt rule under which all behind-the-meter loads would be treated as 
network customers, which would enable the Utilities to charge rents to co-located load for services 
that it does not need or use.  In short, these Utilities are determined to position themselves as 
intermediaries with veto power over privately-negotiated economic arrangements that do not 
involve them, but do limit their ability to collect rent when not actually providing service. 
It is of course true that co-located load is electrically connected to generation that in turn 
is often connected to the grid.  But the fact that co-located load is indirectly connected to the grid, 
without more, does not mean that co-located load is taking FERC jurisdictional service from the

grid.11  Indeed, the specifics of Susquehanna’s co-location arrangement make clear why it would 
be a mistake for the Commission to assert blanket jurisdiction over co-location arrangements based 
on that load’s supposed receipt of jurisdictional transmission service of ancillary services.  As 
Talen has elsewhere explained at length, the data center co-located with Susquehanna is not a 
network customer and has no right to take transmission service or backup power from the grid.12  
Susquehanna installed a protection scheme under which the co-located load will separate in the 
event of a loss of output from the generation facilities; in such a case, “redundant signals will be 
sent to an isolation breaker which will then open and prevent the co-located load from receiving 
any power or services from the grid,” meaning that it is physically impossible for power to be 
wheeled from the network to the generator and then to the co-located load.13  It therefore makes 
no sense to suggest that FERC’s jurisdiction over this type of co-location arrangement stems from 
any draw that the data center makes on the network, since such a draw would be impossible.   
Any assertion of FERC jurisdiction premised on the idea that “islanded” co-located load 
always takes jurisdictional transmission service would be overbroad and arbitrary.  The practical 
effect of such an assertion would be to improperly federalize the entire electric system, in 
contravention of the careful jurisdictional lines Congress has drawn.  Importantly, the Commission 
can order PJM to implement all five options not currently in its Tariff that it presented for the co-
location of large loads while respecting the jurisdictional lines Congress has established.

b.  
FERC Jurisdiction Over Interconnection.    
In Exelon’s pending PDO Proceeding (EL24-149), the Utilities have argued that the 
interconnection of end-use load is not FERC jurisdictional, supposedly because the 
interconnection procedures implemented by Order No. 2003 apply only to generation and not to 
load.14  At bottom, the Utilities’ position reduces to the notion that, if a State has jurisdiction over 
one stream of a wholesale generator’s output (e.g., a retail sale to co-located load), then the 
Commission somehow loses jurisdiction over that generator’s interconnection to the interstate 
grid.   
That makes no sense.  These Utilities cannot be right that the presence of non-jurisdictional 
facilities behind the meter divests FERC of all jurisdiction over the generator’s interconnection, 
because non-jurisdictional facilities (including the generator itself) always exist behind the meter.  
Interconnection service is a form of FERC-jurisdictional service, and a generator’s interconnection 
to the interstate transmission system does not stop being FERC-jurisdictional merely because there 
is co-located load behind the point of interconnection.  For that reason, FERC has for decades 
approved amendments to ISAs designed to accommodate co-located load.  Were that precedent 
not enough to resolve the question, Order No. 2003 itself expressly rejected the idea that FERC

co-located load as always taking FERC-jurisdictional transmission service.17  The Commission 
should reject these Utilities’ outcome-driven jurisdictional theories. 
3.  
The Commission’s Jurisdiction Is Limited to Establishing Operating 
Parameters for Co-Located Load Arrangements.  
Once the Commission confirms that it does have jurisdiction over the co-location 
arrangements contemplated by PJM’s Answer to the Show Cause Order, its next task will be to 
define the scope of that jurisdiction and explain how it will be exercised. 
As the Supreme Court has explained, the Federal Power Act contemplates a system of 
cooperative federalism under which both FERC and the States have important roles to play.18  With

its limited jurisdiction are “just and reasonable”19—e.g., that the co-location arrangement does not 
impair overall system reliability; that interconnections between generators and the grid are safe 
and reliable; and, when co-located load takes jurisdictional transmission service, that the rate for 
that service is reasonable.  But because States have “regulatory authority over retail sales (as well 
as intrastate wholesale sales),”20 FERC cannot act to superintend the more granular details of co-
located load arrangements.21  Likewise, FERC cannot prohibit or discourage co-location 
arrangements because of supposed retail rate impacts,22 and cannot force entities to participate in 
federally regulated energy or capacity markets.23  Nor can FERC put a thumb on the scale and 
favor one type of co-location arrangement or configuration over another; it is the role of the 
market—not the Commission—to pick winners and losers.24  In short, FERC’s role is simply to 
set basic guardrails within which co-location arrangements must operate.   
States, by contrast, have the power to fill in the details.  States control facility siting and 
the resource generation mix, as explained above.  States decide which entities (if any) can make 
retail sales in co-location arrangements, and how costs will be recovered with respect to those 
sales.  States should therefore be in the drivers’ seat with respect to crafting the bulk of the on-the-
ground regulatory framework governing co-location.  Indeed, a central feature of “Our

The Eight Options Presented by PJM are Just and Reasonable, and FERC Should 
Order PJM to Make Necessary Modifications to its Tariff to Effectuate the Five 
Options Not Already in the PJM Tariff Within 30 Days of FERC’s Order.  
It is important for the Commission to provide clarity and certainty to PJM, its stakeholders, 
and market participants.  As such, Talen respectfully requests that the Commission direct PJM to 
submit Tariff modifications to address the five co-location configuration options proposed by PJM 
and not already addressed in its Tariff.  Talen urges the Commission to order PJM to make all 
necessary Tariff modifications to effectuate the selection of Options 4 through 8 as a co-located 
load configuration within 30 days of FERC’s order in this proceeding.  
As PJM explained in its Answer, its Tariff already contains language to configure co-
located load as: network load with its own point of interconnection (“POI”) to the grid (Option 1); 
network load that shares the same POI with the generator but is separately metered (Option 2); or 
network load that has a behind-the-meter generation agreement with a co-located generator 
(Option 3).28  Conversely, PJM believes it will need to modify its Tariff language to allow co-
location configuration arrangements where: co-located load is non-network load connected 
directly to a generator connected to the PJM transmission system with various protections in place 
to avoid the potential of delivery of system energy to serve the load (Option 4); co-located load is 
configured the same as Option 4 and also has conditional approval from PJM to take back-up 
power from the grid if necessary (Option 5); co-located load is network load and it brings 
additional generation to the PJM transmission system that could serve as a capacity resource for 
PJM (Option 6)29; co-located load is network load but it uses a new transitional non-capacity

II. CONCLUSION 
The framework presented by PJM is just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory.  
PJM stakeholders rely on the Commission to make it possible for PJM to administer its 
transmission system in a way that meets the needs of its diverse stakeholders.  The Commission 
has limited jurisdiction to order PJM to enact the framework it introduced.  It is urgent that the 
Commission act in accordance with its jurisdiction as soon as possible to promote economic 
growth and support PJM in maintaining reliability on its transmission system.
