COMMENTS OF BLOOM ENERGY CORPORATION 
 
 
Pursuant to Rule 213 of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or 
“Commission”) Rules of Practice and Procedure,1 Bloom Energy Corporation (“Bloom”) 
submits comments in response to the Commission’s February 20, 2025 order.2  In addition to 
these comments, attached hereto is a report by the Brattle Group (“Brattle”) entitled, 
“Accelerating Integration of New Co-located Generation and Loads” (“Brattle Report”).    
Bloom’s comments herein focus on two approaches addressed in the PJM 
Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) answer:3 (i) PJM’s existing Behind the Meter Generation rules 
(labeled Option 3), and (ii) PJM’s proposal for co-located load that brings its own generation 
(labeled New Option 6).  Bloom appreciates PJM’s desire to expand options available to 
interconnect large load co-located with generation, but as explained below, finds that PJM has 
unnecessarily narrowed their applicability and created hurdles to their implementation.  As 
Option 3 and New Option 6 are the only options included in the PJM Answer that apply to co-
location with new generation, as opposed to existing generation, they are insufficient.

Given the identified national security goal to advance artificial intelligence (“AI”), there 
is a need to accelerate and coordinate the interconnection of data centers and co-located supply. 
The options proposed by PJM do not adequately meet this challenge.  The Brattle Report 
assesses the concept of interconnecting new generation and co-located load as a composite 
facility called an “Energy Park,” which shares some traits with the approach PJM calls New 
Option 6.  An Energy Park as analyzed by Brattle refers to: (i) load designated as Network Load, 
(ii) co-located generation that provides primary power to load,4 and (iii) controls and protection 
mechanisms used to limit physical withdrawals and injections between the Energy Park and the 
grid to pre-specified amounts.  Brattle finds that an integrated interconnection approach in 
assessing new load and new generation can significantly reduce the need for transmission 
upgrades, potentially avoiding billions in transmission expansion costs that have in the recent 
past been allocated to other consumers,5 while also significantly accelerating the interconnection 
timeline.  This approach would in turn creates an incentive for data centers to bring along 
primary generation resources that supply their needs on-site, ease resource adequacy concerns 
that have been highlighted by PJM, NERC and others.  
Bloom urges the Commission to develop and provide detailed guidance to PJM, as well 
as clear compliance deadlines for future filings to implement co-location tariff rules, including 
the reforms proposed in the Brattle Report.

I. 
INTRODUCTION 
The Commission has increasingly recognized the national security implications of AI 
data centers, particularly regarding their large energy demands and their role in supporting 
technologies.  Likewise, both the current and prior administration have explicitly linked the 
advancement of AI infrastructure (including data centers) to national security.  For example, on 
January 23, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order entitled “Removing Barriers to 
American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence,” requiring within 180 days of the order an action 
plan to enhance AI in order to promote economic competitiveness and national security.6  
Accelerating the interconnection of data centers co-located with generation is an action that the 
Commission can take in support of this national security goal. 
Bloom is a manufacturer of solid oxide fuel cell technology that utilizes an 
electrochemical process to power non-combustion distributed energy resources that can be 
located either behind the meter or in front of the meter.  Bloom’s solid oxide fuel cell systems are 
designed in a modular fault-tolerant format that provides critical reliability, and can be designed 
to provide three nines (99.9%) and five nines (99.999%) availability.  Bloom has installed over 
1.4 GW of its systems, of which over 300 MW power data centers.  The fuel cell systems are 
baseload, with the ability to quickly ramp up and down to follow load fluctuations, and have 
been recognized as an excellent option for data centers in PJM and elsewhere.7  While Bloom 
can install hundreds of megawatts at a site in as little as 6 to 9 months, where grid 
interconnections are needed and interconnection queues are long, meeting a data center’s request

As highlighted in the eight options presented in the PJM Answer, co-located 
configurations generally fall into one of two general buckets:  
• Network Load, Served by a Co-located Generator.  Here, co-located load and generation 
are configured such that (1) load is designated as Network Load and may take power 
from the transmission grid to meet a portion of its demand, and (2) the generator makes 
physical sales directly to the co-located load and also may make PJM market sales 
(though the generator could also be dedicated solely to serve the co-located load).  
Current tariff rules require the co-located generator to enter the interconnection queue 
and be studied for “deliverability” to all PJM load either if the generator intends to 
engage in wholesale sales or will utilize the transmission grid to deliver energy to load.   
 
• Behind-the-Meter, Isolated Load Served Co-located with an Existing Generator.  This is 
the configuration being pursued by a number of nuclear plant owners located in the PJM 
region and elsewhere, as well as the owner of a natural gas facility located in Virginia.  In 
this configuration, an existing interconnected generator co-locates new load and seeks to 
“isolate” the new load behind the co-located generator’s point of interconnection (“POI”).  
Whether the load must be designated as Network Load is a matter being litigated in this 
proceeding, but which Bloom does not address. 
 
Both Bloom’s comments and the Brattle Report’s analysis focus on improvements to the 
interconnection rules associated with the first configuration.  In particular, how to expeditiously 
interconnect generation and co-located load (both new) as a composite facility.  The Brattle 
Report employs the term “Energy Park” to describe this configuration.  While the term Energy 
Park has previously referred to a collection of different supply technologies, focusing on variable 
energy resources,8 as used in the Brattle Report an Energy Park consists of three functional 
components: (i) generation that serves as primary power for on-site supply and is subject to PJM

interconnection,9 (ii) co-located load, and (iii) a physical control or protection mechanism to 
potentially limit maximum flows in and out of the Energy Park at the POI.   
By planning to co-locate new primary generation that can run at any time, the co-located 
load can supply all or part of its energy and resource adequacy on-site, thereby minimizing the 
impact on markets and transmission and supporting the interconnected grid.  And by reflecting 
the controllable nature of Energy Park withdrawals and injections to the grid through one or 
more control and protection mechanism, properly structured studies that reflect the integrated 
nature of these resources when performed by PJM and transmission owners are less likely to 
identify expensive and time-consuming transmission upgrades, thus facilitating the 
interconnection of new loads and much needed new supply.  Any net injections by generation or 
net withdrawals by load at an Energy Park would depend on what the grid is able to 
accommodate in real-time operations.  Thus, withdrawals by load would be based on available 
grid capacity, and injections (market sales) by the co-located generator would be on a non-firm 
basis.  Brattle demonstrates that all of this can be accomplished while meeting PJM- and NERC-
mandated N-1 and N-1-1 requirements.   
Adoption of Brattle’s proposed integrated interconnection process for Energy Parks will 
require a number of reforms in the studies, protocols and procedures of PJM and transmission 
owners, which are necessary to achieve the speed and cost benefits of the proposal, including: 
• PJM and transmission owners: develop and document protocols for integrated Energy 
Parks interconnection studies, as an alternative to the currently applicable separate study 
processes, including the PJM generator interconnection study process, the PJM ‘Do No 
Harm’ study, transmission owner load integration study, and in limited cases the PJM 
regional transmission expansion process (RTEP).  Protocols would need to include 
provisions for coordination between separate study processes.

II. 
Comments 
Bloom’s comments in response to the PJM Answer focus on PJM’s Option 3 (existing 
Behind the Meter Generation rules) and New Option 6 (Co-located load that elects to be 
Network Load and bring own generation BYOG).  As to Option 3, PJM makes clear its lack of 
support for large loads to use of the Behind the Meter Generation tariff rules and omits that this 
option has been employed by both large and small loads over the past two decades.  Regarding 
New Option 6, the PJM matrix provides that all generation associated with New Option 6 must 
be a capacity resource, which as a result calls into question PJM’s statement that the option is 
available both to physical and financial arrangements by co-located load that brings its own 
generation.  Finally, we briefly describe the attached Brattle Report on “Accelerating Integration 
of New Co-located Generation and Loads.”

A. 
Option 3: PJM Should Clarify its Statements that Existing Behind the Meter 
Generation Rules were Not Intended for Use by Large Load (Question a.iv) 
 
In describing the existing Behind the Meter Generation rules,10 PJM repeatedly states that 
its use was never intended for large loads: “While this option is already available under the 
Tariff, it was conceived for, and has been implemented for, substantially smaller loads (like large 
warehouse with solar panels) and not on the scale of large data centers.”11  Data centers can have 
a range of customer loads—from just a few megawatts, to tens of megawatts, to hundreds of 
megawatts.  PJM, however, neither defines what it deems to be a “large load” nor provides the 
Commission with any sense of the customers being served by Behind the Meter Generation 
today, beyond large warehouses and solar panels.   
For years, cogeneration facilities have served behind the meter customers.  For example, 
the Linden Cogeneration Plant (Linden 6) sells to a behind the meter customer, the Tosco 
refinery, up to 160 MW.12  Likewise, Delaware City Refining Company owns a 324 MW 
combined cycle cogeneration facility, which has been described as “an integral part of the 
Delaware City refinery and refining operations.”13  Although the exact amount of customer load 
served from the co-located generation facility is unknown, it can readily be distinguished from

PJM’s desire to accelerate interconnection studies for new generation brought on by new

of the new generation to all PJM load, thus ignoring the intent of the generation to supply the co-

processes.20

C. 
Brattle Report: Integrated Energy Park Interconnection is a Practical 
Solution (Questions d.vii, e.v, f.i, f.iii, and f.iv) 
 
The attached Brattle Report assesses the concept of interconnecting new generation and 
co-located load as a composite facility called an Energy Park.  This is not a unique concept.  The 
Electric Reliability Council of Texas (“ERCOT”) has been doing so for years through its 
approach to interconnecting Private Use Networks.  Brattle describes the process it proposes as 
“Energy Park Integration” (“EPI”) interconnection, where the Energy Park is studied as a 
composite load and generation facility, instead of studying the load in one process and the 
generation in another.23 
Because an Energy Park supplies all or part of its load on-site, when studied as an 
integrated unit, it can be connected to the grid with fewer transmission upgrades (thus 
accelerating the interconnection).  That is not the case today, as there are no existing protocols 
for doing so.  Transmission owners may study the new load component of an Energy Park

The proposed EPI approach by Brattle models the composite behavior of load and 
generation in an Energy Park by accounting for three essential components: the generator, load, 
and a control mechanism that limits net exchanges with the grid.  Net load impacts would be 
limited to just the nominated net grid withdrawal by load, while net injection impacts from the 
generation would be limited to the nominated injection level. 
The Brattle Report demonstrates that the EPI approach can lower transmission impacts 
and interconnection time without sacrificing reliability (and indeed, improving it through 
accelerated integration of new supply).  The report steps through the standard contingency 
studies used for load reliability planning to show that the proposed EPI study approach yields 
lower transmission impacts even considering the contingency cases used in transmission 
planning.  These analyses also show the role of the control mechanism in reducing such 
transmission impacts.25 
The Brattle Report further explains how the EPI study is practical.  ERCOT for one has 
adopted an integrated approach to studying Private Use Networks, representing over 16,000 
MW, a quarter of which was added in the last decade.26  Other regions have considered similar

The PJM “deliverability” studies currently used to study both network and energy-only 
generation resources will require reform to implement an EPI approach for Energy Parks that

Transmission owners studying load likewise would need to adopt processes to incorporate 
assumptions related to the co-located generator in their load studies.  This is possible so long as

could provide for non-firm grid injection requests based on “as available” grid capacity (i.e., less
