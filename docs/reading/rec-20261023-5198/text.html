Geronimo Power supports the creation of efficient and non-discriminatory procedures that provide for the coordinated study of new generation capacity and large load customers. MISO’s LARS proposal does not meet this standard.  As currently conceived, LARS would provide incumbent utilities with undue influence over the ability of interconnection customers to access the process.  Notably, an interconnection customer will not be able to participate in LARS unless the interconnecting transmission owner “pre-approves” its proposed point of interconnection and a MISO transmission customer—which typically will be a transmission owner and load-serving entity (“LSE”)—submits an application into the LARS process.10  The result is a framework that will provide incumbent utilities with new opportunities to discriminate against competing suppliers and secure preferential access to interconnection service for their own generation resources.  Ultimately, this will provide incumbent utilities with a powerful tool that they can use to pressure large load customers to rely on their generation resources, which will be eligible to receive interconnection service more quickly and at lower cost than competing resources that have been relegated to the standard interconnection queue.

At the same time, certain aspects of MISO’s proposed framework appear to arbitrarily exclude from LARS generation resources that are being developed to meet the demand of large load customers in a manner that supports resource adequacy and protects ratepayers.  For instance, the requirement that an interconnection customer provide an offtake agreement demonstrating that its facility will be used to provide energy to large load customers would appear to prevent a generation resource that has entered into a virtual power purchase agreement (“PPA”) with a large load customer from participating in LARS.  Additionally, MISO’s description of the scope of LARS could be interpreted as prohibiting co-located generation and load from accessing the LARS

A. MISO’s Proposal To Allow Incumbent Utilities To Determine Access To
LARS Is Unduly Discriminatory And Will Undermine The Objective Of
Efficiently Integrating Large Loads

The current design of LARS will create opportunities for undue discrimination by providing incumbent utilities with the ability to control which interconnection customers are able to participate in, and obtain service through, the process.  Even if a generation resource has entered into an agreement to serve large load and otherwise meets the criteria to participate, an interconnection customer will be unable to access LARS without the cooperation and support of incumbent utilities within the MISO region.

Indeed, from the beginning of the process, incumbent utilities within MISO would have the ability to create barriers to competing suppliers.  As an initial matter, an interconnection customer would be ineligible to participate in LARS unless the transmission owner that owns the facilities that the customer is planning to interconnect to agrees to the proposed point of interconnection.12  If the interconnection customer and the transmission owner are unable to jointly agree on a point of interconnection at least ten business days prior to the next LARS application deadline, then the interconnection customer would be ineligible for the LARS.13  A transmission-

The risk of undue discrimination is not limited only to incumbent utilities vetoing a proposed point of interconnection or declining to submit an application into the LARS process for a competing supplier.  Because LARS applications are processed on a serial, time-stamped basis and only a limited number of projects may participate in each study period,22 an incumbent utility also can favor its own generation through more subtle means.  For example, an LSE could submit a LARS application for its own generation capacity and an associated large load in advance of submitting a LARS application for a large load customer that has agreed to contract with a competing supplier to ensure that the application for its own resource is higher in the queue and processed more quickly than the application for a competing supplier.

While IPPs will continue to have access to the legacy interconnection queue, this does not mean that LARS is not unduly discriminatory or preferential or consistent with open access.  The prohibition on undue discrimination mandates that LARS be designed in a manner that provides all interconnection customers with comparable access to interconnection service.  A structure that provides incumbent utilities with the ability to discriminate and erect barriers to competing suppliers is inconsistent with this requirement.  And the fact that LARS would be a permanent feature of the MISO market—rather than a one-time process similar to ERAS or similar processes that have been adopted by other markets—only increases the need to scrutinize the proposal to ensure that it does not create opportunities for undue discrimination.

In short, the design of LARS would allow incumbent utilities to play a gatekeeper function that is incompatible with open access and unnecessary to achieve the objectives that LARS seeks to achieve.  Rather than creating opportunities for incumbent utilities to discriminate against competing generation resources, LARS should be designed in a manner that supports the timely entry of new generation capacity that is best positioned to meet the demand of large load customers.  The best way to achieve this objective is to establish a process that is flexible enough to allow large load customers to work with potential suppliers to identify and pursue the generation solutions that most align with their needs. B. LARS Agreement Eligibility Criteria Are Unnecessarily Restrictive And
Unduly Discriminatory

Under MISO’s proposal, a LARS application would need to be accompanied by “an agreement executed by the Interconnection Customer and either the LARS Associated Transmission Customer or the entity that owns or controls the LARS Associated Load evidencing that the output of the Generating Facility for which LARS processing is sought shall be used to provide energy to the LARS Associated Load.”32  MISO proposes to limit the universe of acceptable agreements to a PPA or similar off-take agreement, a build-own-transfer agreement, and, for LSEs or Alternative Retail Electric Suppliers only, other agreements such as special contracts, large load tariff agreements, or retail service agreements.33

“used to provide energy to the LARS Associated Load” has the potential to arbitrarily exclude commercial agreements between generation and loads that align with the objectives underlying LARS, support the reliable integration of large loads into the MISO system, and protect ratepayers. Importantly, given the significant financial and commercial risks associated with entering into long-term supply agreements, utilities increasingly are encouraging large loads to take a more proactive role in identifying, and entering into contracts with, the generation resources needed to meet their needs.  Although these agreements may involve a physical sale of electricity to the large load customer, large load customers and generation resources increasingly are turning to virtual PPAs as another option to bring new capacity online.  Under a virtual PPA, a large load enters into a financially settled arrangement with a generation developer that provides the revenue certainty needed to finance and build a new resource, but does not provide for the transfer of title to energy between the parties.  Yet, it is not clear that a virtual PPA would qualify an IPP’s project for LARS given the requirement that the generator’s output “be used to provide energy” to the large load.34 MISO has not demonstrated that excluding customers that into enter virtual PPAs and similar arrangements from LARS is just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential. A virtual PPA does exactly what LARS is meant to encourage: it de-risks a new generation project and supports the entry of new capacity necessary to offset the resource adequacy requirements associated with new large loads.  Additionally, a virtual PPA can protect ratepayers by ensuring that the large load—rather than the LSE—is responsible for the costs of the generation resource, reducing the risk that customers will be assigned costs for stranded assets.  And since a virtual PPA generally calls for the resource to sell its generation into the market, a resource that is constructed based on a virtual PPA will be available to serve the needs of the region as a whole.

Categorically prohibiting co-located generation and load from participating in LARS would be unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory and preferential.  A co-located generation and load pair is similarly situated to an electrically proximate pair in every respect relevant to LARS: both involve a formal contractual pairing, both are located in the same LRZ, both would be studied together for their combined system impacts, and both are intended to bring new generation online to serve new large load.  The only difference is that the co-located pair is closer together and less likely to trigger network upgrades.  MISO has not identified any material difference between electrically proximate load and co-located load that would justify allowing one to use LARS while excluding the other and it is difficult to see how the greater proximity of co- located load could justify less favorable treatment in a process that uses proximity as its organizing principle.  Excluding co-location arrangements would arbitrarily deny an entire class of large load access to interconnection service through LARS.

Excluding co-location arrangements would also undermine the incentives the Commission directed MISO to pursue in the Show Cause Order and the objectives MISO itself has identified for the LARS process.  Specifically, in the Show Cause Order, the Commission found that co- locating generation with large load or locating it in an electrically proximate area, could reduce “the impacts to the transmission system of interconnecting the generating facility to serve that electrically proximate large load or large co-located load … thereby potentially reducing the need for Network Upgrades, which can accelerate the generator interconnection process.”39 Importantly, the Commission’s findings on this issue made no distinction between electrically
