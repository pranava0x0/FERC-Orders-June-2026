A. PJM’s Stakeholder Process Must Be Reformed

PJM’s current reliance on a consensus-driven stakeholder process in which multiple committees and subcommittees develop, modify, and ultimately approve (or disapprove) of proposals is impairing the ability of PJM to move decisively to address market design issues and ensure reliability for the PJM region.9  Under the existing framework, an individual proposal often is required to navigate layers of committee and task force review before a proposal reaches the senior committees and, ultimately, the board.10  New initiatives, regardless of their importance or the timeline upon which they should be addressed, must generally proceed from an issue charge, through task force or subcommittee deliberations, to standing committee review, before receiving votes from the Markets and Reliability Committee and Members Committee.11  Throughout these stages, stakeholders are encouraged to resolve their differences and develop consensus packages, with proposals often requiring majority or supermajority support before a proposal can advance.12 The existing consensus-based approach ensures that all stakeholders can provide input on relevant proposals and register their objections.  But it also creates opportunities for delay, procedural disputes, the preservation of the status quo, and results in compromise policies that too often fall short of what is necessary to address critical market design issues. B. PJM Should Have All FPA Section 205 Filing Rights Necessary For It To
Administer Its Markets And Plan And Operate Its System
1. PJM’s Section 205 Rights Should Be Expanded To Allow It To Make
Section 205 Filings Respecting The Tariffs That It Administers
Section 205 of the FPA reflects Congress’s determination that public utilities—not FERC or other third parties—should have the unilateral right to propose changes to the rates, terms, and conditions of service that it provides.  As the courts have explained, “Section 205 of the Federal Power Act gives a utility the right to file rates and terms for services rendered with its assets.”17  If FERC or other third parties wish to make changes to the rates, terms, and conditions of service, then they are required to make a filing under Section 206 of the FPA demonstrating that the existing rate is unjust and unreasonable and the proposed alternative is just and reasonable.18

The allocation of Section 205 filing rights in PJM has strayed from this statutory scheme. In PJM, Section 205 filing rights are divided between PJM and the PJM Transmission Owners Committee depending on the governing document and subject matter.  PJM generally has the right to make Section 205 filings proposing changes to its Open Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT”) or the Reliability Assurance Agreement (“RAA”),19 while the PJM Transmission Owners retain exclusive rights over transmission revenue requirements, regional transmission rate design, and recovery of transmission-related costs.20  Additionally, PJM’s ability to make a Section 205 filing respecting the Operating Agreement is conditioned upon approval by a super-majority vote of the

PJM should be responsible for market outcomes and should have the filing rights under Section 205 of the FPA necessary to propose tariff revisions that it believes are required to maintain the efficiency and reliability of the PJM markets. PJM should be granted the FPA Section 205 rights necessary for it to present its preferred reforms, regardless of stakeholder approval, to the Commission.

2. FERC Should Reject Requests To Give States FPA Section 205 Rights
Granting states Section 205 filing rights would be inconsistent with the FPA, whether these rights are exercised directly by the state regulator or indirectly by compelling PJM to file a state’s preferred rates, terms, and conditions of service with FERC.  Section 205 authorizes a public

Indeed, providing states Section 205 filing rights would be inconsistent with the limitations established on FERC’s authority by Congress. As the courts have recognized, the “structure of the Federal Power Act clearly reflects Congress’s intent to exempt governmental entities from the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s” authority.26  Even if PJM were inclined to delegate filing authority to state regulators, placing FERC in the position of passing judgment on rates, terms, and conditions proposed by state actors under Section 205 of the FPA is inconsistent with the intent of Congress and the plain language of the FPA.

There also is no compelling reason that states need to be granted Section 205 rights, as Congress already has granted them ample opportunities to ensure that their views are considered by the Commission.  States can intervene27 in proceedings concerning PJM and lodge protests (or supporting comments) in response to filings with FERC. To be clear, Geronimo Power believes that states have an important perspective that should, as discussed above, be considered as part of a PJM-driven stakeholder process.  But elevating states in a manner that allows them to dictate the Section 205 filings made by PJM is incompatible with the statutory scheme enacted by Congress.  And, as courts have recognized, elevating the role of states in a multi-state region like PJM so that they have effective FPA section 205 filing rights “threatens confusion, possibly chaos” when different states seek to impose different filing demands on PJM.28  To the extent states want to pursue options that differ from PJM’s preferred approach, they have ample opportunity to express these views through existing PJM functions (e.g. the PJM state relations function), by participating in stakeholder meetings and by making filings with FERC.  These avenues, however, must remain consistent with the framework of the FPA and ensure that PJM, as the entity ultimately responsible for operating efficient markets and maintaining a reliable system, has Section 205 filings rights that allow it to carry out its core operations, planning and market functions.

C. PJM Board Service Should Be Limited To Two, Five-Year Terms
Geronimo Power believes that the existing framework for electing members of the PJM board—coupled with their three-year terms—creates a disincentive for board members to make decisions that have the potential to trigger opposition from certain constituencies within the region. Since board members are nominated by a committee of PJM Members and must be approved by the Members Committee, board members have a keen understanding of how their votes, particularly when their term is near expiration, may affect their chances to be re-elected to the board.  This structure has the potential to undermine independent decision-making in a manner that is in tension with the independence that is at the core of PJM’s mandate.

The terms of PJM board members should be modified to mitigate the risk of political pressure and provide board members with greater flexibility to take the actions necessary to serve the interests of the region as a whole.  PJM board members currently serve three-year terms.29 These short three-year terms leave board members that would like to serve longer than three years in a politically sensitive position knowing that any decision they make upon taking their board seat may be held against them by the PJM Nominating Committee or Members Committee if they seek reappointment.  This issue is compounded by the rules allowing board members to be reappointed for additional terms without limit and also risks the entrenchment of such board members with particular interests or perspectives that may not always align with the needs of PJM. Geronimo Power urges PJM and the Commission to extend the term of PJM board

members to five years and limit the ability of board members to serve an additional term to a single additional term.  Establishing a five-year term for PJM board members will further insulate board members from political pressure and provide these individuals with greater latitude to vote for those proposals that they believe align with the longer-term needs of the region.  Moreover, providing board members with the ability to seek only one additional term will better ensure that board members retain their independence and that fresh perspectives are regularly introduced to the board.30  A board member that may only seek reappointment one-time at the conclusion of a five-year term will have more independence to cast votes based solely on what is best for the PJM region as a whole than board members that can seek continual reappointment following short three- year terms.

11) Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes only inform the Board? X

Yes.  PJM must have the right to make unilateral Section 205 filings respecting core RTO functions such as planning, markets, and operations.

An advisory role does not mean that PJM should be free to disregard stakeholder input, but the obligation on the Board to consider stakeholder advice should be modeled on an administrative rulemaking process where parties can submit comments, but the agency (or in this case PJM) ultimately decides. PJM should be required to respond to stakeholder comments, but should retain control over whether a proposal moves forward through the stakeholder process.  Similarly, when the Board makes a decision it should publish the reasons for its decision, including addressing the positions of stakeholders at a high level. This is similar to the process the Commission follows in a rulemaking.

Stakeholders that believe that a market rule needs to be changed will retain their rights under Section 206 to file a complaint with FERC.

14) Should OPSI have “jump ball” rights over resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a supermajority of OPSI agrees on an alternative proposal?
