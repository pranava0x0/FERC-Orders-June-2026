American Electric Power Company, Inc. is one of the largest investor-owned electric public utility holding companies in the United States.  Its electric utility companies provide generation, transmission, and distribution services to more than five million customers in Arkansas, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana, Michigan, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, and West Virginia.  It also partially owns entities that own transmission facilities in states outside of its service territories. American Electric Power Company, Inc.’s affiliates Appalachian Power Company, Indiana Michigan Power Company, Kentucky Power Company, Kingsport Power Company, Ohio Power Company, and Wheeling Power Company are load serving entities (“LSEs”) in PJM. C. AEP’s Comments Focus on Three Proposed Amendments in the IRAS Proposal

AEP’s comments and request for clarification of the IRAS Proposal focus on three specific proposed amendments to PJM’s governing documents:  the definition of New Large Load to be included in the PJM Tariff, section IV(b)(iii) of the new Attachment DD-4 to the PJM Tariff (which is referenced by the New Large Load definition and regulates when that definition is dormant and active), and the revised language in the RAA, Schedule 8.1, section D.1.1., which forms the FRR Capacity Rule.  These proposed amendments are reproduced below for ease of reference:

1. PJM Tariff Definitions, Definition of New Large Load
“New Large Load” shall mean end-use customer load that is either (a) a Large Load that goes into service after June 1, 2027, or (b) incremental load in- service after June 1, 2027, that increases the existing end-use customer load at a single electrical site that, prior to such incremental load, did not previously meet the definition of Large Load.  However, to the extent three consecutive Delivery Years meet the requirements of Tariff, Attachment DD- 4, section IV(b)(iii), then New Large Load shall mean end-use customer load that is either (a) a Large Load that goes into service after June 1 of the first Delivery Year for which the requirements of Tariff, Attachment DD-4, section IV(b)(iii) are not met or (b) incremental load in-service after June 1 of the first Delivery Year for which the requirements of Tariff, Attachment DD-4, section IV(b)(iii) are not met that increases the existing end-use customer load at a single electrical site that, prior to such incremental load, did not previously meet the definition of Large Load.

2. PJM Tariff Attachment DD-4, section IV(b)(iii)
In the event ([the amount of Capacity Resources committed for a given Delivery Year (as determined after the Third Incremental Auction) equals or exceeds the PJM Region Reliability Requirement determined prior to the Third Incremental Auction] divided by the Pool-wide Accredited UCAP Factor), then the Office of the Interconnection does not need any Eligible New Large Loads to be available to provide Interim Resource Adequacy Service for that Delivery Year.

IV. COMMENTS
A. PJM Should Clarify that FRR Requirements Are Intended to Mirror
the RPM Requirement Such That When IRAS is Dormant, the FRR
Capacity Rule is Also Dormant.
The IRAS Proposal provides a mechanism for IRAS to deactivate after three consecutive years of sufficient capacity clearing the RPM auction, as described above in section III.B.  PJM should clarify that the FRR Capacity Rule likewise deactivates when IRAS deactivates.  Since both the IRAS deactivation mechanism and the FRR Capacity Rule hinge on the definition of New Large Load, it should be evident that the FRR Capacity Rule is inactive when IRAS is inactive.  Nonetheless, an explicit clarification will ensure that no future FRR load is subject to capacity restrictions above and beyond those for RPM load.

The IRAS Proposal defines New Large Load as Large Load that goes into service after June 1, 2027.  However, once system conditions are met such that IRAS is no longer necessary, IRAS is deactivated.  When this happens, the definition of New Large Load becomes Large Load that goes into service after June 1 of the next Delivery Year that PJM faces capacity scarcity and IRAS must be reactivated.28

Under the proposed FRR Capacity Rule, “An FRR Entity may not include in its FRR Capacity Plan any MW quantity of Existing Generation Capacity Resource in excess of what such FRR Entity owned or contracted by such FRR Entity prior to June 1, 2027, to serve New Large Load.”  The final clause “to serve New Large Load” clearly modifies the entire sentence.  The inclusion of this phrase demonstrates that the FRR Capacity Rule should be read to only apply to New Large Load as it is defined at that time.  Accordingly, when IRAS is inactive, no new load will meet the definition of New Large Load.  In this circumstance, the FRR Capacity Rule would also effectively be inactive because there would be no identifiable New Large Load for the rule to apply to.

Although it is possible to determine via the proposed PJM Tariff and RAA amendment language that the FRR Capacity Rule is effectively dormant at the same time that IRAS is dormant, the Commission should clarify that this is the case to avoid any confusion in the future.  Without clarification that the FRR Capacity Rule goes dormant simultaneous with IRAS, this aspect of the IRAS Proposal is unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory because it could be used to treat RPM load and FRR load differently

B. PJM Should Clarify that When the Relevant In-Service Date for the
Definition of New Large Load Resets, the Capacity Procurement
Deadline under the FRR Capacity Rule Also Resets.
The fixed date of June 1, 2027 in the FRR Capacity Rule is inconsistent with the flexible in-service date in the proposed definition of New Large Load.  PJM should clarify that when the in-service date for New Large Load resets, the procurement deadline for Existing Generation Capacity Resources under the FRR Capacity Rule likewise resets. PJM’s transmittal letter describes that the FRR Capacity Rule is proposed to ensure that “FRR Entities (or their New Large Loads) bring new capacity to the system to meet the New Large Loads’ resource adequacy needs, and precludes FRR Entities from satisfying load growth from New Large Loads with existing Capacity Resources that could potentially be lured from the RPM Auctions (and further exacerbating the shortage conditions in the capacity market).”29  Thus, the rationale for the FRR Capacity Rule appears to be to establish an obligation for FRR Entities that parallels the IRAS Proposal’s incentives for RPM load to bring its own new capacity.

Under the FRR Capacity Rule as drafted, however, an FRR Entity cannot use Existing Generation Capacity Resources that were procured after June 1, 2027 to serve New Large Load even when IRAS goes dormant and then reactivates.  Thus, as written, the FRR Capacity Rule creates a likely scenario where in future years, if IRAS reactivates after a period of dormancy and the relevant in-service date for the definition of New Large Load has been reset, FRR Entities are still restricted from using Existing Generation Capacity Resources to satisfy New Large Load capacity requirements unless those Existing Generation Capacity Resources were procured prior to June 1, 2027.  This outcome is inconsistent with the intent of the IRAS rule and, if left unclarified, would put FRR Entities at a clear disadvantage to entities participating in the RPM.

Accordingly, PJM should clarify that if the in-service date for New Large Load resets to June 1 of a future year, the cutoff date for procuring Existing Generation Capacity Resources under the FRR Capacity Rule also resets.  If the issue is not clarified, the IRAS Proposal will be unjust, unreasonable and unduly discriminatory with respect to the FRR Capacity Rule.  It will restrict FRR Entities to a greater extent than it would restrict RPM load.  Furthermore, such an inconsistent approach would thwart the aligned, parallel approach to FRR and RPM large load capacity that PJM describes in its transmittal letter as the justification for the novel restriction on the historic ability of FRR Entities to plan holistically for the capacity needs of their loads.

C. PJM Should Clarify How FRR Entities Can Comply with Their
Obligations Under the FRR Capacity Rule After IRAS is Reactivated.
PJM should clarify that an FRR Entity can comply with the FRR Capacity Rule during years where IRAS is reactivated because the IRAS Proposal, as written, does not appear to provide sufficient notice for an FRR Entity to develop a compliant FRR Capacity Plan to provide for New Large Loads in reactivation years.

Under the IRAS Proposal, if IRAS goes dormant, the determination of whether IRAS reactivates in a future delivery year is made pursuant to outcome of the final RPM auction for that delivery year, the Third Incremental Auction.30  This auction occurs only three to four months before the start of the delivery year.31  As described above in section IV.A, the mechanism for IRAS to go dormant and to reactivate is the definition of New Large Load and, because the FRR Capacity Rule hinges on its applicability to New Large Load, the FRR Capacity Rule should go dormant and reactivate at the same time as IRAS.32 Thus, in a year where IRAS reactivates, the FRR Capacity Rule should also reactivate. However, as drafted, the FRR Capacity Rule appears to leave FRR Entities with only three to four months to ensure that their FRR Capacity Plan to serve New Large Load complies with the rule.  This timeline will be difficult, if not impossible, for FRR Entities to comply with because FRR Capacity Plans take years to develop.  FRR Entities must first submit their FRR Capacity Plan for a delivery year at least one month before the Base
