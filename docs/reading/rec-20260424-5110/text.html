I. Introduction

On February 23, 2026, PJM Interconnection, LLC (“PJM”) submitted its initial brief1 and 60-day compliance filing2 in response to the Commission’s December 18, 2025, order on the show cause proceeding.3  Earthjustice appreciates the opportunity to respond to the wide range of comments that stakeholders submitted to the Commission in response to PJM’s filings.4 As PJM and other commenters remark, PJM is tasked with reimagining how it will provide transmission services, among other system changes. To date, PJM’s region has absorbed more load growth from large load customers than any other grid operator in the Commission’s jurisdiction. And projected load growth through 2030 is not slowing down.5

B. The Commission should accept PJM’s proposed penalties because financial
penalties alone are insufficient to ensure that data centers will comply with curtailment directives.

PJM is correct that financial penalties alone are not enough to incentivize data centers to follow curtailment or load shed procedures. Some commenters have suggested that financial penalties could in theory be sufficient to deter data centers from failing to curtail when instructed,24 but no comments actually describe what financial penalty could be sufficient in practice. That notable silence is likely because none of the commenters know what financial penalty could convince large load customers—with virtually unlimited capital and very high contractual uptime requirements—to curtail when directed. Though commenters argue that operational penalties are too strict, they are far more effective than financial penalties. To be effective, financial penalties must be more than merely the cost of doing business for well- financed large loads. However, because data centers are wealthy and have diverse business practices, calculating an effective financial penalty is extremely difficult. Moreover, payments made after the fact will not prevent the harm from non-performance.

Data center spending is growing exponentially, making it increasingly difficult to determine a uniform financial rate that could convince this multi-billion dollar—soon trillion dollar—industry to follow curtailment procedures instead of just paying the penalties. As of December 2025, data center deals hit a new record of $61 billion globally.25 According to McKinsey, global spending on data centers could reach $7 trillion by 2030.26 With data center

Joint Co-Location Developers attempt to compare underperforming capacity resources to the data centers here, arguing that a rate similar to PJM’s Non-Performance Charge for a capacity resource that fails to perform during an emergency would be appropriate and sufficient in this context.37 However, PJM’s penalty charges for poorly performing capacity resources are not a model for a penalty for large loads that abuse new transmission services for three key reasons: (1) PJM’s Capacity Performance structure is ineffective; (2) PJM’s Capacity Performance structure is designed to transfer penalties from underperforming resources to bonus payments for overperforming resources, and there is no comparable structure for the new transmission services; and (3) the risks of nonperformance for PJM’s curtailment procedures are much graver than underperformance of a capacity resource during an emergency event. III. The Commission should require PJM to clarify how and when PJM will enforce its curtailment procedures.

While PJM’s proposed curtailment procedures and penalty structure are generally reasonable, PJM has not provided sufficient detail about implementation. As failures to curtail can cause dire ramifications for the transmission system, the Commission must have clarity on several important points: (1) how PJM will communicate its curtailment directives to Transmission Providers and Data centers; (2) how and when Transmission Providers will determine that load shed is necessary, which loads will be curtailed first, and at what stage curtailments will occur; and (3) how a data center will be disqualified and the timeline for that disqualification.

B. The Commission should require PJM to clarify its curtailment procedures to ensure that PJM will adequately protect the reliability and safety of the transmission system.

In addition to the concerns raised in initial comments, PJM’s initial brief, tariff, and proposed service agreements fail to adequately state (1) how PJM and its Transmission Providers will direct customers to curtail, (2) how and when Transmission Providers will decide that load shed is necessary and how they will choose which specific loads are curtailed first, (3) exactly how and when disqualification will occur, and (4) how PJM will communicate curtailment violations and disqualifications to the public. Without clarity on the issues, the Commission and the public cannot be sure that PJM’s proposal can adequately protect the transmission system. PJM fails to state how it will actually communicate its orders to the Transmission Providers and how the Transmission Providers will actually direct their customers to curtail. PJM’s initial brief states that PJM will issue a directive to curtail, and data centers must implement that directive and respond to any real-time instruction to curtail within five minutes.55 PJM’s proposed tariff further establishes that “the Transmission Provider will notify the data center of any Curtailment in a timely manner” and that any requests to curtail will be consistent with Good Utility Practice.56 Beyond that, PJM’s filings are silent as to how these directives will be made known to data centers and what those directives will say. The Commission should require PJM to clarify what the content of these directives will be and how these directives will be communicated to data centers.

IV. The Commission should decline to require that PJM rush the implementation of protection mechanisms for its new non-firm transmission services.

The Commission should approve PJM’s requested timeline to implement new
transmission services and accompanying RAS and Special Protection Schemes (SPS). Though there is a strong push from some commenters to rush this process, the Commission must ensure that PJM does not sacrifice the reliability of the transmission system to fulfill data centers’ near- term power demands. The Commission has already expressed concern about the reliability and resource adequacy implications of Co-Location Arrangements, stating in its December order that “the speed at which co-located load can be added to the grid may pose particular resource adequacy challenges, especially if co-location arrangements are not adequately and transparently reflected in existing RA planning mechanisms.”63 Similarly, PJM has acknowledged that its new transmission services and protection schemes could threaten the reliability of the transmission system. V. The Commission should require PJM to ensure that rules governing generator interconnection studies and behind-the-meter generation are consistent with the Commission’s Order on the Show Cause Proceeding and Order No. 2023.

 PJM’s proposed rules are unjust and unreasonable because PJM’s proposed generator interconnection study and behind-the-meter generation (“BTMG”) rules fail to comply with the Commission’s Order on the Show Cause Proceeding and Order No. 2023. To ensure that PJM’s rules are consistent with the Commission’s Orders, the Commission should: (1) require PJM to clarify that hybrid resources can nominate their net withdrawal limitations for purposes of the interconnection study process and (2) require PJM to set a materiality threshold for the amount of load a generator can net using BTMG.

The Commission ordered PJM to amend its tariff because it did not include conditions of service for co-location arrangements that are willing and able to limit their withdrawals from the transmission system. B. Initial comments correctly support revising PJM’s Retail-BTMG rules to be consistent with the Commission’s Order on the Show Cause Proceeding.

Initial comments also urge the Commission to require PJM to change its proposed BTMG rules because PJM’s proposal is unjust and unreasonable. PJM’s BTMG rules allow loads to reduce their costs by netting their output from BTMG to determine their transmission charges.74 PJM’s proposed threshold limits retail-BTMG to generation that has a cumulative nameplate rating of no greater than 50 MW.75 PJM’s proposed threshold is materially different from the threshold that FERC ordered because PJM set a MW threshold for the size of generator that can participate in netting rather than a threshold for the amount of load a generator can net. Generators argue that PJM’s nameplate capacity cap unnecessarily limits the types of generators that can participate in BTMG netting.76 Resources that would be excluded under PJM‘s current proposal are technically capable of providing retail-BTMG and should be able to do so.77 Unlike PJM’s proposed nameplate capacity threshold, the Commission’s load netting threshold would apply fairly across all resource types. The Commission should reject PJM’s proposed BTMG rules and require PJM to set a materiality threshold that is consistent with the Commission’s Order on the Show Cause Proceeding.
