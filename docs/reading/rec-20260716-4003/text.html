Recommendations for PJM Governance and Stakeholder Process Reform 
Pat Wood III, past Chairman, Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and the Public Utility Commission of Texas; 
Executive Chairman, Hunt Energy Network 
PJM has served the country well in its 99 years of existence.  Its complex, integrated power market has been a global 
hallmark of reliability, innovation and economic development.  As one of its former federal regulators, I know PJM’s 
history and people. For the past generation, PJM has ably addressed numerous challenges as they arose. But with the 
explosion of power demand in the past few years, numerous issues have burst to the front burner at once: construction 
and interconnection of new resources, strengthening of the transmission grid, data center load treatment, distributed 
resource utilization, flexible customer demand, and end-use customer affordability. I have recently visited with a 
number of PJM Stakeholders and government officials about these matters.  There is consensus that a more nimble, 
transparent process is needed for PJM to effectively and timely manage these challenges.   
My recommendations below draw on a generation of experience by the seven US RTOs and ISOs— each of which has 
practices that have worked well over time.   Implementing some of these recommendations at PJM will require 
amendments to PJM’s governing documents.  
The Federal Power Act (FPA) establishes a federal-state partnership in electricity regulation. RTOs perform functions 
that span both domains.  Alignment between both sovereigns supports a more efficient and durable foundation for 
customers, investors, utilities and suppliers. Of the four multi-state RTOs, I view PJM states’ voice as the most muted. 
1. Establish bifurcated state engagement channels. OPSI serves as PJM’s Regional State Committee (RSC) for 
technical regulatory engagement.  I recommend mirroring the bifurcated New England model to set up a 
complementary PJM States’ Committee on Electricity (PJMSCOE) to foster PJM alignment with state policy/economic 
development perspectives. PJMSCOE would have formal standing at PJM, a defined right to bring issues directly to the 
Board, and a duty to appoint three state members to the current eight-member Board Nominating Committee, including 
the CEO as a voting member.  (It may determine that one of these appointees should be the OPSI President).   
2. Clarify the RSC’s authority. I recommend establishing binding consultative authority for OPSI on matters where 
state jurisdictional authority is clearest: system planning (generation, DERs, transmission), demand side participation 
and resource adequacy program structure.  (I note that Order No. 1920 has already given state regulators a formal role 
in cost allocation of regional transmission.  Like all other RTO/ISOs, PJM should have the rights to submit regional 
transmission planning-relating filings under section 205).  PJM would provide the resources to support the states’ work 
on these issues.  PJM may not file changes at FERC in these categories without first soliciting OPSI’s timely vote and, 
if there is disagreement, PJM would include OPSI’s load-weighted position in its filing.   
3. Enhance transparency and accountability. I recommend PJM’s governing charter clearly state that its Board 
members have a responsibility to the public interest, consistent with the FPA. PJM should adopt public annual/quarterly 
performance metrics and tie management compensation to their achievement (as in MISO). Results for votes taken by 
stakeholders should be published. Board ex parte communication practices should be revisited to allow for more direct 
discussions with customers, stakeholders, elected officials and regulators, with appropriate disclosure. All Board ethics 
documents should be published on the PJM public website.  For public legitimacy, the Board Nominating Committee 
should consider selection of more Board members who live in PJM’s large region.   
4. Simplify the decision-making process. A healthy stakeholder process is important contributor to reasoned decision-
making. But it cannot always fully substitute for the PJM Board Members’ determination of the public interest. In 
every other US RTO and ISO, various stakeholder committee structures are used to provide input to the Boards, but the 
Boards make independent decisions on the matters before them.  This process works reasonably efficiently and well, 
and I recommend it for PJM.  This transition to a stakeholder advisory role should be paired with a more structured pre-
Board Meeting engagement between the Members Committee and the Board, akin to that employed in the SPP.  [If it is 
decided to retain the existing structure, I recommend adopting a bifurcated supermajority threshold: continue to require 
a two-thirds majority for governance issues, and a simple majority for everything else.  The voting makeup of lower 
committees should mirror that of the Members Committee.]  
A final thought. The capacity-market price outcomes became politically intolerable in significant part because default-
service retail load across the footprint was not adequately hedged — the risk the Western markets learned in 1999-
2000. The open states will need either to finish the job on retail competition, or to require default suppliers to manage 
risk as competitive suppliers do; either way, PJM retains a role in managing the residual market for capacity and 
allocating its costs. And every state needs the ability to allocate those costs across customer classes consistent with the 
Governors’ Statement of Principles and the data centers’ Ratepayer Protection Pledge to the President.   
I believe these focused governance reforms, derived from best practices across the nation, would allow PJM to work 
more effectively. Big issues await PJM's and the states' actions. A swift resolution will enable all to focus on the future.
