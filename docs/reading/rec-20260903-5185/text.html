IRAS would be a service utilized solely during periods when there are insufficient resources to reliably meet demand in real time operations.

RESA files these Comments to express the need for market certainty, stability and clarity and the importance of the granular allocation of costs using principles of cost causation. C. Motion to Intervene
On August 13, 2026, PJM made a filing to modify the RAA provisions of its Tariff to
impose IRAS that would enable PJM to prioritize load reduction in zones/areas where New Large Loads have not brought their own new capacity or had their capacity needs covered through the Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”).  New Large Loads would be able to avoid the

condition on taking transmission service by bringing new capacity to the system.  According to PJM, the IRAS proposal is within the Commission’s jurisdiction, but acknowledges that the states will have a role in load reduction.

RESA, with members who are active in PJM markets, have an interest in this proceeding
that cannot be represented by any other party, moves to intervene.

PJM attempts to distinguish compensation under IRAS from other load reduction by alleging that current rapid load growth in Large Loads is different than historical load growth, “in both their inherent concentration of demand and the rapidity at which their cumulative demands have grown throughout PJM.”8 In short, under the IRAS, a New Large Load can interconnect, but must either bring its own capacity or provide IRAS. While these Large Loads may be a primary reason for the rapid load growth in PJM, interconnecting and receiving service before there is capacity to support the New Large Load is the benefit that Large Loads receive by participating in PJM before such capacity is available to support them.  There should be no reason to also compensate the Large Load for being curtailed when that is the result of the benefit of their bargain.  Moreover, PJM’s proposed compensation methodology, along with the uncertainty as to the actual rate that will be paid once each state makes that determination, creates significant market uncertainty.  Section X(b)(ii) of the IRAS Tariff clearly states that LES’s will incur charges to compensate large loads for reductions.9  This risk cannot be appropriately hedged or managed by an LSE and, therefore, adds uncertainty to the market.  Any compensation to a large load customer is a penalty to the customers in that Electric Distributor’s Zone.

B. Market Certainty, Transparency and Stability are Critical to Market
Participants, such as RESA Members
RESA members purchase services in the RTO/ISO markets such as energy, capacity and ancillary services, but they offer more than just electricity service to their customers.  Competitive retail suppliers may provide retail energy to end-use customers at contracted fixed or variable prices which are tied to wholesale electricity prices.  Retail suppliers may offer demand response services – assisting customers in reducing peak demand and receiving benefits for reducing consumption at peak times.  Retail suppliers may offer energy efficiency services to assist customers in reducing their electricity consumption.  Retail suppliers may offer electricity with a certain component of renewable energy – or all renewable energy.  There is no one model – 11

competitive wholesale markets and the introduction of retail choice in states such as Ohio, Illinois, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and Maryland, have allowed significant innovation, which has reduced consumption of energy, lowered prices, and provided valuable services to consumers. Competitive retail suppliers operate in an environment of risk.  Retail suppliers are unlike traditional utilities in several important ways.  For example, retail suppliers are not cost-based regulated by state public utility commissions (although they comply with a myriad of state requirements to be authorized to provide energy-related products and services to retail customers). Retail suppliers do not have a mechanism to defer costs, like traditional utilities, and thus bear the risk of wholesale market volatility and uncertainty.  Retail suppliers do not have a specified service area.  They contract with customers bilaterally.  Generally, these contracts are long-term – of a duration of one to five years. As a result, when a contract expires, it may or may not be renewed. Thus, a customer today may not be a customer tomorrow.  As a result, charges allocated to a utility service area or load zone must be properly allocated to the appropriate retail end use customer, even if the customer has changed suppliers or returned to default service (i.e. the customer is served by different LSEs).

To offer valuable and innovative services at competitive prices, retail suppliers participating in competitive wholesale markets require market certainty, transparency and stability. Market certainty, transparency and stability allow retail suppliers to offer products and services that they know they can deliver during the term of the applicable contract.  The importance of having confidence in the competitive wholesale market cannot be overstated.  As with products and services offered by retail suppliers, the contracts vary by company, by service, by customer type, by term and other conditions.
