POST TECHNICAL CONFERENCE COMMENTS OF DOMINION ENERGY 
SERVICES, INC. 
 
Dominion Energy Services, Inc. (“DES”), on behalf of Virginia Electric and Power 
Company doing business as Dominion Energy Virginia (“Dominion Energy Virginia”), submits 
the following comments in response to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or 
“Commission”) Notice Inviting Post-Technical Conference Comments issued on the July 30, 2026 
in this proceeding (“Notice”).  Dominion Energy Virginia supports the Commission’s focus on 
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) governance reforms to strengthen PJM’s independence as 
well as explore ways to improve its stakeholder process so that PJM can quickly respond to 
emerging issues that require crucial reforms to maintain reliability of the bulk electric system.1 
I. 
Introduction 
DES is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Dominion Energy, Inc., and is a centralized services 
company providing administrative, management and support services to its associate companies.  
Dominion Energy Virginia, a subsidiary of Dominion Energy, Inc., is a public service 
corporation organized under the laws of the Commonwealth of Virginia and a public utility subject to 
regulation by the Commission and does business in the Commonwealth of Virginia and in the State of 
North Carolina. It is a regulated, vertically integrated public utility engaged in the generation,

Comments 
1) Dominion Energy Virginia supports governance reforms that would reinforce PJM’s 
independence as a core governance principle.  
 
PJM’s independence is critical for operating a reliable regional grid across a diverse multi-state 
footprint and for executing its responsibilities as an Independent System Operator and Regional 
Transmission Organization.  PJM must remain independent from any single market participant, sector, 
state or group of states, consumer group, industry, or governmental entity. Panelists that participated 
in the July 23 technical conference and other parties broadly agree that establishing greater PJM 
independence is a priority and one of the objectives should be to define the appropriate role for the

Section 205 filing rights over its resource adequacy, electric transmission planning protocols and 
energy and ancillary service rules, 2) establishing a limited jump-ball mechanism for PJM Members 
only, and 3) implementing changes that would provide the states the means to more effectively engage 
in the PJM stakeholder process. Each of these recommendations will be discussed in more detail below.

PJM currently has FPA, Section 205 filing rights over its capacity market pursuant to the PJM 
Open Access Transmission Tariff (“PJM Tariff”) and Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load 
Serving Entities in the PJM Region (“RAA”).  However, PJM does not have that same authority over 
other core responsibilities that include its electric transmission planning protocols because they reside 
in the Amended and Restated Operating Agreement of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM Operating 
Agreement”) and PJM Members have FPA, Section 205 rights under the PJM Operating Agreement. 
Since its transmission planning protocols reside in the PJM Operating Agreement, PJM is unable to 
ask the Commission to modify its transmission planning protocols under a Section 205 filing without 
receiving a 2/3 supermajority approval vote by its members.    PJM also effectively lacks FPA, Section 
205 filing rights for its energy and ancillary service markets. While these market rules reside in the 
PJM Tariff, the identical provisions are also in the PJM Operating Agreement. Therefore, if PJM 
Members do not authorize PJM to make changes to duplicative sections in the PJM Operating 
Agreement with a 2/3 supermajority vote in favor of the changes, PJM cannot submit changes under a 
single FPA, Section 205 filing.  This division not only hinders PJM’s ability to timely present necessary 
reforms to the Commission, but it also creates a precarious situation in which, without the approval of 
the Members Committee, PJM would be “required to proceed under the more restrictive requirements 
of section 206.”7 This, in effect, can “limit the ability of important issues to reach the Commission for 
adjudication in a timely manner.”8 
By making the PJM stakeholder votes advisory, stakeholder and member filibusters would no 
longer prevent PJM from cohesively bringing important market, planning, and reliability reforms to 
FERC under the FPA, Section 205 for timely consideration.  Creating a process by which the Members 
Committee votes become advisory, which follows from the enhancement of PJM’s FPA, Section 205

rights, would still preserve transparency by showing PJM, stakeholders and the Commission where

3) The Commission should remove restrictions on PJM’s ability to exercise filing rights over 
its energy and ancillary service markets as well as its transmission planning protocols.  
 
Electric planning protocols and reliability procedures in both the PJM Tariff and PJM 
Operating Agreement require PJM to file the proposed changes under FPA, section 206 if Members 
do not provide the 2/3 supermajority vote in favor of the changes. One way Members Committee votes 
can be made advisory is by removing all non-governance provisions from the PJM Operating 
Agreement and migrating those provisions to the PJM Tariff to the extent they are not already 
contained therein.  The only requirements that should remain in the PJM Operating Agreement are 
those that pertain to the management and operation of PJM as is typical for most limited liability 
companies.   Moving the transmission planning protocols or reliability provisions in the PJM Operating 
Agreement to the PJM Tariff, similar to how other Regional Transmission Organizations and 
Independent System Operators have such provisions in their open access transmission tariffs, will 
enable more efficient decision-making processes, while maintaining robust stakeholder engagement 
and transparency.9  This migration of the non-governance provisions would then provide PJM with 
unconditioned FPA, Section 205 filing rights over its energy and ancillary services markets, 
transmission planning protocols, and reliability provisions.  Making this change will allow PJM to

4) Stakeholder rights can be preserved by granting PJM members with limited “jump ball” 
rights for capacity, energy and ancillary service market reforms.  
 
Once the Members Committee vote is made advisory, the stakeholders’ ability to develop 
alternative solutions can be retained using a “jump-ball” mechanism for reforms of the capacity, energy 
and ancillary services market rules.10  PJM members will retain the ability to approve alternative 
proposals that could ultimately be filed by PJM under its Section 205 rights with equal footing before 
the Commission under limited circumstances in which PJM and its members cannot achieve consensus 
on specific reforms. As part of the stakeholder process, the states, for example, through OPSI, may 
also bring forward alternatives for the Members Committee to consider, which if endorsed, could be 
submitted to the Commission on equal footing as the PJM proposal for consideration. A “jump-ball” 
option does several things.  First, it creates an incentive for PJM to continue to work with stakeholders 
to reach a consensus on a reform of the PJM Tariff while still enabling it to submit changes to be timely 
adjudicated by the Commission.  Second, it preserves members’ ability to develop competing proposals 
under the FPA, Section 205 standard of review in which the Commission would only have to deem the

5) Granting states authority to direct FPA, Section 205 filings would undermine PJM’s 
independence.   
 
Dominion Energy Virginia opposes granting states authority to direct FPA, Section 205 filings 
over PJM Tariff matters because it would undermine PJM’s ability to act independently.    PJM should 
take into consideration input from the states and stakeholders regarding when tariff and market rule 
reforms are needed, but ultimately it is PJM that must prioritize where and when changes are most 
needed to meet its obligations as the Regional Transmission Organization and Independent System 
Operator.  Unfettered ability of the states to direct PJM to make a Section 205 filing risks PJM’s 
independence and could result in PJM  being sidetracked from its core responsibilities and may disrupt 
progress on important priority issues if it is forced devote resources to an issue given priority by a 
majority of PJM states.  Additionally, state majorities may not reflect the interests or regulatory 
structures of all states in the PJM Region or benefit the “market” as a whole but rather may be self-
interested. A central concern is that allowing a majority of states to direct a Section 205 filing to change 
the PJM Tariff could result in advancing proposals harmful to minority-state interests, including 
vertically integrated states such as Virginia that have different retail regulatory structures, planning 
obligations, and resource adequacy responsibilities than restructured states, or fail to align across all 
14 jurisdictions in the PJM Region. The better approach is to strengthen OPSI’s ability to coordinate 
state views, provide technical input, and present majority and minority state positions to PJM and

FERC. State input should inform PJM decision-making and FERC review but should not displace 
PJM’s independent regional role or create new procedural veto points. 
 
6) OPSI can improve its efficacy within the PJM stakeholder process through greater 
coordination between state commissioners, legislators and governors. 
 
The PJM states play an integral role in PJM’s stakeholders processes.  OPSI is an organization 
consisting of state commissions, including the District of Columbia, that was established in 2005 and 
has played a crucial role as stakeholder advocates for solutions that it deems in the public interest.11  
OPSI regularly meets with the PJM Board of Managers and also actively participates in the PJM 
stakeholder process to weigh in to share the perspective of its members with PJM and PJM members. 
The Commission accepted a PJM Member-funding mechanism in the same year to enable the 
organization’s ability to effectively share public policy perspectives. OPSI has the staff, technical 
expertise, legal support, and analytical capability to engage more effectively in PJM matters.  
Dominion Energy Virginia is open to discussing ways to support OPSI’s efforts and potentially 
expanded role.  Additionally, OPSI should collect and reflect input from both state utility commissions, 
state governors and their designated energy policy representatives, while the states work to further 
coordinate and collaborate among their respective structures (e.g. governor, public utility commission, 
legislators) that will provide a more cohesive state perspective and reduce conflicting messages where 
possible. By doing so, OPSI can serve as the single authorized representative for providing the 
collective state perspectives, reducing the risk that PJM receives conflicting or duplicative direction 
from multiple state entities on behalf of each state. Where there is no unanimity, OPSI can present 
majority and minority state perspectives where consensus cannot be reached, so that stakeholders, PJM 
and FERC, can understand the range of state views. This would not prevent states from sharing their

APPENDIX  
 
Dominion Energy Virginia provides the following answers to the following questions that 
were provided in the FERC supplemental notice for comments.   
PJM Board Independence 
1. Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years?: No, however we 
support modest term extensions if they enhance continuity and independence, provided 
there are reasonable total service limits and regular performance review. 
2. Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may serve (e.g., 
10 years)? Yes, a term limit should be established of no more than 10 years to balance 
institutional knowledge with fresh perspectives. 
3. Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a preference for 
some Board members that reside in the PJM region? No, however we support 
considering regional knowledge as one factor, but avoid residency requirements that 
narrow the pool of independent, qualified candidates. 
4. Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off period for 
Board members from two years to one year? No, the cooling off period should not be 
reduced; independence should remain paramount. 
5. Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable  
more communication between the Board and stakeholders? Yes, we support more 
structured communication between the Board and stakeholders, with safeguards to 
preserve independence and avoid undue influence. 
6. In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the Board 
be required to explain its reasoning? No, Board votes should not be made public, but 
we do support a greater explanation of Board reasoning with respect to their votes, 
particularly on major market, reliability, and governance reforms; publicizing individual 
votes may be less important than explaining the basis for decisions. 
7. In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or available 
to the public? Yes, we support limited public or open sessions on broad policy issues, 
while preserving confidential executive sessions for commercially sensitive personnel, 
litigation, cybersecurity, or reliability-sensitive matters.  
8. Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the 
responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest? No, but we 
support clarifying that PJM and its Board serve the public interest through reliable,

efficient, nondiscriminatory regional transmission service and wholesale markets 
consistent with the Federal Power Act.  
9. Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the Nominating 
Committee? Yes, we support one OPSI-appointed state representative in a defined role, 
provided the process preserves Board independence and avoids control by any state or 
group of states. 
10. Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management compensation 
to their achievement? Yes, we support public performance metrics focused on 
reliability, market efficiency, project execution, and transparency, but compensation 
design should remain within the Board’s oversight.  
Section 205 Filing Rights 
11. Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes only inform the 
Board? Yes, we support making stakeholder votes advisory so PJM can bring timely 
proposals to FERC, while preserving transparent voting records and meaningful 
stakeholder input. 
12. Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over the energy market and ancillary 
services unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of the 
Members Committee? Yes, we support PJM having FPA, Section 205 filing rights over 
energy and ancillary service market rules without the need to obtain a 2/3 super majority 
vote of the Members Committee. PJM needs authority to respond quickly to changing 
operational conditions, market inefficiencies, and reliability risks. 
13. Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over Regional Transmission Expansion 
Planning (RTEP) unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-
thirds of the Members Committee?: Yes, we support PJM having FPA, Section 205 
filing rights over RTEP without the need to obtain a 2/3 super majority vote of the 
Members Committee with appropriate transparency and consultation. PJM must be able 
to adapt planning rules and protocols quickly as load growth, generator retirements, 
interconnection needs, and regional infrastructure requirements evolve. 
14. Should the Organization of PJM States Inc (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights over 
resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a supermajority of 
OPSI agrees on an alternative proposal?:  No, we do not support granting OPSI direct 
jump ball filing rights as a substitute for PJM independence. Instead, strengthen OPSI 
funding and consultation rights and allow OPSI to provide majority and minority state 
views to PJM and FERC. 
15. Should the Members Committee have “jump ball” filing rights over the portions of 
the tariff where PJM has 205 filing rights when a supermajority of stakeholders 
approves an alternative proposal? Yes, we support a Members Committee with jump 
ball filing rights over defined resource adequacy and energy and ancillary services 
reforms when a supermajority supports an alternative. The possibility of a “jump-ball”

filing on an issue will provide incentive for both PJM and stakeholders to strive for 
consensus on a single proposal to obviate the risk of the Commission choosing one 
proposal over the other.   
 
Stakeholder Process 
16. Should the approval threshold in the Members Committee be lowered from two-
thirds sector-weighted voting?: No, if a “jump-ball” mechanism is adopted by PJM, a 
lower threshold could undermine PJM’s ability to present reforms and the Commission’s 
ability to act decisively on emergent issues by unnecessarily increasing the number of 
“jump-ball” filings  
17. Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing 
committees, also apply to lower-level committees? No, however we support consistent 
affiliate voting safeguards where votes have meaningful governance consequences, while 
preserving broad technical participation at lower-level committees. 
Stakeholder Process 
1. In addition to OPSI, should there be a second state representative group consisting 
of governors’ representatives and/or state legislators’ representatives? No, we do not 
support creating a separate state organization. Instead, we recommended that PJM and 
stakeholders encourage state governors to work with OPSI to include structured input 
from governors or their designated representatives. State governors should work with the 
existing OPSI entity that has been participating in the PJM stakeholder process for many 
years and is well versed in the issues that come before PJM and its membership for 
consideration. 
2. Should state representatives have a formal voting role in the stakeholder process 
(limited to certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional transmission cost 
allocation) in addition to the consumer advocate votes? No, a formal state voting role 
in the stakeholder process could distort sector balance or enable a majority of states to 
override minority-state concerns. State views should be elevated through OPSI 
consultation, not through direct voting control. 
3. Should PJM be required to consult with OPSI prior to making a section 205 filing 
related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation? Yes, we support 
requiring meaningful consultation with OPSI before PJM makes Section 205 filings 
related to resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation, while preserving PJM’s 
ability to file timely proposals and FERC’s authority to decide contested issues.  This will 
not be a huge departure from what PJM has been doing since OPSI was created – taking 
OPSI’s input (when provided) into consideration prior to submitting filing with the 
Commission.
