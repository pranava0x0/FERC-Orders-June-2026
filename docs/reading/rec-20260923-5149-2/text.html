5. Those explanations do not identify the residual loss the buyer-side collateral is intended to secure. PJM instead explains the initial $1.5 million per MW as approximately fifty percent of the gross fifteen-year contractual value at $555/MW- day. PJM Answer at 87–88; Kelly Aff. ¶ 26. That arithmetic explains the source of the figure. It does not establish that the figure corresponds to the loss that would remain after an LSE default, application of collateral, reallocation under Tariff section 18.9(a), and any further default allocation under Operating Agreement section 15.2.2.

6. PJM’s illustrative allocation assigns the NOVEC zone 1,302.6 RBP UCAP MW, or approximately 19.07 percent of the Initial RBP Target, producing a collateral requirement of approximately $1.954 billion at the filed Credit Rate. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 17–18; Rollins Aff. at 2:15–17. As Mr. Rollins explains, that amount exceeds the assets on NOVEC’s balance sheet. Rollins Aff. at 3:7–8.

7. My principal opinion is therefore methodological. Before a buyer-side Credit Rate is selected, the credit framework should identify the residual loss being secured, the population that bears it, the quantity of MW to which the requirement applies, how the amount changes as RPM prices and remaining tenor change, and the standards for recalculating and releasing collateral. The filed construct and PJM’s subsequent explanations do not yet establish those relationships.

II. PJM HAS IDENTIFIED A PAYMENT STREAM, NOT THE RESIDUAL LOSS TO BE SECURED

9. Collateral is a credit-risk instrument. In my original NOVEC affidavit, I distinguished counterparty credit risk from the separate economic risk that forecast large load does not materialize. Counterparty credit risk is the risk that an LSE fails to pay an RBP Charge; the parties exposed are PJM and, through the default provisions, other market participants. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 32–34. The amount of collateral should be related to that loss.

10. PJM now states that the buyer-side requirement is intended to cover the full forward RBP payment obligation, including the RPM component that Peak Market Activity does not prospectively collateralize over an up-to-fifteen-year term. PJM Answer at 87. It also states that $1.5 million per MW is approximately fifty percent of the notional contractual value at the maximum $555/MW-day RBP price. Id. at 87–88; Kelly Aff. ¶ 26.

11. At $555/MW-day, fifteen years of gross payments equal $3,038,625 per MW. Fifty percent is $1,519,312.50 per MW. The filed $1.5 million figure is therefore approximately 49.4 percent of that gross amount. But the RBP does not settle as a single fifteen-year payment at inception. Load pays the RPM clearing price through ordinary capacity settlement and pays or receives the difference between the RBP commitment price and the RPM clearing price through the RBP settlement mechanism.

PDF page 4

12. Gross contractual value therefore is not itself a measure of loss on default. The relevant credit exposure depends on when a default occurs, what amounts remain payable, what RPM prices have been established, what collateral has already been posted and applied, what amounts are reallocated under section 18.9(a), and what residual, if any, reaches the broader PJM membership.

13. PJM’s Answer describes that default consequence in two ways. In responding to EKPC, PJM states that an unpaid RBP Charge is reallocated among other LSEs already responsible for RBP Charges. PJM Answer at 67 n.205. In defending the magnitude of the credit requirement, PJM states that the approach “balances the protection of Members against the effects of LSE default,” citing Operating Agreement section 15.2.2. Id. at 84 & n.252. Those provisions may describe successive levels of a default waterfall. Their relationship determines the residual loss. See EKPC Reply Aff. ¶¶ 18–24.

14. PJM addressed the same question during the stakeholder process. Its April 2026 design presentation states, under the heading “Default Allocation,” that “[i]f a default occurs as a result of a nonpayment of a RBP charge, that amount would be handled as a default allocation assessment under the existing OA Section 15.2.2,” and described that treatment as consistent with the existing RPM market. April 17 Design Presentation at 47. That statement indicates that unpaid RBP Charges were expected to reach the broader membership through the same mechanism that applies to other market defaults.

15. NOVEC carries approximately nineteen percent of the Initial RBP Target. Its original affidavit explained that a failure to post collateral or a payment default at that scale would not remain confined to the cooperative. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 14(h), 68–69. The size of the residual loss, and who bears it, therefore should be identified before the amount of collateral intended to absorb it is selected.

16. In my opinion, a formula based on approximately one-half of gross contractual value does not establish that relationship. Additional procedures should first define what remains unpaid after the RBP settlement, collateral application, section 18.9(a) reallocation, and any Operating Agreement section 15.2.2 allocation. Only then can a buyer-side Credit Rate be evaluated against the loss it is intended to secure.

III. PJM’S CREDIT-MIGRATION ANALYSIS DOES NOT SUPPORT A UNIFORM RATE
17. PJM defends a Credit Rate that does not vary with an obligor’s credit condition in part by reference to credit migration. PJM extrapolates a ten-year S&P Global corporate rating-transition study to fifteen years and reasons that an individualized credit approach could require additional collateral as an LSE’s creditworthiness deteriorates. PJM Answer at 81–83 & Table 2.

PDF page 5

18. Credit migration over a fifteen-year horizon is a legitimate credit consideration. But the fact that credit quality can change supports periodic measurement and adjustment; it does not explain why the obligor’s credit condition should be ignored.

19. NOVEC illustrates the problem. Assuming an annual carrying cost of 100 basis points, the requirement would cost approximately $19.5 million per year, approximately 94 percent of NOVEC’s 2025 patronage capital and operating margins. At 150, 200, and 300 basis points, the carrying cost rises to approximately 141, 188, and 281 percent of that margin, respectively. Mr. Rollins further explains the consequences for NOVEC’s credit rating, its ability to transact bilaterally, and its capacity to borrow for infrastructure. Rollins Aff. at 3:7-20.

20. A generalized corporate transition matrix does not measure the effect of imposing collateral comparable to or exceeding an obligor’s balance sheet. A requirement of that magnitude can consume liquidity, encumber assets, absorb credit-facility capacity, and impose recurring financing expense. The requirement can therefore affect the very credit quality the migration analysis is used to predict. EKPC Reply Aff. ¶¶ 25–30.

21. PJM already has current, participant-specific information concerning NOVEC. PJM bills NOVEC, observes its payment performance, measures exposure under Attachment Q, monitors internal and external credit ratings, and receives financial information through its existing credit processes. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 25–27. At December 31, 2025, NOVEC reported approximately $34.4 million of cash collateral posted to PJM against its existing market activity. Id.

22. The proposed RBP requirement changes that result by a factor of approximately 57. The original affidavit compared approximately $34.4 million of existing PJM collateral with approximately $1.954 billion of incremental RBP collateral. Id. ¶¶ 10(d), 27.

23. PJM also uses a term-sensitive methodology on the seller side of the same RBP construct. The seller requirement is tied to a defined period of exposure, is discounted, declines as the commitment runs off, and permits reductions that take account of participant credit quality. My EKPC reply affidavit compares that methodology with the buyer requirement and explains that, at the same $555/MW- day price, the seller posts $121,545 per MW while the buyer posts $1.5 million per MW. EKPC Reply Aff. ¶¶ 28–30.

24. I do not suggest that the buyer and seller risks are identical or that the same numerical requirement should apply to both. The comparison shows that long-term collateral need not be fixed or uniform. PJM’s seller methodology discounts the exposure, reflects remaining tenor, and permits reductions that take account of credit quality. PJM’s migration analysis does not explain why those variables should be omitted from the buyer-side methodology.

25. In my opinion, any additional procedures should permit the buyer-side requirement to respond to changes in both the obligor and the exposure over time. A

requirement intended to address possible future deterioration should not itself create a present liquidity stress by ignoring the obligor’s financial capacity.

IV. THE OPT-OUT DISTINCTION DOES NOT TRACK THE RISK IT IS SAID TO
ADDRESS
26. I addressed the opt-out at paragraphs 12(f), 35, 52 through 57 and 74 of my original affidavit and do not repeat that analysis here. PJM’s Answer now explains the provision and applies it to another Virginia cooperative. Proposed Tariff, Attachment DD, section 18.1(d) permits certain Electric Distributors to elect out of the RBP. In its Answer, PJM explains the provision as addressing “a limited regulatory gap faced by cooperatives and municipal utilities whose rates are not regulated by the states,” on the reasoning that where no state regulatory authority is responsible for addressing the reallocation of RBP costs, those utilities’ customers are “at risk of absorbing the costs of an ‘overbuy’ if a particular large load fails to materialize.” PJM Answer at 30 (citing July 31 Filing, Transmittal Letter at 45).

27. PJM concludes that the opt-out “should appropriately be limited only to those Electric Distributors that are not regulated by the state,” and applies that conclusion to Old Dominion Electric Cooperative: because ODEC “is not subject to rate regulation by state commissions,” ODEC “would qualify as an Electric Distributor that is not subject to regulation by the state and may elect to opt out of the RBP.” PJM Answer at 30–31.

28. NOVEC is a Virginia distribution cooperative whose rates the State Corporation Commission regulates. On PJM’s stated criterion, NOVEC is ineligible. Two Virginia cooperatives therefore reach opposite results under the same provision.

29. The distinction does not track the risk. The risk PJM identifies is that utility customers may absorb the cost of an overbuy if the forecast large load does not materialize. Whether that occurs depends on whether the cost can actually be reassigned to the large-load customer and whether the utility can carry the obligation in the meantime. State rate regulation does not answer either question. It identifies who reviews retail rates; it does not establish a mechanism for transferring a fifteen-year wholesale obligation to the customer that caused it.

30. NOVEC’s exposure to that risk is not reduced by the presence of state rate regulation. Its allocation of 1,302.6 RBP UCAP MW derives from a large-load forecast submitted for transmission planning purposes, and the filed Tariff provides no reconciliation of allocated megawatts against load actually realized. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 9(c), 20–22. The overbuy risk PJM describes therefore applies to NOVEC in the same form.

31. In my opinion, the economic rationale for the opt-out would be stronger if eligibility tracked the overbuy risk PJM identifies rather than state rate-regulatory status standing alone.

V. THE CONTROLLING MEGAWATT QUANTITY REMAINS UNDEFINED
32. PJM states in footnote 234 of its Answer that no collateral would be required for RBP Resources that are not in service for a Delivery Year and that no RBP Charges would be assessed for those resources. PJM Answer at 78 n.234. NOVEC’s answer appropriately asks that this interpretation be reflected in the Tariff.

33. This is not only a drafting issue. Proposed Tariff, Attachment DD, section 18.8(b)(i)(A) defines the quantity to which the Credit Rate applies by reference to “RBP UCAP MW committed”: commitments received through the RBP minus commitments rescinded under section 18.6(e). The provision does not state an in- service limitation.

34. That distinction matters because rescission does not occur immediately when a resource fails to enter service. As explained in my EKPC reply affidavit, section 18.6(e) permits a resource to remain committed for up to three Delivery Years before rescission, and PJM’s September 14 implementation example for a resource that does not come online continues to show committed MW even while Daily Owned MW is zero. EKPC Reply Aff. ¶¶ 37–43.

35. The filed provisions also use the Final RBP Target in determining individual LSE postings. Section 18.8(b)(i)(B)(1) scales reported RBP Obligation MW where the reported values do not sum to the fixed Final RBP Target. If the procurement clears short of the Final RBP Target, committed MW and the Final RBP Target are different quantities. My EKPC reply affidavit explains that PJM has identified both as governing in different statements. Id. ¶¶ 44–46.

36. At the filed Credit Rate, each 100 MW difference changes the aggregate collateral amount by $150 million. For NOVEC, whose illustrative allocation already produces a $1.954 billion requirement, the quantity definition is therefore economically material.

37. In my opinion, an LSE should be able to calculate its requirement directly from the Tariff. If PJM intends collateral to follow only in-service MW, that should be stated expressly. The Tariff should also establish the relationship among committed MW, in-service MW, RBP Obligation MW allocated to an LSE, and the Final RBP Target.

VI. PJM’S PEAK MARKET ACTIVITY CLARIFICATION DOES NOT RESOLVE THE
CREDIT QUESTION
38. My original NOVEC affidavit stated that once RBP Charges were billed, the same economic activity would also enter Peak Market Activity under Attachment Q, creating an overlap between the new RBP requirement and existing credit treatment. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 10(d), 25–29. PJM has now responded directly to that issue.

PDF page 8

39. PJM consents to deleting the proposed sentence stating that settlement of RBP positions “may be included, as appropriate, in Peak Market Activity,” because that sentence could create confusion that RBP Charges may be captured in Peak Market Activity. PJM Answer at 86 n.259. In light of that consent, I do not rest my opinion on the proposition that an invoiced RBP Charge would itself be collateralized again through Peak Market Activity. The remaining issue is PJM’s treatment of the forward RPM component, addressed below.

40. PJM’s new explanation raises a different issue. PJM states that the separate RBP requirement must secure the forward RPM component as well as the contract-for- differences component, because Peak Market Activity does not prospectively collateralize the RPM component over the fifteen-year commitment. PJM Answer at

87. 41. The relevant comparison is not PJM’s stated rationale but the ordinary treatment of the RPM component PJM invokes. Every LSE carries, as a matter of course, a forward RPM capacity obligation extending roughly three years. PJM applies its ordinary Attachment Q credit framework to that obligation; it does not require collateral equal to the full three-year forward RPM payment stream.

42. Consider an RBP commitment with three Delivery Years remaining at an RBP commitment price of $555/MW-day and an RPM clearing price of $554/MW-day. The forward RPM exposure then has the same horizon as an ordinary capacity obligation. Per MW, the contract-for-differences amount over those three years is $1,095, while the remaining gross payment stream is $607,725. Applied to NOVEC’s illustrative 1,302.6 MW allocation, the three-year contract-for-differences amount is approximately $1.43 million, while the total remaining payment stream is approximately $791.6 million.

43. PJM’s own roll-off illustration shows the collateral it would hold at that point. At three Delivery Years remaining, PJM’s schedule sets collateral equal to the total remaining payment obligation: $60,772,500 per 100 MW, or $607,725 per MW. September 14 Presentation at 69. Applied to NOVEC’s illustrative allocation, that is approximately $791.6 million.

44. At three years remaining, therefore, an ordinary LSE and an RBP buyer carry forward RPM obligations of the same horizon at essentially the same price. The ordinary obligation remains subject to PJM’s ordinary Attachment Q credit treatment. Under PJM’s illustration, the RBP obligation carries collateral equal to every remaining dollar of the payment stream. What distinguishes the two is a contract for differences of $1/MW-day.

45. In my opinion, the existence of an RBP commitment can justify considering risks not present in an ordinary capacity obligation. But at three years remaining the horizon is the same, and the RPM exposure is the same. The methodology should explain why the credit treatment differs at that point, and how the separate requirement converges with ordinary RPM credit treatment as the contract-for-differences

amount and remaining tenor decline. The answer should not depend solely on whether a MW carries the label “RBP.”

VII. PJM’S REDUCTION MECHANISM REMAINS A GROSS-PAYMENT ROLL-OFF,
NOT AN EXPOSURE METHODOLOGY
46. PJM states that the Credit Rate can decline when previously required collateral exceeds remaining expected payment exposure. PJM Answer at 87 & n.261. The September 14 Presentation then illustrates a 100 MW obligation at the $1.5 million rate.

47. As described in my EKPC reply affidavit, that illustration holds $150 million of collateral unchanged for the first eight Delivery Years. The first reduction occurs in the ninth Delivery Year, when the remaining gross payment obligation falls below the collateral held. Thereafter, the requirement follows the remaining gross payment stream. EKPC Reply Aff. ¶¶ 62–68.

48. That illustration provides useful information about how PJM presently expects the permissive reduction language to operate. The reduction is therefore driven by remaining gross payments, not by the RPM clearing price, the contract-for- differences amount, participant credit condition, or modeled loss on default.

49. For NOVEC, a discretionary reduction standard is particularly significant because the starting amount is approximately $1.954 billion and the carrying cost is itself financially material. NOVEC Coscia Aff. ¶¶ 63–66. A reduction that occurs only after remaining gross payments fall below posted collateral can leave the requirement unchanged for years despite changes in market prices, remaining tenor, or credit condition.

50. In my opinion, additional procedures should establish objective, tariff-based standards for recalculation and release. Those standards should state the frequency of review, the inputs used, the circumstances requiring a reduction, and the time within which excess collateral is returned.

VIII. A CREDIT DESIGN OF THE KIND NOVEC SEEKS WAS BEFORE PJM IN THE STAKEHOLDER PROCESS

51. NOVEC’s answer points to the voluntary, subscription-based framework that received sector-weighted supermajority support. On the credit question, that framework was specific.

52. At design component 22.0 of PJM’s package matrix, the package proposed by the Joint Electric Distribution Companies and the Data Center Coalition would have used a registry to identify, before the procurement, the LSEs required to hold collateral and the megawatts for each. It provided that “[i]f an LSE-Large Load cannot post

PDF page 10

collateral, the load is not procured for through the backstop,” and that buyer default exposure would be recognized early and funded continuously over time. Package Matrix, tab “3. Package Matrix,” design component 22.0.

53. Two features of that approach bear on the questions addressed above. The obligated entity and its megawatt quantity were identified before the procurement rather than allocated afterward. And an inability to post operated on the procurement rather than on other parties: the megawatts were not bought, and no unsecured obligation arose.

54. PJM states that the Board “adopted various concepts from the Coalition RBP Proposal,” identifying the “credit requirement for Load Serving Entities” among them. July 31 Filing at 22. The filed construct assigns the obligation to LSEs. It does not incorporate the registry, the identification of the obligated entity before the procurement, the requirement to post before the obligation attaches, or the continuous measurement of exposure as it emerges.

55. I express no view on whether the Commission should direct adoption of that framework. I note only that a credit design addressing the matters identified in this affidavit was before PJM and received stakeholder support.

IX. ADDITIONAL PROCEDURES SHOULD ADDRESS THE ENTIRE BUYER-SIDE
CREDIT METHODOLOGY
56. PJM has consented to Commission resolution of the buyer-side Credit Rate through additional procedures and has also stated that the initial rate could be keyed to the actual MW-weighted average of selected RBP Sell Offers rather than the maximum $555/MW-day. PJM Answer at 92–93.

57. Using the actual RBP procurement price would reduce the requirement if the procurement clears below $555/MW-day. It would not resolve the underlying methodology. If the same approximately fifty-percent-of-gross-contractual-value approach is applied to a lower price, the number changes but its relationship to residual credit exposure remains undefined.

58. In my opinion, the scope of additional procedures should therefore encompass the buyer-side credit framework, not merely the numerical Credit Rate. At a minimum, the procedures should determine the residual loss the requirement secures, the parties that bear it, and the order in which collateral, section 18.9(a) reallocation, and any Operating Agreement section 15.2.2 allocation apply. They should also determine the controlling MW quantity, the relationship between RPM payments and the contract-for-differences amount, the period of exposure, participant credit condition and permissible forms of support, and objective recalculation and release standards.

PDF page 11

59. The methodology should then produce the Credit Rate. The reverse approach — selecting a collateral amount first and then describing it as a fraction of gross contractual value — does not demonstrate that the amount is calibrated to the credit loss that can result from default.

60. PJM’s Answer and subsequent implementation materials provide explanations that were not available when NOVEC filed its Protest. Those explanations narrow certain issues, including the Peak Market Activity issue, but they leave unresolved the exposure, quantity, and release mechanics that determine the buyer-side credit requirement.

61. For NOVEC, those choices have immediate financial consequences because the filed methodology produces an initial requirement of approximately $1.954 billion. In my opinion, a requirement of that scale should rest on a defined relationship between the amount posted and the loss secured. The present record does not establish that relationship.
