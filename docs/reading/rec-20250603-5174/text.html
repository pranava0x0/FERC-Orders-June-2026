I. The Standard Under Federal Power Act Section 206 Has Not Been Met. 
The Federal Power Act (“FPA”), under section 205, requires “[a]ll rates and charges made, 
demanded, or received by any public utility for or in connection with the transmission or sale of 
electric energy subject to the jurisdiction of the Commission, and all rules and regulations affecting 
or pertaining to such rates or charges shall be just and reasonable.”6  FPA section 206, on the other

must be found to be unjust and unreasonable before alternatives may be considered is a necessary, 
real, and meaningful one.  Only after a successful showing that the existing rate is unjust and 
unreasonable, may the Commission order a replacement rate.8  And, “[t]he burden of proving an 
unjust or unreasonable rate rests with the party that initiated the proceeding—that is, the 
Commission or the third-party complainant.”9  Neither the Commission nor any of the parties

A. The Current PJM Tariff Is Just and Reasonable and Not Unduly
Discriminatory, and No One Has Demonstrated Otherwise.
Notwithstanding proposed alternative arrangements for co-located load, no party has 
shown that the PJM Tariff is unjust and unreasonable.  Several parties have offered proposals that 
they claim would aid the development of co-located loads.  But, the Indicated TOs have explained 
at length why these proposals would harm, rather than help, the ability of PJM to accommodate 
co-located interconnection arrangements, and the attached Declaration of Danielle S. Powers 
(“Powers Declaration”) elaborates on those arguments.  More importantly, however, the claim—
even if it were true, which it is not—that a different tariff might advantage certain load does not 
demonstrate that the existing tariff is unjust or unreasonable.   
Some parties try to turn this process on its head.  They claim that the PJM Tariff should be 
changed because the Indicated TOs have not made an adequate “showing that PJM’s Tariff is just 
and reasonable or not unduly discriminatory, or that changes are not needed to enable progress.”10  
As the Indicated TOs have also repeatedly stressed, that is not the law; indeed, it is 180 degrees 
off.  The burden is not on PJM or the transmission owners to show that the PJM Tariff is just and 
reasonable; the burden is on parties seeking a change in the rate to demonstrate the existing tariff 
is not just and reasonable.  The Commission may order a replacement rate under Section 206 only 
after a successful showing that the prevailing rate is unjust and unreasonable.11  No such showing 
has been made.

V.

A. Bonbright Rate Design Principles Do Not Support Price Signaling for Specific 
Commercial Arrangements. 
30. E3 contends that the proposed rate designs are consistent with the Bonbright 
principles and are therefore just and reasonable. However, the use of price 
signals—specifically, a discounted transmission rate—to facilitate commercial 
arrangements such as a co-located business model is not among Bonbright’s 
criteria for sound rate design. 
31. As explained previously, offering a discounted rate to co-located load while 
requiring other load customers to pay the full transmission rate contravenes 
the Bonbright principle of Fairness in Cost Allocation, which holds that costs 
should be allocated equitably and without undue discrimination among 
customer classes or individuals.  
32. E3’s proposal to send price signals to facilitate the co-located business model 
alone, while charging undiscounted rates to all other customer classes, is 
misaligned with sound rate design principles. Indeed, if some customers pay 
discounted rates, other customers will have to make up that slack (the amount 
not paid on account of the discounts) by paying a yet higher rate. The Indicated 
PJM Transmission Owners have previously documented, in this proceeding, 
the material existence of unduly discriminatory cost shifting that would attend 
to such proposals.9 It is not sound rate design to give one group of customers a 
discount and charge a disproportionately high rate to all other customers, even

B. Transmission Rates Differ Fundamentally from PJM’s LMPs. 
33. E3 attempts to justify its price signal rationale by drawing a parallel to PJM’s 
energy market.10 However, this comparison is flawed—PJM’s energy market is 
in a competitive framework, whereas transmission is part of a regulated utility 
framework. Applying market-based pricing logic to the pricing framework of a 
regulated transmission system misconstrues the role and purpose of transmission 
rates.  
34.  Among other things, the LMP energy market sends short-term price signals that 
allow market participants to rapidly respond to the changing need for energy. The 
transmission grid, however, is a fixed investment in both the short-term and 
intermediate-term. When one customer avoids the cost of the transmission grid, it 
falls to another to pay it.

Costs

locate with generation. This is incorrect: load entities seeking interconnection 
already face strong financial drivers to minimize overall costs, including those 
associated with transmission upgrades, transmission charges, and energy 
procurement. Co-location often requires fewer transmission upgrades and 
reduces energy costs due to lower congestion and losses.
