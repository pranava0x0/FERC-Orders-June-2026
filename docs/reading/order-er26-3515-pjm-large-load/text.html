2. We reject PJM’s remaining proposed Tariff revisions, as discussed below.

15. Moreover, because the elements of PJM’s proposal other than the Large Load definition and Large Load Registry represent an integrated package that must be viewed as a whole, we reject the remainder of PJM’s proposal.  PJM, while consenting to severance and modification of the proposal, acknowledges that the RAA and OATT revisions are “designed to work in lockstep and provide complementary pieces.”17  We find that the remaining elements of PJM’s proposal, such as the proposed rules for BYONC arrangements and the exclusion of Eligible New Large Loads from the VRR curve, are not simply complementary, but instead are inextricably linked.  For example, the new obligation for LSEs seeking to use NITS to serve New Large Load to bring new capacity to avoid curtailment under IRAS is foundational to both the IRAS and BYONC frameworks.  It also establishes the need for the rules for BYONC arrangements and facilitates excluding Eligible New Large Loads from the VRR curve.  As such, we conclude that, contrary to PJM’s statements in its transmittal, the only elements of its proposal that are severable are the proposed definition of Large Load and the proposed Large Load Registry, which can be implemented independent of the IRAS and BYONC frameworks.

ROSNER, Commissioner, concurring:

1. I agree with the decision to reject the bulk of PJM’s filing for the reasons articulated in today’s order.  I write separately to emphasize that, if PJM chooses to refile its proposal, it must justify its proposal to curtail IRAS load on a mandatory basis before load that has voluntarily enrolled in a demand response program.  I also urge PJM to carefully consider the consequences any refiled proposal might have on its capacity market.

2. Customers in a demand response program allow a utility to reduce their demand without resorting to the extreme measure of manually pulling breakers on the utility’s distribution network.  Demand response customers also receive payments in exchange for volunteering to reduce their usage when the grid is stressed.  This structure is designed to protect consumers and their equipment from the draconian measure of being involuntarily forced off the system, as well as to send efficient market signals based on actual system conditions.

3. PJM’s filing flips this paradigm, proposing to force IRAS load off the system in the earliest stages of emergency conditions,1 before PJM deploys demand response.2

4. Moreover, PJM’s proposal would force IRAS load off the system in lieu of sending capacity price signals that are needed to get new infrastructure built.  Because PJM would forcibly curtail IRAS load before other firm load, PJM excludes IRAS load from its capacity market demand curve.  The result could be a capacity market signaling that PJM has more than enough capacity while the region sheds thousands of megawatts of demand on an involuntary basis.  That’s not only economically inefficient, but also likely to prevent supply and demand from coming back into balance.
