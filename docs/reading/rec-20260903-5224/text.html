COMMENTS AND LIMITED PROTESTS OF OHIO ENERGY LEADERSHIP COUNCIL 
 
 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy 
Regulatory Commission (the “Commission”), Ohio Energy Leadership Council (“OELC”) 
respectfully submits these comments and limited protests in response to the Interim Resource 
Adequacy Service (“IRAS”) and Large Load Registry proposed in this proceeding by PJM 
Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”). 
I. 
SUMMARY OF COMMENTS AND LIMITED PROTESTS. 
OELC supports the objective of maintaining resource adequacy and recognizes that new 
large loads should bear responsibility for the incremental resource-adequacy needs their additions 
create. OELC also recognizes the value of an interim conditional service option that allows 
qualifying new large loads to connect subject to curtailment when system conditions require it. 
But the Commission should not allow PJM’s proposal to shift to existing customers the risks and 
costs associated with its proposal in this case. Accordingly, OELC respectfully requests that the 
Commission condition any acceptance of PJM’s IRAS proposal on the following: 
1. 
Establish a separate, protective threshold for non-data-center customers. A non-data-
center customer—including but not limited to traditional manufacturing, steelmaking, 
chemical, industrial, educational, institutional, and retail customers—should be treated as 
a Large Load only if its Peak Load Contribution (“PLC”) is at least 150 MW. The lower 
50 MW threshold should not apply to non-data-center customers. 
2. 
Preserve the intended sequential load-reduction order if PJM accepts New Large 
Load demand response as BYONC. PJM’s proposal may permit New Large Load to

reduce its IRAS responsibility by registering its own load as a new Demand Resource or 
as part of a new DER Capacity Aggregation Resource. OELC does not oppose this proposal 
provided that curtailment sequencing should be preserved such that such new demand 
resources offered by the New Large Load should be curtailed before PJM calls on existing 
Demand Resources and existing DER Capacity Aggregation Resources as part of Pre-
Emergency and Emergency Load Management Reduction Actions. 
3. 
Ensure IRAS compensation is reasonable and just while preserving cost recovery 
questions to the appropriate state commissions. While OELC does not oppose 
appropriate compensation for IRAS curtailments, the Commission should ensure the 
compensation regime is just and reasonable and that the framework leaves to the 
appropriate state commissions how such costs are recovered from the local utilities and 
their customers. 
These protections are necessary for the proposal to satisfy the Federal Power Act’s just-
and-reasonable standard. Section 205 requires that all Commission-jurisdictional rates, charges, 
practices, and classifications be just and reasonable. PJM bears the burden to demonstrate that each 
proposed IRAS condition, including its BYONC eligibility, load-reduction sequencing, and 
compensation provisions, satisfies that standard. 
II. 
OELC’S INTEREST AND EXPERIENCE. 
 
OELC is a not-for-profit trade association that represents its members’ interests in utility 
and ratemaking matters in Ohio. Managing the cost and availability of energy is a crucial 
component of success for many businesses, and OELC advocates for competitive, reasonable, and 
transparent laws, policies, and utility rates through effective lobbying and legal advocacy. OELC’s 
work is directed to ensuring Ohio remains an attractive place for businesses and enterprises to 
remain, grow, invest, and create jobs. 
For more than 30 years, OELC has helped its members understand and manage their 
electric and natural gas supply costs. OELC and its members have direct, long-standing experience 
with the practical effects of utility tariffs, rate design, energy legislation, and regulatory policy on 
Ohio businesses that must manage significant energy consumption and exposure to volatile energy 
costs. OELC’s members include energy-intensive manufacturing, industrial, educational,

A reliable and reasonably priced electric system is essential to OELC’s members. OELC 
therefore supports measures that maintain system reliability and allocate the responsibility for new 
load fairly. The Commission should ensure that the IRAS construct does not allow new load to 
obtain preferential treatment in emergency curtailment or to impose on existing customers the cost 
of compensating that new load for complying with the conditions of its earlier, conditional service. 
III. 
PJM SHOULD ADOPT A SEPARATE 150 MW THRESHOLD FOR NON-DATA-
CENTER CUSTOMERS BASED ON PLC VALUES. 
PJM proposes to define a Large Load as an end-use customer load with a cumulative peak 
load of 50 MW or greater at a single electrical site.1 OELC does not oppose a definition that 
identifies the extraordinary new load additions producing the resource-adequacy concerns 
underlying IRAS. But PJM should not apply a uniform 50 MW threshold that treats traditional,

energy-intensive, non-data-center customers as similarly situated to data centers or apply that 
threshold without considering the materially different characteristics of these customer classes. 
Large non-data center loads include manufacturers, steel mills, chemical facilities, 
universities, and retailers, among others. Traditional industrial facilities are foundational to 
domestic supply chains. They supply critical domestically produced materials and products used 
in infrastructure, transportation, construction, energy systems, agriculture, health care, and 
national-defense-related production. A durable national-security and national economic strategy 
require that these industries have access to reliable and reasonably priced power. Reliability and 
affordability are complementary objectives, not competing ones.  
The Commission need not choose between supporting data-center growth and supporting 
domestic industry. It can do both by applying a definition that targets the new load that creates the 
IRAS regime requirement while maintaining reliable service to the traditional industrial base. The 
Application itself describes specific characteristics of new data-center load that support 
differentiated treatment. PJM states that an “unprecedented surge in data center load” has arrived 
faster than new supply can come online; the speed of connecting these loads has created a 
“fundamental misalignment between demand and supply cycles,” resulting in significant resource-
adequacy stress.2 PJM likewise cites data centers’ incomparably large energy demands, rapid 
energization, and unique collective potential to affect grid reliability and raise rates for other 
customers.3 PJM’s Application also identifies data-center growth as the predominant source of the 
near-term load-growth problem. PJM forecasts 32 GW of peak-load growth between 2024 and 
2030, of which 30 GW is projected to come from data centers.4

In this context, penalizing existing manufacturers for energy intensity simply weakens the 
very industrial capacity on which the nation depends, while doing little to improve the signal sent 
to the new load that drives the need for additional capacity. Energy prices are already a decisive 
component of manufacturing competitiveness. Capacity charges and other demand-related costs 
are not absorbed in a vacuum. They increase the delivered energy cost of production, reduce 
operating margins, constrain capital spending, and affect decisions about production volumes, 
facility expansions, and employment. OELC’s members compete not only with other domestic 
facilities but with international producers that may operate under materially different energy, 
regulatory, labor, and capital-cost conditions.  
The Commission should not introduce a new cost burden on domestic manufacturing at 
this critical time in our nation’s economy. The likely effect of an overbroad 50 MW threshold is 
not confined to the affected bill. Energy-intensive manufacturers transmit higher production costs 
through supply chains, or they absorb them by reducing investment, output, or employment. 
Neither outcome advances reliability, affordability, or economic resilience. Moreover, traditional 
energy-intensive customers often operate equipment and facilities with which utilities are familiar, 
promoting more accurate load forecasting and system planning for traditional loads. In the current 
inflation-sensitive environment, the Commission should avoid a tariff design that may add 
avoidable cost pressure to domestically produced materials and the goods made from them.  
A tailored distinction between data center and non-data center load is neither unduly 
discriminatory nor preferential. That distinction has already been implemented in Ohio where the 
Public Utilities Commission of Ohio has found a reasonable basis for treating data centers 
differently where their unique, concentrated load characteristics and system impacts distinguish

6 
 
them from other large-load customers.5 The Federal Power Act does not require PJM to disregard 
material differences among load categories. Rather, a just-and-reasonable tariff should recognize 
the materially different characteristics of data centers and avoid placing traditional manufacturing 
and industrial customers in the IRAS category solely because they exceed an undifferentiated 50 
MW threshold.  
OELC therefore requests that the Commission direct PJM to establish a separate 150 MW 
threshold for non-data-center customers. A 50 MW threshold that indiscriminately includes every 
customer category is overinclusive. It risks imposing undue IRAS-related obligations on 
traditional industries based on electricity intensity alone, rather than on a demonstrated causal 
nexus between incremental demand and the specific reliability concern the IRAS proposal is 
designed to address. Additionally, the non-data center 150 MW threshold should be calculated 
based on the customer’s system-peak PLC, rather than a non-data center customer’s highest 
demand during any hour of the year. Capacity-related classifications should be based on capacity-
related data—demand when the PJM system is under peak conditions—not an isolated 
noncoincident operating maximum. A threshold must do more than identify customers that use a 
meaningful amount of electricity. It must identify customers whose incremental system-peak 
demand creates the need for the specific capacity procurement and associated charges.

That is because capacity procurement exists to ensure that sufficient resources are available 
when the PJM system faces its highest coincident peak demand. The relevant question, therefore, 
is not whether a particular customer ever reached a high demand level in an isolated hour. It is 
whether the customer materially contributes to the regional peak conditions that establish the 
system’s capacity need. A customer-specific annual maximum can occur in a season, hour, or 
operating condition that does not coincide with the PJM system peak. It may reflect a one-time 
production event, an outage recovery, a weather-related operational adjustment, commissioning 
activity, or another idiosyncratic event with no meaningful relationship to the capacity issues in 
PJM that its IRAS proposal attempts to address. Using that noncoincident maximum would 
overstate the customer’s capacity responsibility and produce classifications untethered from the 
need for incremental capacity. 
By contrast, system-peak PLC is a familiar and objective planning construct used by PJM 
for many years. It measures demand when capacity is most needed by the region. PJM itself relies 
on load adjustments and capacity-obligation concepts that are intended to assign changes in 
forecasted load to the zone or area where they occur. The Large Load definition should employ 
the same fundamental principle: identify load by its contribution to the relevant system condition, 
rather than by an isolated and potentially unrepresentative maximum peak demand. 
If a customer can be classified as a Large Load based on its highest demand at any time in 
a year, a single non-coincident operating hour could trigger long-lived tariff consequences. That 
approach would be unpredictable, hard to audit, and unfair to customers with variable 
manufacturing processes. It could also perversely encourage customers to alter operations during 
individual high-demand hours without reducing their contribution to system peaks, thereby 
producing no genuine reliability benefit.

8 
 
The Commission should thus reject a methodology that conflates a facility’s maximum 
operating demand with its system-relevant contribution to capacity requirements. A capacity-
related classification must be based on capacity-related data. Accordingly, the Commission should 
direct PJM to revise its proposed tariff language to resolve at a minimum the following issues: 
1. 
Establish a separate, protective 150 MW threshold for non-data-center customers. A 
non-data-center customer—including but not limited to traditional manufacturing, 
steelmaking, chemical, industrial, educational, institutional, and retail customers—should 
be treated as a Large Load only if its PLC is at least 150 MW. The lower 50 MW threshold 
should not apply to non-data-center customers. 
2. 
Use of PLC Values. Identify the precise PJM peak-hour set and the period used to calculate 
the customer’s peak load for purposes of defining a “Large Load Customer,” including the 
process for publishing those hours and correcting data errors. OELC recommends that PJM 
be directed to use the existing PLC methodology; an annual maximum, an unannounced 
ad hoc interval, or a noncoincident peak should not be used. 
3. 
Net-load treatment. Specify the treatment of behind-the-meter generation, demand 
response, energy storage, and other load-management arrangements, including the data 
necessary to verify net system contribution. 
4. 
Customer notice and verification. Provide customers with advance notice of proposed 
classification, their underlying interval data and calculated PLC, a reasonable time to 
correct errors, and a transparent mechanism for challenging a classification before charges 
take effect. 
5. 
Temporal nexus to new load. For purposes of the IRAS, require PJM and the relevant 
electric distributor to demonstrate that the customer’s qualifying PLC reflects new or 
incremental load relative to the forecast or baseline used to establish the relevant target. 
Existing load should not be reclassified solely because it is large. 
These elements are indispensable. Without them, manufacturing customers cannot forecast 
their exposure, evaluate the accuracy of their bills, or meaningfully participate in any state retail-
cost-allocation proceeding. Ambiguity in the definition and measurement of load invites 
inconsistent results, disputes, and cost shifting—the opposite of the transparent and reasonable 
tariff administration OELC seeks.

IV. 
THE SEQUENTIAL LOAD-REDUCTION STACK PROPOSED BY PJM SHOULD 
BE PRESERVED EVEN IF NEW LARGE LOAD USES DEMAND RESPONSE AS 
BYONC. 
PJM’s application explains that its emergency procedures use sequential load reduction. 
Sites registered in the PJM demand-response program as Demand Resources may be called to 
reduce load in Pre-Emergency and Emergency Load Management Reduction Actions before firm 
exports are reduced in a Maximum Generation Emergency. PJM proposes to add IRAS as a 
category of load reduction that precedes Demand Resources and DER Capacity Aggregation 
Resources in that sequence.6 That sequencing is integral to the proposed incentive structure. PJM 
explains that placing IRAS after Pre-Emergency Load Management would reduce the incentive 
for New Large Loads to bring new capacity because the new loads would instead be insulated by 
Demand Resource capacity products.7  
OELC recommends that the Commission approve the curtailment sequencing proposed by 
PJM in this case. This is particularly important because PJM’s proposal may permit New Large 
Load to rely on demand response resources to meet BYONC requirements. A New Large Load 
that has not brought new generation could, in theory, register as a Demand Resource, avoid the 
earlier IRAS tranche, and move into the later Demand Resource tranche. It could improve its 
position in the sequential load-dump stack through a contractual arrangement rather than through 
the procurement of distinct incremental supply. The question is not whether Demand Resources 
provide reliability value. Properly structured and verifiable demand response can provide 
substantial benefits to the system, including demand resources offered by New Large Load. Rather, 
the question is whether a New Large Load’s participation in Demand Response should change the 
curtailment priority proposed by PJM under IRAS.

This tension can be resolved by creating a new category of Demand Resources consisting 
of New Large Load that elects to utilize its own curtailment capability as BYONC. The 
Commission should, therefore, direct PJM to revise its proposed tariff to provide that, to the extent 
New Large Load utilizes new Demand Resources or new DER Capacity Aggregation Resources 
to satisfy the BYONC requirements, for purposes of load-curtailment sequencing those resources 
should be curtailed before other PJM demand-response resources. This conditional acceptance of 
PJM’s proposal would help maintain power availability for traditional manufacturers and industrial 
customers as grid conditions become constrained by the increasing load brought about by 
increasing data center demand in PJM. 
V. 
THE COMMISSION SHOULD ENSURE ANY IRAS COMPENSATION IS JUST 
AND REASONABLE AND RESERVE RETAIL COST RECOVERY QUESTIONS 
FOR THE STATE COMMISSIONS. 
Finally, the Commission should fully evaluate whether PJM’s proposal to offer 
compensation at fifty (50) percent of the Non-Performance Charge Rate is just and reasonable.8 
Under PJM’s IRAS proposal, a New Large Load’s obligation to curtail when required to protect 
reliability is a condition of receiving earlier service. PJM’s own framework is premised on 
allowing qualifying large loads to connect subject to operational controls, including curtailment 
during specified system conditions, in order to protect customers from potential outages.9 The 
Commission should carefully evaluate the appropriate compensation scheme for New Large Loads 
that are complying with an IRAS curtailment condition that permits such New Large Loads to 
connect and energize earlier than the grid may otherwise permit.  
Further, OELC respectfully submits that the Commission should avoid approving a 
structure whereby existing industrial and manufacturing customers will subsidize any such

VI. 
CONCLUSION AND REQUEST FOR RELIEF. 
OELC does not oppose new load connecting to the PJM system or the development of 
mechanisms that protect reliability while such load is integrated. To the contrary, reliable service 
is essential to OELC’s members and to Ohio’s manufacturing and industrial economy. OELC’s 
concern is that the IRAS framework must not threaten domestic manufacturing and should 
preserve an appropriate allocation of responsibility. Accordingly, OELC respectfully requests that 
the Commission condition its approval of PJM’s tariff on the adoption of the recommendations set 
forth in these comments and limited protests.
