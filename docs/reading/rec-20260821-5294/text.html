MOTION TO INTERVENE AND COMMENTS OF 
TALEN ENERGY CORPORATION 
The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or “Commission”) established 
independent system operators (“ISOs”) based on a set of principles, including that the 
“management and administration of the ISO should be carried out in an efficient manner.”1  PJM 
Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) current stakeholder process falls short of this principle.  
Chairman Laura Swett summarized the PJM stakeholder process recently as “slow where it must 
be fast, opaque where it must be transparent, and vulnerable to vetoes and agenda control exactly 
when the region needs immediate action.”2  Chairman Swett’s observation aptly sets forth three 
priorities the Commission should seek to bolster through PJM governance reforms: efficiency, 
transparency, and independence.

Talen Energy Corporation (“Talen”) welcomes the opportunity presented by the

I. 
MOTION TO INTERVENE 
Pursuant to Rule 214 of the Commission’s Rules of Practice and Procedure,3 Talen 
respectfully submits this motion to intervene in the above-captioned proceeding.  The formal legal 
name of Talen is Talen Energy Corporation.  Talen is a corporation organized and existing under 
the laws of Delaware.  Talen maintains its principal place of business at 2929 Allen Parkway, 22nd 
Floor, Houston, Texas 77019. Talen is a leading independent power producer and energy 
infrastructure company which owns and operates approximately 15.7 gigawatts of power 
infrastructure in the United States.  Talen produces and sells electricity, capacity, and ancillary 
services into wholesale U.S. power markets, including and primarily in PJM.  Accordingly, the 
actions taken by the Commission in this matter will directly affect the interests of Talen.  Talen 
has a direct and material interest in the instant proceeding that no other party can adequately 
represent.  Talen therefore requests that the Commission grant this motion to intervene and permit 
Talen to participate in this proceeding with full rights as a party thereto.

inadequate in this regard.  For example, recent and significant Critical Issue Fast Path (“CIFP”)

PJM States should also have a role in the stakeholder process. Talen generally supports 
State involvement because States have a unique perspective to be able to opine on local issues and 
priorities.  For this reason, Talen is supportive of providing a second state representative group 
consisting of governors’ representatives and/or state legislators’ representatives, in addition to the 
Organization of PJM States Inc. (“OPSI”). 
Talen also supports OPSI consultation early in the stakeholder process.  It would arguably 
be too late and inefficient to consult with OPSI “prior to making a section 205 filing” because any 
constructive feedback from OPSI would need to be circulated through the stakeholders again.   
Talen further supports allowing states to vote for informational purposes on motions before 
the Members Committee.  However, providing formal voting rights to States puts PJM on the 
precipice of a slippery slope.  The Federal Power Act provides the Commission with exclusive 
jurisdiction over wholesale sales of electricity in the interstate market and “leaves to the States 
alone, the regulation of [retail electricity sales.]14  States cannot set an interstate wholesale rate 
because it would “contraven[e] the FPA’s division of authority between state and federal 
regulators.”15  Accordingly, providing States formal voting rights—or providing OPSI “jump ball” 
filing rights—is ill-conceived and arguably inconsistent with the FPA.  States and state 
commissions may continue to initiate their own proceedings with FERC should they desire to do 
so.16  Subject to the limitations and context above, Talen votes “yes” on Nos. 9, 18, and 20 
and votes “no” on Nos. 14 and 19.

B. 
Talen supports reforms that would result in increased transparency in the 
PJM stakeholder and decision-making process. 
Reasoned decision-making ensures that there is a logical corollary between the facts 
presented and the decision made.  Further, reasoned decision-making protects the legitimacy of 
the decision-maker.17  The PJM Board should provide the vote count and a reasoned explanation 
(similar to Decisional Letters) based on the facts and issues considered that resulted in the decision 
rendered.  For this reason, Talen votes “yes” on No. 6. 
Legitimacy is also fostered when voting rules bar the furtherance of affiliate interests.  As 
such, Talen also supports applying voting rules barring affiliate votes to lower-level committees.  
For this reason, Talen votes “yes” on No. 17. 
Notwithstanding the need for increased transparency, some existing and proposed 
measures may be counterproductive towards the goal of increased efficiency.  For example, 
restrictions on Board and stakeholder communications may undermine efforts to coalesce around 
best paths forward for imminent or priority issues.  More frequent and recurring meetings between 
the Board and stakeholders would be beneficial for all parties. The Board should also be permitted 
to entertain individual meeting requests for imminent or “priority” issues.  Subject to this 
limitation and context, Talen votes “yes” on No. 5. 
In addition, opening Board meetings to the public may unnecessarily politicize the Board’s 
decisions and delay progress.  PJM would not be alone in holding closed Board meetings.18  For

C. 
Talen supports reforms that would protect the independence of the PJM 
Board. 
The Commission explained in Order No. 2000, that RTO independence is based on three 
main criteria: (1) RTO employes and directors have no financial stake in any market participant; 
(2) the RTO must have a decision-making process that is independent of control by any market 
participant or class of participants; and (3) the RTO must have exclusive and independent authority 
to file changes to its transmission tariff.19  The Commission should ensure PJM’s compliance with 
this criteria, but additional restrictions that becomes superfluous or restrict PJM’s ability to attract 
the best talent to serve on its Board are unnecessary.  For example, Board member qualification 
rules should not include a preference for PJM residents.  Prior experience with and an aptitude for 
PJM issues is more important than PJM residence.  In addition, “cooling off” periods with respect 
to conflict-of-interest rules are unnecessary, provided that all financial conflict of interest rules 
remain intact and consistent with Order No. 2000. This approach would be consistent with how 
other ISO/RTOs, such as CAISO,20 the Electric Reliability Council of Texas,21 the Independent 
System Operator of New England,22 the New York Independent System Operator (“NYISO”)23

It is also not advisable to revise PJM’s governing documents in a manner that could subvert

reason, Talen votes “no” on No. 10.

documents.  Section 7.7(i) of PJM’s Operating Agreement already provides that the Board’s
