Re:  
Docket Nos. EL25-49, EL25-20 and AD24-11 
Dear Chairman Christie and Commissioners Chang, Phillips, Rosner and See: 
We are a group of retired military officers and national security experts who share a deep 
commitment to ensuring that the United States has the artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities vital to 
defend the nation.  The data centers that provide these capabilities to the Department of Defense (DOD) 
depend on resilient electric power to function.  We urge that the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission 
act to ensure that data centers can meet their growing power requirements as quickly as possible. 
Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth emphasizes that AI capabilities are vital to DOD’s success in 
the future.  Secretary Hegseth has pledged to prioritize the use of AI “from the boardroom to the 
battlefield” across a broad range of missions, including monitoring cyberspace, countering transnational 
criminal organizations, testing and evaluating weapons, and other critical operations and support 
functions.1  Many of these AI activities occur every day and around the clock.  However, if a conflict occurs 
with the People’s Republic of China (PRC) or other adversaries, AI is becoming crucial to support the 
command and control of US forces and decisionmaking by military commanders.  
Chinese leaders are well aware of the decisive military advantages that AI can offer.  DOD warns 
that “China is a global leader in AI technology and aims to overtake the West in AI research and 
development by 2025 to become the world leader in AI by 2030.”2  China’s DeepSeek AI reflects that drive 
for supremacy.  As President Donald Trump has stated, “the release of DeepSeek AI from a Chinese 
company should be a wake-up call” for US industry, and that “we need to be laser focused on competing.”3 
The US will lose that competition unless AI data centers have the power they need.  Speed is of 
the essence in providing that power.  In some states, including those in the Eastern US served by PJM 
Interconnection, L.L.C., data centers seeking to connect and take service from the grid may face delays of 
seven to ten years before they can receive power.  Those delays are unconscionable, given the DOD’s 
rapidly growing requirements for AI services and the imperative to counter China’s drive for AI primacy.  
FERC’s Order Instituting Proceeding Under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act and Consolidating 
with Other Proceedings in Docket No. EL25-49-000 (February 20, 2025), focuses on another option to serve 
data centers: co-locating data centers with current nuclear power plants or other generation resources

that can be very rapidly deployed (including gas-fired generators or renewable resources combined with 
energy storage systems). President Trump has noted that such co-located generation projects can go 
forward on a “very rapid basis” compared to other options.4  Moreover, nuclear plants serving co-located 
data centers provide a reliable source of power and are uniquely hardened against cyber and physical 
attacks.        
FERC’s Order calls for assessments of “any national security implications associated with co-
location arrangements.”5 Co-location arrangements provide crucial advantages in terms of the speed with 
which they can meet defense-critical data center needs, and the resilience of the power that nuclear 
plants provide.  We respectfully request that you take these benefits into account as FERC’s proceedings 
go forward and ensure that all options to quickly serve data centers are made available.
