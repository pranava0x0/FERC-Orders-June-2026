COMMENTS OF GOOGLE LLC

Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission”),1 Google LLC (“Google”) submits these comments on the Midcontinent Independent System Operator Inc.’s (“MISO”) Zero Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement (“ZGIA”) proposal.  The ZGIA proposal is a step forward.  Google applauds MISO for working constructively with stakeholders to develop the proposal, which will help facilitate the timely and efficient interconnection of badly needed new generation capacity. Google has consistently supported efforts to accelerate the pace at which new generation facilities are interconnected to the transmission system.  Those efforts are particularly important now given the rapid load growth experienced throughout much of the country, including in MISO. The best way to ensure the reliability and affordability of our electricity system under those conditions is to enable the development of more generation capacity.

That goal—the development of new capacity—is as much an infrastructure problem as it is anything else.2  Aging, over-burdened transmission facilities are the primary barrier to supply to keeping pace with demand.   Accordingly, building out the nation’s power grid with new

For that reason, Google has also strongly supported “reforms that will expedite the interconnection of facilities that maximize the use of existing infrastructure and minimize strain on the grid.”3  Locating electric generating facilities close to the loads they will serve is one of the most efficient means of doing so.   As a matter of engineering, economics, and basic physics, generation sited near the load it serves puts less strain on the grid and requires less investment in new or upgraded transmission infrastructure. Solutions that capitalize on that fact by facilitating efforts to site generation near paired load therefore minimize the network upgrades needed to interconnect new resources and, in turn, accelerate the pace at which new generation comes online.

The ZGIA proposal is a step in that direction.  It recognizes the importance of accounting for how a generator will operate in practice when studying generator interconnection requests, including the reality that generators serving electrically proximate loads can “minimize the impact on the transmission system.”4  Specifically, the ZGIA would create a “study process tailored to the operational characteristics of generation serving co-located load, including a study process that

On the latter point, MISO suggests “the ZGIA process can take place quickly, with the ZG Impact Assessment completed within 90 Calendar Days,”6 much faster than the multi-year timelines that can apply to generator interconnection applications.

The ZGIA is, however, only a step in the right direction.  That is because the ZG Interconnection Service can be used only to serve designated load that is either at the same Point of Interconnection as the paired generator or has a Point of Withdrawal on the same voltage side of the same substation as the paired generator.7  That limitation is considerably more restrictive in practice than the reforms that the Commission proposed to require MISO to adopt in the Large Load Show Cause Order.8  In that proceeding, the Commission described electrically proximate load and generation as those that are no more than two substations apart.9  Similarly, SPP’s High Impact Large Load Generation Assessment (“HILLGA”) process, which offers an expedited 90- day interconnection study and a limited, provisional form of transmission service, is also available to generators that are located within two substations of the large load they will serve.10

As MISO develops its response to the directives in the Large Load Show Cause Order, Google urges it to expand the principles established in the ZGIA to apply to circumstances at least as broad as the within-two-substations configuration contemplated in the Large Load Show Cause Order and permitted under HILLGA.  By including more potential load and generation configurations, MISO can unlock more new generation on an expedited basis.  That is particularly so because the distinction between being at the same point of interconnection and within two substations is a meaningful difference in practice.  For example, a major energy park that includes significant networked load and generation is exactly the type of facility for which a ZGIA framework would make sense.  But if, as is common in these configurations, that energy park has multiple substations with the generation and associated load interconnecting through different substations, the ZGIA avenue may be unavailable, leading to costly delays and unnecessary expense in bringing the new generation online.

At the same time, although Google urges MISO to make the most out of approaches like ZGIA, MISO cannot lose sight of the importance of accelerating its principal interconnection process:  the Definitive Planning Process (“DPP”).  As noted, it can take several years to move an interconnection request through the DPP process.11  That timeline creates an understandable urge to pursue short-term solutions to accelerate the interconnection of a limited subset of generator— most notably, the Expedited Resource Addition Study (“ERAS”).  Nevertheless, Google believes that fixing the sluggish timelines in the DPP is a more durable and lasting solution to MISO interconnection challenges than an endless series of shortcuts, even if some of those shortcuts are merited for the foreseeable future.

MISO appears to agree.  In its ERAS filing, MISO stated that it “remains committed to reducing its general interconnection review period to one year,” with a goal of doing so by 2028.12 This proceeding provides one window into how it might achieve that goal.  Drawing on the idea underpinning the ZGIA, MISO should amend the DPP study process to ensure that it is studying generators as they actually intend to operate and based on the effects they can realistically be expected to have on the system, which will avoid saddling generators with unnecessary network upgrades that are required only to manage extreme circumstances.  At the end of the day, MISO needs a single, well-functioning generator interconnection process that can facilitate the timely entry of new resources onto the system.  Otherwise, the region’s supply of generation will perpetually lag behind future increases in demand.

In addition, Google strongly supports continued efforts to integrate transmission planning and generator interconnection.   MISO’s MTEP has been one of the country’s leading examples of forward-looking transmission planning, particularly its Long-Range Transmission Plan.  Holistic transmission planning, that considers generator interconnection needs, among other drivers, helps avoid the piece-meal, inefficient development of the transmission system while simultaneously reducing the risk that interconnection customers will be forced to shoulder enormous network upgrade costs that can stymie the development of new generation facilities and create considerable uncertainty for other generators through restudies.  “No power plant can obtain financing when the transmission costs are either unknown or subject to significant revision based on ‘that next

This type of proactive transmission planning is also important for policy priorities like the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, to which Google is a signatory,14 as well as broader efforts to incentivize customers to bring their own new capacity.  Those policies and efforts, which are consistent with Google’s longstanding energy procurement philosophy and essential to ensuring reliable service and affordable rates for residential customers and other existing commercial customers, simply will not succeed if new generation cannot get through the interconnection queue.
