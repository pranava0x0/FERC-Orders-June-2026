I. 
Summary of Answer to the ICC’s Request 
For context, in Pennsylvania, certain existing industrial customers have invested 
significantly in combined heat and power (CHP) cogeneration facilities that support their 
manufacturing processes and offset their electricity consumption. The cogeneration facilities 
generate combined electrical and thermal energy. Thermal energy, or heat, is used in the 
manufacturing process and the electricity byproduct is used to power their business. These CHP 
cogeneration facilities are BTMG, are not used in PJM’s energy or capacity markets, and do not 
use the PJM transmission system because when the generation trips offline, the load connected to 
the BTMG (or almost all of the load) automatically trips offline. Such a configuration ensures that 
the load with BTMG does not materially “lean” on PJM.5 
In addressing PJM’s BTMG rule, FERC should adhere to the jurisdictional limits expressed 
in the Order to preserve exclusive state retail authority and protect consumers from cost shifts, 
because doing so will improve the Order’s legal durability and effectuate its purpose. Furthermore, 
FERC should evaluate whether and how to set an appropriate threshold(s) with continued attention 
to the distinction between unique new large loads—primarily data center large loads—and existing

II. 
Background on the Order’s BTMG Provisions 
In the Order, the Commission found PJM’s existing BTMG rules to be no longer just and 
reasonable because “loads with BTMG are not fully accounted for in resource adequacy planning,” 
which creates a “potential transmission and resource adequacy planning problem” and “raise[s] 
cost shifting concerns.”6 FERC reasoned that the existing rules may present “both reliability and 
resource adequacy risks because PJM, per its Tariff, is obligated to serve transmission customers 
using BTMG but does not consider such customers in transmission and resource adequacy 
planning.”7 On that basis, FERC directed “PJM to revise its BTMG rules.”8  
In recognition of “concerns raised in the record about the potential impacts on existing 
BTMG customers of the Commission changing PJM’s BTMG rules, however, [the Commission] 
direct[ed] PJM to maintain the current BTMG rules for customers that fall below a new [megawatt] 
[(MW)] materiality threshold for the amount of load at a particular electrical location that Network

III. 
FERC Should Adhere to the Jurisdictional Limits Expressed in the Order to Preserve 
Exclusive State Retail Authority and Protect Consumers from Cost Shifts, Because Doing So 
Will Improve the Order’s Legal Durability and Effectuate Its Purpose. 
 
The ICC’s Request correctly notes that “the Order does not sufficiently explain the legal 
basis for asserting jurisdiction over retail customers with [BTMG] that is not otherwise used in 
PJM’s energy or capacity markets and does not use the PJM transmission system.”24 In accordance 
with FERC’s jurisdictional analysis in the Order, the ICC identifies that “any regulations the 
Commission fashions must be based on its statutory authority over interstate transmission and 
wholesale sales.”25 
In evaluating PJM’s tariff revisions, FERC should ensure that PJM has clarified exactly 
how it interprets the definitions of BTMG and Co-Located Load, especially in relation to 
jurisdiction. The Order “clarif[ied] that, consistent with the definition in 159, provided above, 
supra P 164, BTMG is not Co-Located Load.”26 But the exact relationship between them remains 
unclear. 
FERC should ensure that PJM’s Tariff reaffirms and clearly demarcates distinctions 
between (1) retail BTMG, (2) retail BTMG that directly affects interstate transmission or interstate 
wholesale sales but retains an exclusively state-jurisdictional retail component, and (3) the FERC-
jurisdictional aspects of Co-Located Load configurations—which FERC carefully explained are

IV. 
FERC Should Evaluate Whether and How to Set a MW Threshold(s) with Continued 
Attention to the Distinction Between New Large Loads—Primarily data center large loads—
and Existing BTMG Facilities. 
 
In determining the threshold for load being served by BTMG, FERC should keep in mind 
its choice to highlight PJM’s comments expressing concern specifically about “the scale of large 
data centers.”34 The Commission remarked that “PJM has persuasively explained the problems that 
could occur if Network Customers with large loads were allowed to use the existing BTMG rules 
in the Tariff. For example, PJM explains that ‘[a]llowing very large loads like data centers to net 
is problematic because loads in this configuration do not carry reserves.’”35 
PJM did not support “PJM option 3,” a configuration in which Network Load may Co-
Locate and use BTMG to engage in netting, because “historically, BTMG has supported relatively 
small loads, not on the scale of large data centers.”36  Furthermore, PJM commented that “charging 
load for its net use of the grid under the BTMG rules may not capture the costs caused by co-

The threshold’s primary purpose is to protect “existing BTMG customers.”49 
FERC should continue to effectuate this intent by ensuring that new rules for BTMG due
