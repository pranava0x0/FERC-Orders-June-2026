I. Summary of Answer to the ICC’s Request
For context, in Pennsylvania, certain existing industrial customers have invested significantly in combined heat and power (CHP) cogeneration facilities that support their manufacturing processes and offset their electricity consumption. The cogeneration facilities generate combined electrical and thermal energy. Thermal energy, or heat, is used in the manufacturing process and the electricity byproduct is used to power their business. These CHP cogeneration facilities are BTMG, are not used in PJM’s energy or capacity markets, and do not use the PJM transmission system because when the generation trips offline, the load connected to the BTMG (or almost all of the load) automatically trips offline. Such a configuration ensures that the load with BTMG does not materially “lean” on PJM.5

In addressing PJM’s BTMG rule, FERC should adhere to the jurisdictional limits expressed in the Order to preserve exclusive state retail authority and protect consumers from cost shifts, because doing so will improve the Order’s legal durability and effectuate its purpose. Furthermore, FERC should evaluate whether and how to set an appropriate threshold(s) with continued attention to the distinction between unique new large loads—primarily data center large loads—and existing

II. Background on the Order’s BTMG Provisions
In the Order, the Commission found PJM’s existing BTMG rules to be no longer just and reasonable because “loads with BTMG are not fully accounted for in resource adequacy planning,” which creates a “potential transmission and resource adequacy planning problem” and “raise[s] cost shifting concerns.”6 FERC reasoned that the existing rules may present “both reliability and resource adequacy risks because PJM, per its Tariff, is obligated to serve transmission customers using BTMG but does not consider such customers in transmission and resource adequacy planning.”7 On that basis, FERC directed “PJM to revise its BTMG rules.”8

III. FERC Should Adhere to the Jurisdictional Limits Expressed in the Order to Preserve Exclusive State Retail Authority and Protect Consumers from Cost Shifts, Because Doing So Will Improve the Order’s Legal Durability and Effectuate Its Purpose.

The ICC’s Request correctly notes that “the Order does not sufficiently explain the legal basis for asserting jurisdiction over retail customers with [BTMG] that is not otherwise used in PJM’s energy or capacity markets and does not use the PJM transmission system.”24 In accordance with FERC’s jurisdictional analysis in the Order, the ICC identifies that “any regulations the Commission fashions must be based on its statutory authority over interstate transmission and wholesale sales.”25

In evaluating PJM’s tariff revisions, FERC should ensure that PJM has clarified exactly how it interprets the definitions of BTMG and Co-Located Load, especially in relation to jurisdiction. The Order “clarif[ied] that, consistent with the definition in 159, provided above, supra P 164, BTMG is not Co-Located Load.”26 But the exact relationship between them remains unclear.

FERC should ensure that PJM’s Tariff reaffirms and clearly demarcates distinctions between (1) retail BTMG, (2) retail BTMG that directly affects interstate transmission or interstate wholesale sales but retains an exclusively state-jurisdictional retail component, and (3) the FERC- jurisdictional aspects of Co-Located Load configurations—which FERC carefully explained are
