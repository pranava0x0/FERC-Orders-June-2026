POST-CONFERENCE COMMENTS OF ADVANCED ENERGY UNITED 
 
Advanced Energy United (“United”)1 appreciates the opportunity to provide post-
conference comments following the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or 
“Commission”) technical discussion of Governance and Stakeholder Reforms in the PJM 
Interconnection (“PJM”) region on July 23, 2026. The Commission should be commended for 
facilitating a constructive and candid exchange of ideas and for setting a clear plan for the region 
to take swift and decisive action. 
The comments below expand upon pre-conference comments submitted by United in this 
docket, which outlined our perspective on the root causes of the challenges PJM faces and 
offered recommendations with respect to the role of states, the PJM Board, and PJM 
stakeholders.2 United’s primary objective in this proceeding is to ensure that PJM retains its role

I. 
COMMENTS 
The Commission’s Notice requests that respondents “indicate the PJM member sector to 
which your company or organization belongs.”4 As stated in pre-conference comments, 
Advanced Energy United is a national trade association that represents a wide range of market 
participants in the PJM region and beyond. United itself is an “Associate” member of PJM, 
which means we pay a nominal annual fee and participate in PJM stakeholder meetings, but do 
not cast votes or belong to a sector. Our member companies span multiple sectors of PJM 
membership, primarily the Generation Owner and Other Supplier sectors. United’s membership 
also includes companies that are not yet PJM market participants, but that aspire to be in the 
future, subject to either expansion of their business into the region or implementation of market 
reforms that remove barriers to participation.

2) 
Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may 
serve (e.g., 10 years)? 
X 
 
Comments: 
Term limits can be a useful tool for ensuring turnover, guarding against directors becoming too 
closely aligned with incumbent management or entrenched market participants, and creating 
opportunities to refresh the Board’s mix of skills and perspectives over time. Extending terms 
to five years and setting a two-term limit (i.e., 10 years total) would be a reasonable approach. 
At the same time, a rigid cap could force out a Board member who has become particularly 
effective. If a term limit is imposed, PJM should consider a mechanism to override the limit to 
extend the term of a particularly effective Board member where there is a demonstrated case 
that continuity of that member's service is in the best interest of PJM and its stakeholders. 
3) 
Should the Board member qualification rules be revised to provide a 
preference for some Board members that reside in the PJM region? 
 
X 
Comments: 
While there is valid underlying logic to the idea of a residency preference, the priority in 
identifying Board candidates should be to find individuals with the right mix of qualifications, 
expertise, and independence, and adding a residency preference risks undermining those goals. 
PJM’s existing conflict-of-interest and independence restrictions already narrow the eligible 
candidate pool, and adding a residency requirement risks narrowing it further. Furthermore, a 
residency requirement should not be necessary to ensure that Board members have the best 
outcomes for the PJM region as their top priority; Board members’ fiduciary and statutory 
obligations, not their address, should be the operative check on their decision-making. 
Outside perspective is a genuine asset to the Board, so if a residency preference is added, it 
should apply to some seats rather than the Board as a whole to avoid robbing the Board of 
expertise from other regions and even other countries with different market structures. We 
would have no concern with a residency preference used only as a tie-breaker among similarly 
qualified finalists.

4) 
Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off 
period for Board members from two years to one year? 
X 
 
Comments: 
Having Board members who understand the industry in sufficient depth to meaningfully 
challenge and engage with PJM leadership on substantive issues is critical to effective 
governance. The current two-year cooling-off period, while well-intentioned, meaningfully 
narrows the pool of candidates with recent, hands-on industry experience. Relaxing the 
restriction, even modestly, should help expand the pool of eligible, well-qualified candidates 
without unduly compromising Board independence. A one-year cooling-off period would be 
an appropriate middle ground to allow for eligible candidates that are not too closely aligned 
with a member perspective.  
5) 
Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable more 
communication between the Board and stakeholders? 
X 
 
Comments:  
It is preferable for Board members to have greater direct exposure to the commercial realities 
and practical implications of PJM’s rules, as well as visibility into new innovations, 
technologies, and ideas emerging among stakeholders. Expanded communication between the 
Board and stakeholders, with guardrails to preserve Board independence and avoid improper 
influence, would promote exactly this kind of exposure and should result in a better-informed, 
more engaged Board. United supports further discussion as to what guardrails must be put in 
place.

7) 
In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or 
available to the public? 
X 
 
Comments:   
Public Board meetings would be most beneficial if they are used as a forum between 
stakeholders and the Board, as they are in SPP, where Board meetings are used to review and 
vote on all revision requests. Those revision requests that are more controversial are generally 
allocated extra discussion time to allow stakeholders to share their arguments with the Board; 
before a final vote, Board members can choose to share how they will vote and explain their 
reasoning. Making some Board meetings open to the public is also a no regrets reform: it costs 
little, increases the visibility of the Board to stakeholders and the public, and is consistent with 
a broader push toward greater transparency. 
8) 
Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the 
responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest? 
 
X 
Comments:  
While PJM and its Board should act in accordance with the public interest, adding a public 
interest responsibility into PJM’s governing documents is not a necessary revision, as PJM 
already has a duty—which FERC has an independent obligation to enforce—to ensure that 
PJM’s rates are just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory, and that PJM's operations 
maintain reliability. Faithful execution of those duties is consistent with serving the public 
interest. A freestanding “public interest” mandate is susceptible to being interpreted or invoked 
in ways that go beyond PJM’s and the Board’s existing statutory obligations, potentially 
justifying actions or interventions that undermine market certainty and confidence and that are 
ultimately inconsistent with PJM’s role. If a public interest provision were to be adopted, its 
intent must be a clearly defined, and PJM should offer an explanation of why and how such a 
mandate is not covered under the existing obligations of PJM and FERC. 
9) 
Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the 
Nominating Committee? 
X 
 
Comments:  
Allowing states to participate and vote in the Nominating Committee would appropriately 
reflect the important role states have in ensuring PJM’s success, and the impact that PJM’s 
decisions have on outcomes at the state level. Giving states a seat on the Nominating 
Committee would also increase confidence in the integrity of the Board selection process by 
exposing states to this closed process. However, states should be granted one, or at maximum 
two, voting member(s) on the Nominating Committee. Adding more than two state votes to the 
eight seats on the Nominating Committee risks introducing a perception of undue political 
influence into the process. There is strong precedent for an RTO to include states in this way; 
ISO-NE and MISO both include states as voting members but limit the number of votes

10) 
Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management 
compensation to their achievement? 
 
 
Comments: 
This proposal could be beneficial or could have unintended consequences, depending heavily 
on which public performance metrics are adopted and how they are measured. For example, 
tying compensation to public performance metrics could inadvertently reinforce risk aversion, 
which is already generally well rewarded. Tying compensation to performance could also 
promote more short-term thinking among management, when many beneficial RTO initiatives 
require a long-term view that may not pay off within a single compensation cycle. Rather, 
compensation could be tied to accomplishment of annual goals detailed in the RTO’s strategic 
plan, like what is done in MISO.8 Should this reform be adopted, it will be important to ensure 
that any resulting metrics support a shift toward a culture that embraces innovation and new 
tools and technologies and promotes good outcomes in the long-term as well as the near-term.

11) 
Should the stakeholder process be advisory, i.e., stakeholder votes only 
inform the Board? 
 
X 
Comments:  
In United's experience, a purely advisory stakeholder process sidelines the legitimate concerns 
of market participants—ultimately resulting in a more contentious debate when a proposal 
reaches the Commission. Stakeholders bring valuable perspective on the commercial and 
practical realities of proposed changes that PJM management and the Board do not always 
have direct visibility into. Listening to these concerns supports an investable and reliable 
market; proposals that are not workable in practice tend to generate downstream problems 
regardless of how sound they look on paper. Additionally, policies with stakeholder support 
are more durable, and less prone to legal challenges or significant changes from either 
stakeholders or leadership. While stakeholder perspectives can still be shared in an advisory

That said, United is sympathetic to concerns that the incumbent-dominated stakeholder

stalling or blocking reforms that would enhance competition or facilitate new market entry, 
thereby undermining reliability and just and reasonable rates. Rather than preventing 
stakeholder logjams by making the process purely advisory, we would propose the following 
targeted solutions to ensure progress is made while preserving a substantive stakeholder role: 
• 
Change the voting process to reduce the ability of a small set of market participants to 
block beneficial proposals, and to prevent outright blocks of proposals at the lower 
committees before consideration by the full stakeholder body; 
• 
Re-examine and consider reforms of the sectors to ensure meaningful participation of non-
incumbent interests—as NEPOOL did through the creation of its Alternative Resources

Questions/Comments

Comments:   
Transmission planning is a core role of the RTO, and it is critical that the planning process be 
oriented toward outcomes that are efficient, cost-effective, competitive, and aligned with 
regional needs. Removing the input of the Members Committee and granting section 205 filing 
rights to PJM may appear consistent with those goals, but would instead grant outsize 
influence to transmission owners, who have a direct financial stake in the outcome of RTEP. 
By virtue of the threat to withdraw from PJM, TOs wield enormous influence over PJM’s 
decisions, independent of their voting share. Indeed, PJM’s own polling on proposed 
governance changes indicates that TOs are overwhelmingly supportive of transferring 205 
rights over RTEP to PJM, while the end use customer sector is nearly unanimously opposed. 
This breakdown indicates that TOs generally view this shift as favorable to their interests 
while consumers do not see it as a means to improve outcomes for the region.9 
PJM has also not identified a specific issue or reliability need that was stalled by the Members 
Committee in RTEP matters. In fact, PJM was the first region to submit Order No. 1920 
regional compliance. Removing section 205 filing rights from the Members Committee would 
do little to speed up the planning process, and would remove important stakeholder feedback 
that will otherwise be expressed at FERC. 
Regardless of who has 205 rights over RTEP, United agrees with the Independent Market 
Monitor’s (“IMM”) argument that TO influence must be kept in check and supports the 
IMM’s call for a clearly defined process and set of requirements governing transmission owner 
departure from PJM.10 Without a defined process for leaving PJM, a TO’s departure could 
create such upheaval that it could “bring an end to PJM as it currently exists.”11 This looming 
threat gives TOs an unparalleled influence over the decision making process, and places PJM 
in the delicate position of having to appease TOs while trying to maintain broad stakeholder 
support. A defined exit process would diminish this leverage, while also ensuring that PJM 
and its remaining members are not harmed in the event a transmission owner departs.

15) 
Should the Members Committee have “jump ball” filing rights over the 
portions of the tariff where PJM has 205 filing rights when a supermajority 
of stakeholders approves an alternative proposal? 
X 
 
Comments:  
As discussed above, stakeholders bring critical perspective on market rules and planning 
processes that should not be ignored. Granting the Members Committee “jump ball” rights is 
one way to ensure that stakeholder perspectives are not just acknowledged, but meaningfully 
taken into account. Stakeholder jump-ball rights in ISO-New England have been an effective 
mechanism to: (1) incentivize ISO-NE to work proactively with stakeholders to resolve their 
concerns with its proposals before filing, and (2) encourage stakeholders to work together to 
build consensus around solutions to concerns rather than simply opposing proposals. Without 
jump ball rights, the Commission is only presented one proposal that must meet the relatively 
low bar of “just and reasonable.”  Allowing more parties to file will give the Commission the 
ability to decide which proposal is more “just and reasonable.” Alternatively, the fact that 
stakeholders have jump ball rights does not necessarily result in a jump ball filing at FERC, 
but rather ISO-NE’s adoption of stakeholder-supported amendments. United believes a similar 
dynamic would benefit PJM's stakeholder process. 
A “jump ball” mechanism would be beneficial in any portion of the tariff where PJM has 205 
filing rights, but will be particularly important if stakeholder filing rights are reduced 
elsewhere in connection with other reforms.12 If PJM's own unilateral filing rights expand, 
jump ball rights for stakeholders should be correspondingly enhanced in their place, to 
preserve a meaningful stakeholder check on PJM’s proposals.

Comments:  
While the two-thirds threshold sets a high bar to reach consensus that may block some 
beneficial proposals, it also appropriately protects against advancement of reforms that raise 
legitimate stakeholder concerns. Prior to lowering the threshold, PJM should take all possible 
steps to address stakeholder gridlock through mechanisms that would create stronger pathways 
and incentives for stakeholder alignment, such as: 
• PJM taking greater control over the stakeholder timeline to force stakeholder 
alignment, e.g., by making clear that it will file under section 206 by a certain date if 
stakeholder agreement is not reached; 
• Introducing an annual work plan process to align upfront on what needs to be 
accomplished in a given year; 
• Improving and streamlining the “solutions matrix” process by adopting a simpler 
“amendment” process, as used in ISO-NE, to increase the likelihood of alignment 
among PJM members;  
• Fixing the CIFP process that gives stakeholders no incentive to converge, since the 
Board reviews every package regardless of support; to fix this issue, a preliminary vote 
could be used to narrow the field to the top two or three stakeholder proposals before 
the final CIFP vote, so fewer options means less-diluted votes and clearer alignment.

We are encouraged by PJM’s initial proposal “to enhance OPSI to include in a bicameral 
structure for both the state commissions and the executive branches of each state” and to 
support additional funding for the expanded OPSI.14 
The core benefit of a single regional state entity, rather than multiple, is the consolidation of 
state input and the pooled resources and support necessary for states to have meaningful 
engagement. Creating two or more separate state entities risks introducing multiple, potentially 
conflicting state voices on the same issues, ultimately diluting rather than strengthening the 
impact of state input. 
Further, the notion that these two groups would naturally divide their focus—with governors’ 
representatives weighing in on strategic, big-picture issues and utility commissioners focusing 
on implementation details—will likely be difficult to sustain in practice. There is too much 
topic overlap and interaction to create and sustain clearly defined lanes; and a single issue will 
often require both kinds of input at once.  
State representation as a single entity, rather than multiple separate bodies, could encompass 
representatives from governors' offices and/or state legislators, in addition to state 
commissioners. NESCOE (the New England States Committee on Electricity) is a good model 
to follow in this regard, as it brings together utility commissioners and gubernatorial 
appointees to represent the interests of the six New England states in a single, well-resourced 
organization.15 Both governors'/legislators' offices and state utility commissions bring 
valuable, and different, perspectives and the aim should be to capture both. OPSI should be 
better resourced, allowing for a permanent staff in a single organization to more efficiently 
equip the states to reach consensus and engage meaningfully with PJM's stakeholder process.16 
PJM’s initial proposal is consistent with these observations. 
In any scenario, individual states and bodies within states retain their right to lobby PJM 
directly and to file at the Commission directly under Section 206, regardless of how a 
collective state entity or entities are structured. 
To the extent the states themselves cannot align on forming a single entity, United respectfully 
requests that, at a minimum, any resulting multiple state groups be closely coordinated, act 
together wherever relevant and possible, and have clearly defined and non-overlapping roles 
and responsibilities.
