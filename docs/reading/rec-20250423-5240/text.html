COMMENTS OF EDF RENEWABLES, INC.  
 
 
Pursuant to Rules 212 and 213 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal 
Energy Regulatory Commission (Commission or FERC),1 EDF Renewables, Inc. (EDFR) 
respectfully submits these comments in response to the answer filed by PJM Interconnection, 
L.L.C. (PJM) on March 24, 2025 (PJM Answer).  The PJM Answer was filed in response to the 
Commission’s Order Instituting Proceeding Under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act and 
Consolidating with Other Proceedings, issued on February 20, 20252 regarding the co-location of 
large loads in PJM.

I. 
SUMMARY OF COMMENTS  
The challenges of serving new large load data centers are present across the country.  
Data centers and their co-location arrangements can take many forms, including structures with 
operational flexibility that can provide a host of benefits to the transmission system, and some 
that have less operational flexibility and thereby place more demands on the transmission 
system.  Although this proceeding is directed at PJM, any decision in this proceeding may have 
implications for other regions as well.  EDFR urges the Commission in this proceeding to adopt 
a flexible, performance-based framework that recognizes large load diversity within the data 
center industry and other industries (hydrogen, e-fuels, and Carbon Capture, Utilization and 
Storage (CCUS)), encourages “grid-light” development structures, and avoids inadvertently 
penalizing innovative co-located load configuration models.  Such a flexible framework can be 
replicated in other regions.  
Co-location can streamline the interconnection process, reduce the need for expensive 
grid upgrades, and minimize delays in bringing new generation and load online.  Not all large 
electricity-consuming facilities that co-locate with existing or new power generators need to 
undergo the same interconnection and reliability planning processes.  Some large loads can 
locate in areas with surplus energy – generation pockets where frequently-curtailed energy is 
“trapped” behind constrained transmission.  Locating large loads in these generation pockets will 
prevent curtailed energy from being wasted, reduce the frequency of the transmission constraints, 
and may allow the load to be integrated faster with less impact on the bulk electric system.  In 
effect, this load could delay the need for flowrate capacity expansion and reduce congestion cost 
in the case of an RTO LMP market structure.  Due to their flexibility, large curtailable loads can

4 
 
provide net benefits to the grid by reducing congestion, delaying need for transmission, and 
providing demand response services when needed.  
In contrast, some co-locating loads are highly dependent on uninterruptible service and 
thus place significantly more demand on the grid.  Loads that lack operational flexibility can lead 
to grid instability, unexpected power shortages and higher costs for other consumers.  Large 
inflexible loads should not dictate a set of rules that apply to more operationally flexible, 
potentially beneficial, co-location arrangements.  Rather, the rules governing large co-located 
loads should be balanced with the ability for grid operators to use the flexibility of that load to 
operate the system more reliably.  Further, the more flexible the load, the simpler it should be to 
interconnect behind a generator’s meter, thus warranting different rules for interconnecting. 
Large loads are not one-size-fits-all and the processes for interconnecting, serving, and 
allocating costs to large loads should accommodate this diversity.  This proceeding presents an 
opportunity for the Commission to establish rules that (1) encourage co-location development in 
a way that allows large flexible co-located loads to interconnect expeditiously and efficiently and 
(2) can be applied to various reliable and commercially beneficial structures across the country.  
II. 
BACKGROUND 
EDFR, develops, builds, operates, and manages renewable energy projects throughout 
North America, including in PJM, MISO, SPP and ERCOT.  As a market-leading independent 
power producer and service provider for over 35 years, EDFR delivers grid-scale power, 
including wind (onshore and offshore), solar photovoltaic, and storage projects; distribution-
scale power, including solar and storage, and asset optimization.  The company also provides 
technical, operational, and commercial expertise to maximize performance of generating

5 
 
projects.  EDFR is an active participant in interconnection-related policy discussions and 
rulemaking proceedings, including interconnection reform efforts.   
EDFR has also been exploring data center co-location in other parts of the country and 
has announced plans for a 166 MW data center to co-locate with EDFR’s 273 MW Las Majadas 
Wind Project in ERCOT.  The data center will connect to EDFR’s private substation and the data 
center and Las Majadas will co-own as tenants-in-common the private electric delivery facilities 
interconnected to the grid pursuant to a Shared Facilities Agreement.  
The data center will utilize behind-the-meter (BTM) power generated by the wind 
project, while also curtailing their operations under certain market conditions when energy is 
most needed by the grid.  This BTM offtake arrangement presents a unique advantage for 
market-exposed renewable projects by physically delivering a portion of a plant’s power directly 
to the co-located buyer’s facility.  This innovative power purchase agreement (PPA) structure 
provides a flexible solution to the challenges of transmission constraints and curtailment, 
essentially allowing an alternate route to capture under-utilized electricity. Concurrently, it 
provides an opportunity for an energy-intensive load that is seeking clean power solutions to 
fulfill that need while advancing computing applications, including artificial intelligence (AI). 
III. 
COMMENTS 
 
As the Commission noted in the February 20 Order, co-located load arrangements are 
becoming increasingly common, in large part driven by the development of new data centers 
(e.g., facilities used for AI, cloud data storage, and cryptocurrency mining, etc.).3  Many of these 
new large load facilities are already operating, and forecasts suggest that many more are in 
development.  Traditional utility structures and current RTO interconnection rules are often

unworkable to meet this new demand, in large part due to the facilities’ near-term need for 
power.  Given the speed with which the new large load facilities can be built, the existing load 
interconnection process and the congested generator interconnection processes currently in place 
are often insufficient to meet the timing of power needs.  Co-location structures have emerged as 
a solution to meet this need.  The question is how these large load facilities can co-locate with 
existing power generators in a manner that is just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory 
for cost allocation purposes and that is reliable from a grid operation perspective.   
A. 
The Commission Should Act Expeditiously to Encourage Flexible Co-
Location Structures that Will Enable Speedy and Reliable Interconnection 
for Large Loads.  
 
This proceeding is an opportunity for the Commission to provide guidance to PJM and 
other RTO/ISO and non-RTO/ISO regions for developing a standardized and transparent load 
interconnection process at the federal level.  A generic rulemaking proceeding would take too 
long.  It is possible that a case-by-case approach can be workable if it is fair and non-
discriminatory, such as the Expedited Project Review Request in MISO.  In some instances, 
existing rules prohibit certain co-location arrangements that should otherwise be allowed.  For 
example, EDFR has found that SPP does not provide a co-location option for optimal 
commercial operation in that region.4  
Here, the Commission has an opportunity to determine what structures work and how 
costs should be allocated, and to identify the barriers to the efficient and reliable co-location of 
large loads.  Lessons learned and best practices identified here should be applicable across other

regions on an expedited basis to ensure that new large loads can be served while maintaining

The Commission can encourage PJM and other RTOs, as well as transmission providers 
in non-RTO regions to develop standardized yet flexible rules to accommodate different co-
location structures with varying operational capabilities.  Specifically, the Commission should:  
1. 
Clarify that co-located loads using non-firm grid power and backed by onsite 
generation may qualify for modified transmission cost allocation (e.g., based on 
usage, not full network load charges). 
2. 
Direct each RTO and ISO to develop and file tariff provisions for co-located load 
and generation under a shared interconnection with clear netting, metering, and 
settlement rules. 
3. 
Consider standardized registration and modeling of flexible co-located load 
behind interconnections. 
4. 
Encourage RTOs/non-RTOs, if not already the case, to simultaneously evaluate 
flexible load and generation with expedited timelines.  
5. 
Ensure that any capacity market participation requirements for co-located 
generation should depend on the type of co-location arrangement. 
6.  
Guarantee that states and local communities can make their own policies 
regarding distribution system interconnection processes and retail rates when co-
located generation is not available to supplying load. 
While Federal rules should be consistent across all regions, they should also be flexible 
enough to allow developers to work within different state rules/policies.  Any Commission action 
in response to PJM’s proposal should enable flexibility in other regions.   
B. 
Co-Locating Large Loads at Existing Generation Sites Can Provide 
Significant Benefits to Existing Generation and the Transmission System. 
Co-location arrangements can benefit the transmission system in many ways.  Large 
flexible co-located load arrangements can help streamline the generator interconnection process 
by reducing the need for expensive grid upgrades and avoiding queue delays in bringing new 
generation online.  Even where new transmission needs to be built, co-location can minimize

PDF page 8

8 
 
required new upgrades.  Co-locating large loads can also reduce congestion costs and provide 
demand response services if the power demands are flexible.  When near-term load growth 
forecasts are high, co-location can help with transmission planning as well.   
Co-location arrangements can provide significant benefits to existing generators.  For 
instance, by locating in areas with surplus energy, large loads can help prevent wasteful 
curtailments.  By using energy that would otherwise be curtailed, large flexible loads like crypto 
mining or batch AI data centers can significantly improve generator economics without stressing 
the grid.  As a result, large interruptible co-located load pairs well with resources like wind and 
solar by using underutilized resources and not requiring new transmission build. 
Co-location arrangements also provide benefits to American industries looking for 
affordable, carbon-free energy. Industrial consumers can access areas of surplus energy to foster 
the growth of globally competitive industries otherwise subject to expensive utility pricing and 
potentially lacking alternative energy options. 
Finally, co-location arrangements will provide benefits to states and communities. Large 
load facilities can provide a host of benefits to local communities in the form of tax revenues, 
construction jobs, and employment.  In this regard, it is important that any rule coming out of 
this proceeding allows states and local communities the freedom to make their own policies 
regarding retail rates and distribution system interconnection processes.   
C. 
Co-Location Arrangements are Not One-Size-Fits-All; Any New Rules 
Should Be Flexible.  
 
As demonstrated by PJM’s eight options to facilitate co-location arrangements, there are 
many potential co-location structures that vary depending on the size and operational flexibility

PDF page 9

9 
 
of the load.  AI data centers that need “five-nines”5 power reliability should not be treated the 
same as crypto mining load that has considerable operating flexibility. For example, EDFR’s Las 
Majadas project is a flexible BTM structure where the data center can curtail when Las Majadas 
generation capacity is needed in the ERCOT market. In contrast, AI data centers typically require 
24/7 firm service and do not provide the same flexibility for the co-located generation resource 
to inject into the grid. Some industries able to co-locate load behind the generator’s meter do not 
require grid firming beyond their auxiliary power needs at all. 
The Commission should ensure that any new rules and processes that are developed have 
flexibility for different co-location arrangements.  Rules should accommodate diverse structures 
while ensuring that those structures pay their fair share of grid costs.  AI data centers with five-
nines reliability needs should not dictate rules for large loads that have significant flexibility.  
The recent PJM example involving Talen Energy’s AI data center paired with the Susquehanna 
nuclear facility presents a much different set of needs and risks than a flexible crypto-mining 
facility paired with an intermittent wind resource.  The latter has significant operational 
flexibility and can even offer services back to the grid that can help with grid operations.  
 
As was developed in ERCOT, the Commission should encourage PJM and other ISOs to 
develop an expedited process for controllable load to interconnect and economically dispatch in 
the wholesale market, or to participate as voluntary load shed resources.  These flexible 
resources present a minimal impact on the transmission system and can even offer system-wide 
reliability benefits while maximizing the efficient use of otherwise underutilized energy 
resources.  In this proceeding, the Commission should encourage:

 Implementation of an expedited interconnection process for controllable co-located 
load resources or voluntary load-shed resources to come online and provide benefits

 Establishment of a requirement that the size of the co-located load must be a certain 
percentage less than the generation at an interconnection point.  (E.g., Load can be up

 Implementation of an ERCOT-type settlement rules during production period so that

In addition, the Commission should recognize the importance of allowing co-location to 
evolve with RTO design.  The Commission should ensure that flexibility is maintained as market 
designs evolve, including recognition of load response, load netting, and demand-side dispatch. 
D. 
Transmission Costs Should be Assigned Based on the Specific Grid Services 
Used in a Particular Co-Location Structure Consistent with the 
Commission’s Beneficiary Pays Principle. 
 
 
Consistent with the Commission’s beneficiary pays principle,6 transmission charges 
should be assigned to large load facilities based on the specific grid services that the load uses 
and the configuration of the co-location structure.  Therefore, a range of transmission system 
charges should be available that match the load’s reliance on the grid.  Co-location structures that 
place minimum demands on the transmission system (i.e., “grid-light”) or that allow load to be

available for demand response or periodic outages to respond to grid congestion should pay less 
in grid costs than co-location structures where the load requires five-nines reliability.  Full 
network load charges should not be the default rate charged to all co-location structures. 
Flexible co-located load that does not rely on the transmission system the same way as 
network connected load should only be assigned charges for grid services when they are taking 
power from the grid.  For example, under ERCOT’s generation netting rules, generation 
resources that are co-located behind a shared POI are netted together such that in each market 
settlement interval if more power is flowing out (net generation) then it is settled as generation 
and no grid charges apply.  If more power is flowing in during the interval (net load), then it is 
settled as load with applicable grid charges.  
EDFR appreciates PJM’s efforts to develop Options 1 through 8 outlining potential co-
location structures.7  These options, some already existing and some that need further 
development, show that there is considerable variation in potential co-location configurations 
with differing reliance on grid services.  Grid charges should be assessed according to the co-
location arrangement, the flexibility of that load, and its reliance on the grid.  Thus, the 
Commission should affirm that discrete delivery points with flexible arrangements, mostly 
behind-the-meter use, and minimal reliance on the grid should not be swept into a network load 
classification.  Only co-location structures with load that regularly and materially use the grid 
should pay network load transmission charges, consistent with the Commission’s beneficiary 
pays principle.

IV. 
CONCLUSION 
For the foregoing reasons, EDFR requests that the Commission adopt a flexible, 
performance-based framework that recognizes load diversity, encourages “grid-light” 
development, and avoids inadvertently penalizing innovative renewable-first load models.
