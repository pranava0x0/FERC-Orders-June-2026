THE SOLAR ENERGY INDUSTRIES ASSOCIATION

Pursuant to the July 30, 2026 notice inviting comments,1 MAREC Action2 and the Solar Energy Industries Association (“SEIA”)3 submit these recommended governance reforms for the PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) Board and stakeholder process.

MAREC Action and SEIA represent developers of all different sizes and business models. These run the gamut from small rooftop solar installers to large multinational corporations. It includes both public and private companies, some are North American subsidiaries of international companies, some are owned by pension funds or private equity funds, are smaller owner-operated businesses. They have a variety of business models. Some are owner-operators, others build and sell projects, while others build projects under contract to other entities. But they all have something in common—they want to get generation projects built in the PJM region to serve the growing load.

Many of these developers represented by MAREC Action and SEIA have project
development experience outside of PJM, both in other Regional Transmission Organization (“RTO”) markets and in non-RTO regions. Some, that are subsidiaries of international

I. Stakeholders need insight into Board direction and decisions
Insight into Board decisions is sorely lacking in PJM. Stakeholders have very little knowledge of how and why the Board acts as it does s. Many decisions in the recent past seem to be rushed attempts to put out fires rather than deliberate well-planned decisions that would move PJM into the future. Short-term fixes seem to have become the norm. PJM has jumped from emergency to emergency, establishing reforms that are simultaneously rushed and too late. This can be seen in the multiple uses of the Critical Issues Fast Path Process over the last few years that have resulted in some major changes to the PJM markets with very little insight into how the Board arrived at those decisions.

Meanwhile, reforms that could address many of the fundamental problems, such as comprehensive transmission and generation interconnection planning, and energy and capacity market reforms, have fallen by the wayside. For years, as we watched other regions move to more granular markets, stakeholders have told themselves, “this is the year PJM will enact

A. Recommendation: The Board and PJM should work with stakeholders to
identify initiatives and develop an annual priorities plan.
Identifying initiatives and then setting annual priorities is fundamental to any organization. The Board and PJM staff need to work with stakeholders to identify critical needs and then prioritize the work. PJM has conducted some of this type of priority planning in the past but then failed to hold to those priorities. To be fair, the constant putting out of fires has interfered with this. However, if many of the long-time fundamental issues were identified earlier and worked on more holistically, the fires would not be as critical. Addressing these emergency needs must be done in the context of longer-term priorities. PJM just released a new strategy document, Shaping a Powerful Change, PJM Five-Year Strategy.6 While this is a start, the document is high-level and lacking in actionable elements. We hope that more is to come in the form of an actual roadmap and workplan outlining the detailed policies and procedures that should be prioritized and worked.

B. Improvements to Board Transparency
1. Recommendation: The Board should host open meetings, publish roll
call votes, and issue decisional memos with their directives.
PJM’s Board meetings are closed. Cursory, high-level minutes of those meetings are provided after the Board meetings. This is not indicative of good governance. Transparency measures must be implemented to ensure that Members, States, and other stakeholders can engage effectively and meaningfully with the PJM Board. Essential transparency measures include open Board meetings, published roll call Board votes, detailed Board meetings minutes that show the rationale for the Board’s vote, and decisional memos between the Board and PJM Staff.

2. Recommendation: Board members should schedule regular meetings
with Members, States, and Stakeholders.
Access to the Board would be another important step towards good governance. Although the Board meets with the Liaison Committee and has private meetings with the Organization of PJM States, Inc. (“OPSI”) Board three times a year, these meetings are not

II. Recommendation: Consider moving to an advisory voting system.
As stated repeatedly throughout this proceeding, the PJM stakeholder process has become slow and unfocused. There are tools to speed up the process, like the Critical Issue Fast Path which is used to address “time-constrained major issues or existing work efforts that have not achieved, or are unlikely to achieve, consensus.”16 But, as PJM noted in its pre-conference comments, the CIFP has recently resulted in failures to achieve any sort of consensus in a timely manner.17 Such failures will likely continue to happen, since controversial issues cannot move forward without frustrating one group or another. PJM leadership and the Board must be empowered to make difficult decisions. Member advisory voting grants that power. As a practical matter, moving to an advisory voting structure does not change the status quo. According to PJM, “PJM and its stakeholders reach consensus on the majority of issues considered.”18 And with respect to the CIFP process, the votes are already advisory.19 The recommendations made in this proceeding will likely lead to more supported, and sustainable, proposals in the longer-term. However, in the near-term, PJM will be forced to make hard decisions, and it must have the ability to act quickly and decisively. By moving to a member

III. The PJM stakeholder process is in dire need of reform.
Over the years, the process has become slow, unfocused,20 and polarizing.21 The recommendations below would help create a stakeholder process that will ensure robust proposals that address concerns by different sectors and allow PJM to move quickly to address emerging issues.

A. Recommendation: At beginning of stakeholder process, PJM staff should establish the objectives any proposed solution must satisfy and what solutions cannot be entertained.

MAREC Action and SEIA appreciate that PJM leadership welcomes multiple viewpoints throughout the stakeholder process. Hearing from a wide range of stakeholders can create reforms that accomplish PJM’s goals while also reflecting operational realities and market needs. But PJM and its stakeholders should not be spending hours discussing proposals that have elements that cannot be implemented because they are operationally infeasible. To create meaningful reforms, PJM needs to refocus stakeholders on what solutions can be pursued. At the start of each stakeholder process, PJM must clearly define the problem they are seeking to solve, the timeframe for action, and any operational limitations that may limit solutions. PJM issue charges and problem statements do define the problem, set the timing, and

C. Improvements to polling:
Because MAREC Action and SEIA are recommending that PJM consider moving to an advisory voting structure, the remaining recommendations in this section reflect changes to the polling process to allow PJM to gain more insight into the potential market impact on their tariff proposals.

1. Recommendation: Throughout the process, PJM should conduct
polling on specific elements of its proposals.
In addition to gaining critical input through written comments, PJM should seek input on individual elements of a proposal, and should do so early and often throughout the stakeholder process. Polling reports should be divided out by sector and by business type. Voting on entire proposals could result in situations in which small, but impactful elements of a proposal are voted down because they are attached to a proposal that has elements that are unworkable to certain market participants. Polling specific elements of a proposal will allow PJM to understand what changes are necessary to develop a more supportive, and therefore more successful, proposal.

2. Recommendation: When polling members, PJM should reflect poll
results with more granularity.
MAREC Action and SEIA’s member companies participate in PJM as generation
developers. Generation developers are spread between two different sectors: the Generation Owner sector and the Other Supplier sector. This split is because the Operating Agreement differentiates between incumbent resource owners, which are part of the Generation Owner

IV. Strengthening State Participation in the PJM Stakeholder Process
State retail regulators have a statutory responsibility to ensure resource adequacy at the lowest reasonable cost and are directly accountable to consumers for retail electricity rates. Yet many of the rules governing resource adequacy are developed through the PJM stakeholder process, in which state regulators lack a formal role sufficient to effectively advance and implement their solutions. This disconnect between state responsibility and stakeholder authority should be addressed by strengthening the states’ role in PJM governance.

MAREC and SEIA support reforms that would provide states, through OPSI, with a more meaningful and actionable voice in the PJM stakeholder process. Specifically, PJM Transmission Owners should make Section 205 filings on OPSI’s behalf when OPSI reaches consensus on cost allocation methods for regional transmission projects. In addition, if PJM is given authority to make unilateral Section 205 filings, PJM should also be required to make filings on OPSI’s behalf regarding regional transmission planning.

These changes would better align PJM’s governance structure with practices already in place in other RTOs. More importantly, these changes recognize the states’ statutory responsibilities and ensure that the economic regulators accountable to retail consumers have a meaningful, effective, and actionable voice in the PJM decisions that directly affect them.
