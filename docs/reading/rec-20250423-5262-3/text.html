Q13. HOW DOES THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN PEAK DEMAND AND
1
TRANSMISSION COSTS IMPACT RATE DESIGN?
2
A13. The fact that transmission costs are incurred by a need to serve peak demand is the 3

basis for demand charges, which are the industry-standard approach to recovering 4

transmission costs across the U.S. 19  Customers with higher coincident peak 5

demands20 on the transmission system cause a higher portion of total system costs 6

and are therefore allocated a higher share of total system costs. In PJM, the Network 7

Service Peak Load metric represents a customer’s contribution to peak load of the 8

transmission system and is used to allocate transmission service charges for 9

customers that take Network Service.21
10
The fact that these demand charges are nearly universal in the industry to 11

recover transmission costs is a direct reflection of the relationship between 12

transmission costs and peak demand. Put differently, if the size of a customer’s load 13

on the transmission system during the system peak were not indicative of the costs 14

this customer caused, then there would be no basis for differentiating charges based 15

on the size of a customer’s peak load.

17

• Generator-contingent: This represents a configuration where co-located 1

from the grid when it is not. In other words, if the co-located generator is 3

either load curtailment or additional backup generation, but it must be 11

enforceable and long-duration. This means that PJM must be certain that 12

the co-located load will interrupt whenever system conditions require 13

without any limit applied to the length of the interruption.
14

system on an as-available basis.

A53. The resource adequacy impact of a new load is not dependent upon whether it is 4

connected directly to the transmission system or co-located, but instead upon the 5

type of arrangement under which PJM provides that load with transmission service. 6

For example:
7
• If the load is connected directly to the transmission system and takes 8

NITS, it increases demand for capacity which increases the capacity 9

price.
10
• If a load is co-located with a dedicated generator (for example in a 11

generator-contingent configuration), this decreases the supply of 12

capacity (since the generator is dedicated to the load and no longer 13

offering as a capacity resource) which increases the capacity price. 14

15
In both cases, increased load – either directly or indirectly – causes higher 16

capacity prices, which incentivizes more capacity to enter the market. This, in turn, 17

drives the capacity price back down into equilibrium. In fact, new capacity often 18

pre-emptively plans to enter the market in anticipation of higher demand (or lower 19

supply), meaning that a higher capacity price may never materialize in the first 20

place. Finally, because new capacity enters the market in response to higher prices, 21

there is no expected resource adequacy impact due to additional load, either in a 22

co-located or NITS configuration.
