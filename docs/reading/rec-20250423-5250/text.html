INTRODUCTION 
The Commission’s existing pro forma open access transmission tariff (“OATT”), 
and PJM’s OATT-compliant tariff (“PJM Tariff”), already address co-located load in a 
just and reasonable manner. Either network integration transmission service (“network 
service”) or point-to-point transmission service (“point-to-point service”) can apply to co-
located load arrangements under the PJM Tariff and render just and reasonable results 
when properly applied. Network service is the preferred designation to ensure co-located 
load is addressed in a just and reasonable manner, as such designation will reduce 
impacts on existing ratepayers and prevent negative externalities such as free-riding. 
Given the variables involved in point-to-point service, each application of point-to-point 
service would need to be individually evaluated to determine whether it can be applied in 
a just and reasonable manner. The JCA thus support, in part, the position of the Indicated 
Transmission Owners and PJM Interconnection, LLC.

COMMENTS 
I. 
PJM’s current tariff provisions on co-located load arrangements can 
produce just and reasonable results. 
 
A. 
Classifying co-located load as front-of-the-meter network service 
minimizes risks to existing customers compared to other 
configurations. 
Co-located load is load from a large commercial or industrial customer such as a 
data center that has a contractual relationship with an adjacent generating facility for its 
supply of electricity. The PJM Tariff considers co-located load arrangements as network 
service or point-to-point service.1 Under these classifications, co-located load is studied 
to allocate the costs of reliability services, such as ancillary services, and for the impact 
the co-located load will have on the grid for planning purposes. Co-located load receiving 
network service is responsible to PJM for the costs associated with serving its load and 
would be included in PJM’s planning process.2  
Classifying co-located load as front-of-the meter network service is thus 
preferrable from a reliability and cost causation standpoint. When such a large amount of 
load is “off-network” but still benefitting from the transmission system, a basic principle 
of cost allocation is frustrated—the principle that those who cause the costs and reap the 
benefits, bear responsibility for the costs.3 The appearance of co-located load on the grid 
could have the same effect as generation retirement, triggering transmission upgrades

network service customers, if co-located load is located behind the meter, the entity co-

initial upgrades needed because of the co-location arrangement or future upgrades needed 
to serve all customers in the service territory. Other ratepayers, not the cost causers, 
would instead bear these costs, as described in the TO’s Answer.  
The impact on customers from treating services for co-located load as non-
network service could be substantial. As Exelon warned in its comments in Docket No. 
EL25-20 and as the TO’s highlight in their answer, even a relatively small co-located load 
carveout of 480 megawatt (“MW”) from network service in the Southern Maryland 
Electric Cooperative (“SMECO”) territory—where the Calvert Cliffs nuclear facility is 
located—would result in the avoidance of over $53 million in charges per year.4 The 
financial impacts more than double if one gigawatt (“GW”) of load is not considered 
network service, allowing the co-located load customer to shift $112 million of annual 
avoided transmission charges onto other ratepayers. Even if some benefits were to accrue 
to non-cost causers, the ratepayer impact that such an arrangement would cause is too 
large to ignore. The PJM Tariff covering network service prevents this illegal cost 
shifting and should thus apply to co-located load.

Although co-located load is located behind the point of interconnection, it is 
always electrically connected and synchronized to the transmission system; the generator 
cannot operate or supply the co-located load without that connection to the transmission 
system. As the TOs and the Union of Concerned Scientists (“UCS”) emphasize, it is 
economically and technically impossible to “fully isolate” co-located load from the 
transmission grid.5 Indeed, co-location with nuclear generation is being widely discussed, 
and nuclear industry standards require synchronization with the grid for stability, off-site 
power, and restoration. Co-location with baseload generation also poses reliability 
concerns, which are exacerbated if the co-located load is considered non-network load. 
As the Maryland Public Service Commission opined in its recent report on co-located 
load:  
If [non]-network load co-locates with baseload generation, such as the 
Calvert Cliffs nuclear plant, significant quantities of existing capacity will 
be effectively removed from the grid and resource adequacy may be 
degraded at an accelerated pace relative to connecting such load in 
traditional ways. Baseload capacity of the type Calvert Cliffs provides 
could take years to plan, certificate, and build.6 
Therefore, the Commission should clarify that co-located load is always network 
load, with a preference for network service, to prevent evasion of grid service charges 
and transmission grid upgrade charges to the detriment of other customers.

B. 
PJMs’ proposed new options four and five, with special protection 
schemes, are misplaced and unnecessary.  
The Commission should reject PJM’s suggested configurations that offer 
protective schemes to co-located load that is non-network load—specifically new options 
four and five as detailed in Exhibit A to the PJM Answer. As the TOs underscore in their 
answer, inadvertently or directly creating a new class of service, especially non-network 
load, for co-located load is unnecessary and unduly preferential.7 PJM itself cautions 
against these options that involve complex special protection schemes to “prevent” 
withdrawal from the grid, conceding in its answer that such schemes in place at the 
Susquehanna Steam Electric Nuclear Power Facility — which has an interconnection 
agreement with a co-located data center —failed, causing the data center to instead rely 
on the PJM grid for its supply during an unplanned outage.8 Monitoring Analytics, the 
independent market monitor for PJM, also expressed concern over the issues of backup 
power for co-located load arrangements and emphasized it is “not possible for co-located 
load to be off the grid.”9 Moreover, as UCS notes, the variability of power demand from 
data centers creates “imbalances more quickly than generators can correct, resulting in [] 
voltage disturbances, thermal overloads and stability issues.”10 The risk that the 
protective measures in these complex options will fail is extremely high given that such 
variability affects multiple aspects of PJM service, such as ancillary services,

II. 
The Commission should defer to state law control over how the costs of 
the wholesale sale and transmission of electricity are allocated among 
retail customers.  
Maryland recently passed legislation revising the definition of “retail electric 
customer” to include co-located load customers and specifying that a generating facility 
with co-located load can supply retail electricity in these instances.12 Once signed into 
law, this definitional revision will enhance jurisdictional clarity for co-located load within 
Maryland. As the Commission notes, “states retain exclusive jurisdiction over the terms 
of retail sales, generally including the rate designs that determine how the costs of the 
wholesale sale and transmission of electricity assigned to a wholesale customer are

network from which they benefit.” Maryland’s new legislation clarifies how state law 
advances the important goal of ensuring all customers pay their fair share of transmission 
system costs, including co-located load customers.  
CONCLUSION 
Co-located load affects and benefits from the PJM grid. Consequent policy 
decisions about co-located load in PJM will thus have significant implications for all PJM 
customers, in Maryland, New Jersey, and in the PJM region at large. A straightforward
