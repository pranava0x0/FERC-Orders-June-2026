PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM’s”) application in this case to implement a one- time Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) program to acquire new generating capacity needed to serve forecasted Artificial Intelligence (“AI”) and other large data center loads should not be adopted.1 It will unreasonably shift the risks and costs associated with acquiring that new capacity from the data center loads to existing consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM.  The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”) should reject the filing because it fails to protect Ohio consumers and other consumers in PJM from the risks associated with this new forecasted load, and from the associated costs of serving these loads.  PJM’s proposal will result in excessive, unjust, unreasonable and unduly discriminatory and preferential rates for existing consumers.

In the alternative, FERC should set PJM’s filing for evidentiary hearings and/or settlement proceedings so that PJM and stakeholders can adequately consider and vet alternative proposals for acquiring needed new supply for AI and other large data center loads, including

II. PROTEST
OCC protests PJM’s filing because it will not minimize costs for consumer, nor
accomplish its objectives of acquiring new generating capacity in the region in a manner that allocates risks and costs for the new capacity to those causing the costs to be incurred, i.e., AI and large data center loads.  Instead, the proposal will result in shifting these risks and costs to existing consumers who have not caused the need for new generating capacity and who are unlikely to use the generating capacity provided by these new resources.  The risks and costs include the realistic uncertainty that either generating capacity or the forecasted AI or large data center loads will not show up.  Moreover, the proposal inappropriately and unreasonably relies on state laws and regulations, that may not exist in many states, to ensure that these costs and risks in fact are borne by the AI and large data center loads.

Ohio has no statutes governing allocation of large load-related generation capacity costs among retail customers.  And while there is one case in which the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio (“PUCO”) has directed allocation of some of these large load driven costs and risks to the large load data center customers, there are no uniform regulations and tariffs that apply to all utilities and LSEs in the state.25  Thus, PJM’s proposed RBP program leaves many Ohio consumers at significant risk of bearing costs associated with these new large data center loads. In contrast, the backstop procurement proposal prepared by the PJM IMM and proposals discussed by other stakeholders in PJM would directly allocate the risks and costs of this new

OCC is also concerned that PJM’s RBP proposal does little to rectify or minimize the excessive costs existing consumers in Ohio and elsewhere in PJM are paying due to RPM auctions that have already been run for prior years. The IMM has calculated that data center demand increased capacity costs to other PJM customers by approximately $23.1 billion across the 2025/2026, 2026/2027, 2027/2028 Base Residual Auctions.36  The $23.1 billion figure is not a mere historical artifact of load growth. It reflects a structural outcome of a capacity market in which extraordinary new demand (data centers) can raise clearing prices across a much larger base of existing capacity (other customers including residential customers). The result is a system-wide cost shift that extends far beyond the incremental load that triggered it. The IMM’s June 30, 2026 RBP proposal, discussed in section C below, demonstrates that significant reforms to PJM’s RBP proposal are needed to stop the historical subsidies already being provided by existing consumers to AI and large data center loads.

C. The IMM proposed approach to resource adequacy in PJM will ensure
direct allocation of the risk and costs of needed new generation to the
responsible AI and large data center loads in the region.
On June 30, 2026, Monitoring Analytics, the PJM IMM, posted on its website its proposal for resolving the resource inadequacy concerns created by the shortages in the 2027/2028 and 2028/2029 Delivery Years.  The IMM submitted this proposal to the PJM Critical Issue Fast Path stakeholder process as an alternative to the PJM RBP proposal.42  As the IMM states, “[t]he most important criterion for evaluating proposals to address how to reliably serve new data center load is the assignment of the costs and risks associated with LSEs serving data center load.”43  The IMM explains that “the failure to exclude new data center load from the BRAs [RPM base residual auctions], as PJM proposes, would by definition impose significant costs on other customers and therefore violate the basic principle that data center costs should not be imposed on other customers.”44

III. CONCLUSION
FERC may accept or reject a utility filing under section 205 of the Federal Power Act, 16 U.S.C. 824d, depending on whether  the utility has demonstrated that its proposal is just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential.59  However, as PJM notes in its RBP Application, FERC may not modify substantial elements of the proposal without PJM’s consent.60

 Although OCC seeks rejection of PJM’s proposal in this case, OCC agrees that
something must be done soon to address the resource adequacy concerns in the PJM region over the next few years.  Consequently, OCC urges that if FERC accepts the filing, it should do so subject to refund and set the filing for hearing and/or settlement proceedings with directives that the parties use the IMM RBP proposal as a starting point.

Such a process will provide stakeholders an opportunity, subject to Settlement Judge oversight, to develop a just, reasonable and not unduly discriminatory or preferential solution that will protect existing consumers in Ohio and elsewhere from the unreasonable shift of AI and large data center load risks and costs to them, and from excessive, unjust, unreasonable and unduly discriminatory and preferential rates.
