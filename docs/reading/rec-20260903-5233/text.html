PROTEST OF GOOGLE LLC

Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission”),1 Google LLC (“Google”) hereby submits this protest regarding PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) Interim Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”) and Large Load Registry proposal.

Google’s concerns with IRAS center on the proposed mechanism, not the underlying objectives. Specifically, Google agrees that New Large Loads that do not bring sufficient new capacity to offset their expected demand should be subject to curtailment prior to other firm wholesale customers during tight system conditions, ensuring service to residential and other commercial customers remains reliable.  Consistent with this principle, a longstanding and foundational pillar of Google’s energy procurement philosophy has been that Google should enable sufficient new capacity to meet its energy demand.

IRAS would stymie that goal.  It would inject fresh uncertainty into PJM, threatening desperately needed investments to build out the region’s generation fleet.  The fundamental flaw in IRAS is PJM’s proposal to assign general curtailment obligations to Electric Distributors (“EDs”) with New Large Loads that do not bring new capacity, despite lacking jurisdiction over how EDs execute those curtailments..  Consequently, in the event of a shortage, New Large Loads

1. IRAS Exceeds the Commission’s Jurisdiction
IRAS exceeds the Commission’s jurisdiction because it targets particular retail customers: “New Large Loads.”  FPA Section 201(b) “‘limit[s] FERC’s sale jurisdiction to that at wholesale,’ reserving regulatory authority over retail sales (as well as intrastate wholesale sales) to the States.”12  “FERC cannot take an action transgressing that limit no matter how direct, or dramatic, its impact on wholesale rates.”13  To respect that limit, FERC and entities like PJM are prohibited from taking actions that “aim[]” at or “target” particular retail consumers or the retail market generally.14  Instead, as relevant here, a Commission order adheres to the FPA’s jurisdictional divide only where “every aspect of the regulatory plan happens exclusively on the wholesale market and governs exclusively that market’s rules.”15

IRAS violates that principle.  FERC cannot approve tariff provisions where retail consumers are the “target at which [those tariff provisions] aim.” 16  IRAS breaks that rule because it targets retail customers by assigning the wholesale curtailment obligation based on the size (i.e., more than 50 MW) and vintage (i.e., in-service  date and ramp rate) of the relevant retail customers.17  In so doing, the proposal makes the characteristics of the retail customer, not that of the ED, LSE, or Eligible Customer, or any other Commission-jurisdictional entity or transaction, the basis for imposing a curtailment obligation. Finally, although PJM characterizes its proposal as “a product of cooperative federalism,” it is, in fact, quite the opposite.  Cooperative federalism is rooted in the concept that the federal entity need not, and should not, exercise the full extent of its authority when addressing issues that affect both federal and state interests.  For example, in EPSA, while the Commission claimed that it could negate state decisions prohibiting retail consumers from making wholesale demand response bids, it “chose not to do so in recognition of the linkage between wholesale and retail markets and the States’ role in overseeing retail sales.”  That was a scheme of cooperative federalism in “which the States retain[ed] the last word.”  Here, by contrast, PJM has created a scheme that it lacks the authority to carry out and so it has, instead, unilaterally foisted onto the states the responsibility for implementing IRAS without consulting with them on the program’s design.29  The principle of cooperative federalism does not describe a program that would have the federal government exceed its authority and then leave it to the states to resolve the resulting complexities.

3. IRAS Is Unjust and Unreasonable
Last but not least, IRAS is also unjust and unreasonable for two independent reasons. First, PJM is proposing to charge customers for a service that it never intends to procure. Specifically, PJM proposes to exclude from the Variable Resource Requirement Curve (i.e., the capacity market demand curve) New Large Loads that do not bring their own new capacity, meaning that it would, by definition, not procure capacity to serve them, even if that capacity is available.  Nevertheless, PJM proposes to charge the LSEs that will service those same New Large Loads for a share of the capacity (even though none of that capacity will have been procured for them).  Being forced to pay money for nothing is not just and reasonable or consistent with cost causation.

C. A Lawful and Durable Framework Can Protect Residential Customers While
Preserving Incentives for Large Loads to Bring New Capacity
Accepting IRAS would be an egregious misstep because there are ample alternative avenues through which PJM, in cooperation with the States, could facilitate the goals underlying IRAS, rather than impede them.

First, with the appropriate data management and confidentiality guarantees, the Commission should accept PJM’s proposed Large Load Registry as it would provide much needed transparency without the jurisdictional concerns inherent in IRAS.  Second, PJM should reform its wholesale demand response rules39 to unlock the flexibility that large loads are capable of providing.  As explained below, Google has been a pioneer in unlocking data centers’ latent flexibility.  And Google has consistently advocated for demand response reforms at PJM to make data center-driven demand response a pillar of the solution to the resource adequacy challenges associated with rapid load growth.  Breaking down the barriers to demand response supplied by data centers would go a long way to resolving the resource adequacy challenge that motivated PJM

1. The Commission Should Accept the Large Load Registry, Which Will
Facilitate Cooperative Federalism

2. PJM Should Reform Demand Response to Unlock the Resource Adequacy
Value of Large Loads and Other Flexible Demand
Before implementing unprecedented and unworkable new curtailment regimes like IRAS, PJM should focus on making the most of the flexibility that can be provided through more familiar and jurisdictionally sound avenues, such as wholesale market demand response.

PJM’s current demand response regime sidelines significant quantities of flexible load. That is because it presents them with a stark choice: either be available to curtail at any time, with no limits on how long curtailments can last or receive no capacity value for the more limited flexibility they can reliably provide.  But demand response rules in PJM do not need to be an all- or-nothing proposition.  Instead, PJM should establish bounded demand response products with defined availability parameters and accredit those products to reflect participating loads’ marginal contribution to reliability.  Doing so could unlock substantial new demand-side capability. Reforming demand response is a commonsense path to ensuring that existing residential and other existing retail customers continue to receive reliable service.  During the CIFP process, PJM expressed concern that dispatching demand response more frequently to address the reliability effects of load growth could lead to demand response attrition.  But if some existing resources cannot perform under PJM’s current availability requirements when called upon, the answer is not to develop an unworkable and illegal curtailment scheme for involuntarily curtailing certain large loads—i.e., IRAS.  Rather, PJM should create demand response products that require loads to curtail less frequently in exchange for a lower capacity value, which would facilitate voluntary curtailments and unlock the capacity needed to maintain resource adequacy.

3. The Commission Should Order PJM to Create a Program of Genuine
Cooperative Federalism that Relies Upon State-Approved Interruptible Load
Tariffs
As explained above, the question at the heart of this proceeding—which retail customers should be disconnected from the grid in the event demand exceeds supply—is one that is within exclusive state jurisdiction.  On that point, Google agrees with PJM.52   Rather than fight that fact or attempt to bootstrap its way into regulating matters under exclusive state jurisdiction, PJM should embrace the limits of the FPA and develop an actual “program of cooperative federalism” that supports states in pursuing the goals that purportedly underlie IRAS.  To do so, PJM should establish protocols pursuant to which it would send a signal under certain, defined conditions to disconnect retail customers that take service under state interruptible large load tariffs pursuant to the rules and priorities established under those tariffs.

Those tariffs could resemble the model state interruptible tariff that the Virginia State Corporation Commission and the Data Center Coalition have committed to develop.53   The key features should include customer eligibility and classification criteria, the circumstances and procedures for interruption, applicable service rights and obligations, and other associated tariff provisions.
