It is not.  The critical distinction is structural: RPM Entities may rely on the RPM auction as a backstop to meet their capacity obligations, whereas FRR Entities cannot.  Instead, FRR load can come online only if it is served by capacity that the FRR Entity has contracted for or owns.  This fundamental difference in capacity obligation design dooms the claims of undue discrimination or preferential treatment, because the Commission has long held that there is no undue discrimination when entities are

differently situated.5  Moreover, even if FRR Entities and RPM Entities were similarly situated—which they are not—PJM has articulated a sound basis for the differential treatment, and the Commission has confirmed that “there is no undue discrimination or preference [when] we have identified appropriate reasons for it.”6  Thus, the Commission should reject commenters’ discrimination arguments.

flawed reasoning.  EKPC argues that the IRAS Proposal is unduly discriminatory because FRR Entities are exempt from IRAS while RPM Entities that have prudently planned

The question is not whether an entity has prudently planned capacity. Thus, they are not similarly situated.  The structural difference also determines whether the entity contributes to the RPM auction shortfall that IRAS is designed to address.

The FRR Alternative imposes stringent self-supply obligations that distinguish FRR Entities from RPM Entities.  An FRR Entity must identify the load it expects to serve and must own, build, or contract for the capacity necessary to serve that load in each delivery year.9  Critically, the FRR Entity must procure sufficient capacity to serve not only anticipated load but also any actual load that materializes during the Delivery Year.10  If the FRR Entity’s forecast proves wrong, it has no safety net because it cannot turn to the RPM auction for additional capacity.11

At the same time, the IRAS Proposal does not give FRR Entities unlimited flexibility.  Recognizing that FRR Entities could otherwise draw Existing Generation Capacity Resources away from the RPM auction and exacerbate the capacity shortfall that IRAS seeks to remedy, the IRAS Proposal imposes limitations on FRR Entities.  An FRR Entity may use Existing Generation Capacity Resources to serve New Large Load only if the FRR Entity owned or contracted for those resources before June 1, 2027.15  The June 1, 2027 cutoff serves dual purposes.  First, it preserves the operational flexibility that FRR Entities need to structure their FRR Plans.  Second, it protects the RPM auction by preventing FRR Entities from acquiring additional Existing Generation Capacity Resources after the cutoff date to serve New Large Load and ensures that the IRAS Proposal’s goal of addressing RPM auction shortfalls is not undermined by capacity migration to FRR service.16  Again, these arguments that IRAS is unduly discriminatory or results in preferential treatment fail and should be rejected.

In response, PJM contends that these clarifications would “undermine the overarching policy driving IRAS and the Ratepayer Protection Pledge” because the clarifications would not result in FRR Entities serving new Large Load with new capacity.19  PJM’s response mischaracterizes how FRR works. C. The IRAS Proposal Should Incentivize Alignment with Its Stated Goals
and Avoid Jurisdictional Vulnerability.
AEP supports commenters who argue the Commission should ensure IRAS
incentivizes actions that align with the service’s goals and that the tariff changes are legally durable.  For example, IRAS aims to address capacity shortfall in the RPM auction by requiring RPM Entities to Bring Your Own New Capacity (“BYONC”) for Eligible New Large Load, but commenters argue that the proposal leaves it uncertain whether BYONC will allow Eligible New Large Load to avoid being curtailed under IRAS.22  Commenters argue that the IRAS Proposal is structured in a way that leaves open the possibility that Eligible New Large Load could be curtailed under IRAS even if such load has BYONC.23 Thus, AEP encourages the Commission to address any incentive misalignment present in the IRAS Proposal.

Commenters also highlight aspects of the IRAS Proposal that risk succumbing to legal challenge.  They note that PJM’s proposal attempts to thread the needle between state and federal jurisdiction, but this narrow path to “cooperative federalism” is vulnerable to challenge.24  Unlike recent large load tariff reforms that PJM claims are analogous to IRAS,

D. Any Suggestion That AEP’s Ohio Large Load Tariff Is Not Protecting
Retail Customers Is Unfounded.
Finally, the Office of the Ohio Consumers’ Counsel (“OCC”) contends that AEP’s state large load tariff in Ohio26 “does not adequately protect consumers from the federal curtailments PJM is proposing for large load customers” under IRAS.27  To the extent OCC suggests that AEP’s Ohio large load tariff fails to protect Ohio retail customers, its assertion is unfounded.  The AEP Ohio large load tariff was approved by the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio and is designed to recognize the importance of large loads and to ensure they can connect and be served while protecting retail customers from unnecessary cost increases.  OCC provides no evidence that the AEP Ohio large load tariff is not working as intended.  Any suggestion otherwise is misleading.

III. CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, AEP respectfully requests that the Commission:  (1) reject commenters’ claims that the IRAS Proposal’s treatment of FRR Entities is unduly discriminatory or preferential because FRR Entities are not similarly situated to RPM Entities (2) reject PJM’s claims that the clarification sought by AEP will allow it to avoid the proposed requirement to serve new Large Load with new capacity; and (3) ensure the IRAS Proposal is legally durable and incentivizes actions that align with its stated goals.
