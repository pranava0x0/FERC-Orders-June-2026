REQUEST FOR CLARIFICATION OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, REHEARING
OF CONSTELLATION ENERGY GENERATION, LLC
Constellation Energy Generation, LLC (“Constellation”) requests clarification, or in the alternative, rehearing, of the order issued by the Commission on December 18, 2025, in the above- captioned dockets.1 The Commission should clarify two discrete issues. First, consistent with existing rules for non-firm exports, a capacity resource should not be required to delist when it is serving Co-Located Load2 taking non-firm contract demand transmission service, so long as the capacity resource has no obligation to serve the Co-Located Load during a capacity emergency. In that scenario, the capacity resource remains available to serve the grid when needed for resource adequacy; forcing the capacity resource nevertheless to delist would needlessly remove available capacity from serving the grid. Moreover, the Commission should seek to encourage this type of arrangement—in which the generation resource remains available to the grid in capacity emergencies, and the Co-Located Load either curtails or self-supplies using backup generation—

ARGUMENT
I. The Commission Should Clarify that Capacity Resources are Not Required to Delist Before Making Interruptible Commitments.

Read by its terms, the December 18th Order could appear to require the delist of capacity resources even when the capacity resources remain fully capable of serving the grid during capacity emergencies. The Order states, “[i]n accordance with the existing rules for removing Capacity Resource status, … an existing generating facility may not begin serving Co-Located Load that is not Network Load until it has delisted its [capacity interconnection rights] for the portion of the generating facility to be designated to serve any Co-Located Load that is not receiving [Network Integration Transmission Service (NITS)].”5

But generators and Co-Located Load may seek contractual arrangements in which, during a capacity emergency, the generator will serve the grid rather than the Co-Located Load. The Co- Located Load, in turn, could either curtail or rely on its own backup generation. Constellation seeks clarification that the Commission did not intend to discourage or frustrate those arrangements or insist the generation that is capable of supplying the grid during a capacity performance event delist.

Indeed, the Commission should encourage arrangements in which Co-Located Load can receive non-NITS service (such as non-firm contract service) and also curtail its reliance on the Co-Located generator during capacity emergencies. Such an arrangement allows the interconnection of new data center load, important to our economic competitiveness and national security; the co-location of that load, minimizing the need for grid upgrades; and the continued

II. The Commission Should Clarify the Scope of its Jurisdiction Over a Generator’s
Wholesale Sales and Transmission.
Constellation agrees with the Commission’s conclusions regarding the scope of its
jurisdiction but there are two statements in the December 18th Order that could be misinterpreted in a manner inconsistent with settled jurisdictional principles. Accordingly, Constellation respectfully requests that the Commission clarify the following two points.

First, in the December 18th Order, the Commission concludes that “states have authority
over their generation resource mix, including through their siting authority” and that, therefore, “which generating facilities are sited and allowed to serve Co-Located Load is also a question of state law.”14 Often, however, a generator will not serve Co-Located Load directly. Rather, the

generator will first sell electricity at wholesale to an entity, such as a retail supplier, that will then engage in the ultimate retail sale to the Co-Located Load. Such sales for resale are exclusively within the Commission’s jurisdiction, not the states’.15 State jurisdiction is limited to the “ultimate [retail] sale of the energy.”16 The Commission should therefore clarify that state authority does not extend to the wholesale sale of energy by a generator to a retail supplier, even if the retail supplier then resells that energy to a load co-located with the generator. Such jurisdiction lies exclusively with the Commission.

Second, in the December 18th Order, the Commission asserts that “states have exclusive jurisdiction over how to allocate the costs of all Commission-jurisdictional rates (including for energy, capacity, and transmission) among retail customers, including allocating costs among different classes of retail customers.”17 While states generally do have authority over the allocation of the costs of interstate wholesale sales among the wholesaler’s retail customers, that authority is not unlimited. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly stated, “even when States exercise their traditional authority over retail rates,” the “States may not seek to achieve ends, however legitimate, through regulatory means that intrude on FERC’s authority over interstate wholesale rates.”18

We therefore respectfully request that the Commission clarify that, consistent with
longstanding Supreme Court precedent,19 states do not have unlimited authority “over how to allocate the costs of all Commission-jurisdictional rates” and that they cannot use their authority over retail allocation to indirectly regulate the interconnection of generators serving Co-Located Load. For instance, a state cannot impose a requirement via the exercise of their jurisdiction over retail sales that attempts to contradict or prohibit a transaction or sale that the Commission has said is permitted. This is not a hypothetical concern. Some utilities have in fact asked their state commission for authority to condition retail service in the state on the load’s acceptance of a Transmission Security Agreement that calculates its security requirement based on a gross NITS rate despite the fact that this Commission has said that other rates must be made available.20 The Commission should make clear that a state may not frustrate the Commission’s policy in this manner, and, moreover, that any such Transmission Security Agreement would be unjust and unreasonable as a matter of federal law.

CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, the Commission should clarify the December 18th Order or, in the alternative, grant rehearing.
