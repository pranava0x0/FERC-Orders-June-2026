I. INTRODUCTION
The Office of the Ohio Consumers’ Counsel (“OCC”), on behalf of Ohio’s residential utility consumers, respectfully submits these comments regarding reforms to the governance of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or “Commission”) examination of PJM governance is both timely and necessary. Under the Federal Power Act, FERC is responsible for protecting consumers from rates, terms, and conditions of wholesale electric service that are unjust and unreasonable. Yet the institutional structure through which PJM develops the rules governing wholesale markets, resource adequacy, and transmission planning gives the consumers who ultimately pay those costs remarkably little leverage in the processes and decisions that produce them. That is problematic on many fronts.

That imbalance matters. PJM serves approximately 67 million consumers across 14 jurisdictions. But those consumers are represented in the PJM stakeholder process by only 14 statutory consumer advocate votes—one for each jurisdiction. At the Standing Committee and Senior Task Force level, those consumer advocates constitute approximately 1.3 percent of potential voters. Even at the Markets and Reliability Committee (“MRC”) and Members

PDF page 4

2
Committee (“MC”), where sector-weighted voting applies, consumer advocates account for only a small portion of the voting influence.

Meanwhile, entities with direct financial interests in PJM market rules and transmission decisions comprise much of PJM’s membership and exercise substantial influence over the development of those rules. The result is a fundamental structural problem: many of the entities voting on proposals governing wholesale electricity markets and transmission investment have a financial interest in the revenues those rules produce. At the same time, residential consumers who ultimately pay the resulting charges, have comparatively little ability to shape those proposals.

FERC should address that imbalance as a central component of PJM governance reform. But there is more to be done.

Governance reform should not merely redistribute authority among PJM’s existing institutional actors. It should make PJM more accountable to the public that it serves. That requires, at minimum, making affordability and the public interest an express component of PJM’s responsibilities, implementing consumer safeguards for transmission planning, strengthening the voices of statutory consumer advocates, improving transparency and cost oversight, and ensuring that any expansion of the PJM Board’s authority is accompanied by meaningful safeguards protecting consumers.

OCC’s responses to the Commission’s specific list of questions attached to the Notice Requesting Post-Conference Comment issued in this docket on July 30, 2026, are appended as Attachment A to these comments.

II. REFORMS TO PJM GOVERNANCE STRUCTURE
A. PJM governance must expressly recognize affordability and the public
interest.
PJM performs functions that profoundly affect the affordability and reliability of electricity service for over 67 million Americans. Its capacity market, energy markets, and transmission planning processes produce billions of dollars in costs that ultimately flow through to consumers’ electric bills. These electric transmission and supply charges comprise 60 to 70% of an Ohio household’s electricity bill, all pass-through charges from FERC and PJM.1 Yet affordability is not embedded in PJM’s governance structure. Unlike reliability – which PJM expressly calls its “primary responsibility,” – affordability is not treated as a comparable institutional obligation. PJM instead points to the states to address the retail impact of rising wholesale costs.2

Reliability is indispensable. But reliability cannot mean reliability at any cost. The Federal Power Act’s consumer-protection mandate requires rates to be “just and reasonable.” Energy markets and transmission planning should produce reliable service efficiently and at reasonable cost.

PJM’s governing documents should expressly require PJM and its Board to act in the public interest and to consider affordability alongside reliability. This should be a substantive obligation, not an aspirational statement. PJM should be required to evaluate the consumer cost consequences of material proposals and major Board decisions. A governance structure that measures reliability consequences but does not meaningfully measure and consider consumer

B. Independent cost oversight on transmission projects is needed.
Transmission deserves particular attention in the form of an Independent Transmission Monitor. Transmission charges can account for up to 20% of an Ohio household’s electricity bill.3

PJM consumers face substantial and growing transmission costs. From 2017 through 2025, Ohio transmission project costs have ballooned with over $16.4 billion in costs, with supplemental projects accounting for 66% of those projects, $10.8 billion. 4 These huge transmission bills to Ohio consumers emanate from massive transmission investment and the cost allocation methodologies used to collect that investment in regional and supplemental transmission projects.

Ohio consumers’ experience with supplemental transmission projects demonstrates the need for independent transmission oversight. Under PJM’s existing framework, supplemental projects are not subject to PJM review for need, prudence or cost-effectiveness. Yet the costs are recovered from consumers through FERC-jurisdictional transmission rates.

In Ohio, no one is filling that gap by reviewing these projects for prudence, need, or cost- effectiveness before consumers are required to pay for them. OCC’s 2023 complaint to FERC highlighted the magnitude of the problem: based on data through 2022 available at the time of that filing, Ohio utilities had added to local transmission infrastructures over approximately six

Consumers ultimately bear the consequences of those incentives through their electric bills.

An independent transmission monitor would help close this accountability gap by providing ongoing, independent scrutiny of the need, alternatives and costs associated with transmission investment – including Supplemental Projects. This would replace the current process where consumers must rely on after-the-fact challenges to formula transmission rates while billions of dollars in costs continue to be incurred. An independent transmission monitor should be structurally independent from PJM and transmission owners and should have sufficient access to information to conduct meaningful analysis.

Independent oversight would not replace PJM’s planning responsibilities, existing state oversight authority or the Commission’s jurisdiction. It would provide FERC, states, consumer advocates, and stakeholders with an independent source of information needed to determine whether billions of dollars in transmission investment are prudent, efficient, and beneficial to consumers.

C. Enhancing the PJM Attachment M-3 Supplemental Project process.
The growth in AI load interconnection requests is driving the need for improved local and Supplemental Project transmission planning processes. Although the Supplemental Projects are not directly governed by the requirements of FERC Order No. 1920, the Commission’s recent actions reflect an increasing emphasis on transparent planning processes, meaningful stakeholder participation, cost-effective analyses, and well-supported planning records. Those essential elements are lacking in the present PJM process.

In Order No. 1920, the Commission concluded that more forward-looking and
transparent transmission planning is necessary to ensure that transmission investments remain just and reasonable over the long term. While that rule principally addresses long-term regional transmission planning, the policy objectives it advances – including transparency, robust

Importantly, Order No. 8905 required similar transparency, robust planning assumptions and meaningful stakeholder engagement in the local transmission planning process. In PJM, Supplemental Projects are part of the local planning process and must be planned in a manner that encompasses these principles.

The PJM Attachment M-3 planning process for local transmission projects and Supplemental Projects embrace some, but not all these principles. OCC recognizes that FERC accepted the current PJM Attachment M-3 local transmission planning process in 2020 but notes that FERC has more recently questioned whether that process is sufficient in its April 2022 Supplemental Notice of Technical Conference in Docket No. AD22-8-000. That Notice states: The purpose of this conference is to explore measures to ensure

D. Transparency for PJM meetings.
Transparency in PJM meetings and decision-making is essential. PJM makes decisions that have enormous consequences for the over 67 million consumers who pay electric bills. Decisions affecting capacity and energy costs, transmission investment, market rules, and reliability should not be developed through closed-door discussions or processes that leave consumers and their representatives unable to understand who advocated for outcomes and why. Open meetings, accessible materials, meaningful opportunity for public and consumer participation, and a clear record of the basis for PJM’s decisions would promote accountability and public confidence. It would also help ensure that consumer affordability and broader public interest receive meaningful consideration alongside the interests of PJM’s market participants. The Board’s deliberations and decision-making should, whenever practicable, occur in meetings open to stakeholders and the public, with limited exceptions only where confidentiality is legally or commercially required.

III. STATE PARTICIPATION
State participation must be formalized- but state representation cannot substitute for consumer representation.

PJM governance reform should substantially strengthen the role of the states and the role of consumer advocates. FERC should recognize an important distinction: strengthening the “state voice” is not synonymous with strengthening the consumer voice.

State utility commissions, governors, legislators, environmental agencies, economic- development officials, and statutory consumer advocates perform different functions. State commissions may balance reliability, utility financial integrity, economic development,

PDF page 13

11
environmental requirements, and other statutory considerations. Governors and legislatures likewise balance numerous competing public-policy objectives. Statutory consumer advocates have a different responsibility: protecting utility consumers, including residential consumers, from excessive, unjust and unreasonable utility costs.

Those unique consumer interests often overlap with the states’ interests, but they are not identical. Consumer advocates may agree with their state commissions on one issue and disagree on another. PJM governance reform should preserve those independent perspectives rather than subsuming consumer representation within a generalized category of “state interests.” Accordingly, FERC should strengthen the role of both representative voices in the processes. OPSI should receive meaningful jump-ball filing rights where a sufficiently broad consensus of states supports an alternative to a PJM proposal. While the Commission has provided for such filing rights in the context of cost allocation proposals for long-term regional transmission projects in Order No. 1920, no similar rights exist for the Order No. 1000 and Order No. 890 regional and local transmission planning processes. PJM should also be required to consult formally with OPSI before any major section 205 filings, including significant resource- adequacy and transmission planning and cost allocation filings. At the same time, statutory consumer advocates should retain an independent and meaningful role throughout PJM governance, including in the stakeholder process and in the selection of PJM Board members.

IV. STAKEHOLDER PROCESS
A. Consumers need meaningful cost information before PJM makes decisions.
PJM cannot credibly claim that its processes produce cost-effective outcomes if neither decision-makers nor stakeholders are routinely provided with information about what the

PDF page 14

12
proposals will cost consumers. Cost analysis should become an ordinary part of PJM governance
– a part that is freely and timely shared with interested parties, including consumer advocates. For material proposals advancing to the MRC or MC, PJM should provide a reasonable assessment of expected cost impacts sufficiently early for stakeholders to use that information in evaluating competing proposals. The analysis need not – and often cannot – predict costs with precision. But uncertainty is not a justification for providing no analysis. PJM routinely analyzes complicated questions involving reliability, resource adequacy, market behavior, and system conditions under uncertain future assumptions. Consumer cost consequences deserve comparable institutional attention.

Cost information would improve not only consumer protection but decision-making itself. Stakeholders could evaluate whether incremental reliability benefits justify incremental costs, compare alternatives on a more informed basis, and identify proposals that accomplish the same objective at lower consumer cost.

FERC should require PJM to institutionalize meaningful and transparent cost-impact analysis. FERC also should require PJM and the transmission owners to provide answers to specific questions from stakeholders, including consumers advocates, about identified projects during the stakeholder process. Such questions include information regarding cost analyses, planning assumptions, alternatives considered and other cost impact data. There should be consequences for PJM’s and/or the transmission owners’ failure to respond to such specific questions about identified projects. In the absence of any realistic process for challenging the prudence, need for and cost-efficiency of local or Supplemental Projects in the formula transmission rate challenge process, consumers must have access to information regarding these projects during the transmission planning stage before construction begins.

B. Stakeholder voting should prevent affiliate voting from overwhelming
consumer interests.
PJM’s stakeholder process should facilitate genuine consensus rather than allow entities with numerous affiliates to dominate the early stages of policy development. Ohio illustrates how PJM’s affiliate-voting rules dilute the consumer voice at the committees where proposals are developed.

OCC, the statutory representative of 4.5 million residential households, receives one vote to represent Ohio’s residential utility consumers. Utilities participate through corporate families with numerous PJM affiliates. For example, Ohio Power Company is part of the AEP corporate family, which includes multiple transmission, generation, and energy affiliates. Ohio Edison is part of the FirstEnergy corporate family. Duke Energy Ohio participates alongside other Duke affiliates. At Standing Committees and Senior Task Forces, where affiliate voting is permitted, these corporate families can potentially exercise many multiple votes while OCC has one. That distinction is particularly important because the detailed work of designing PJM rules often occurs at Standing Committees and Senior Task Forces before proposals reach the Markets and Reliability Committee (MRC) or the Members Committee (MC). While the MRC and MC may be governed by weighted sector voting requirements, the same is not true of the other Standing Committees and Task Forces. This can permit supply-side or other commercial interests to advance proposals without meaningful support from those who represent the consumers responsible for paying the resulting costs. That is fundamentally unfair. By the time proposals reach sector-weighted voting at the MRC and MC, it may be too late for consumer interests to meaningfully shape their design. Those interests may instead be left primarily with the ability to vote no on a proposal developed without their meaningful participation.

PDF page 16

14
FERC should encourage PJM to address this structural problem by extending sector- weighted voting, or comparable protections against affiliate-vote dominance, to Standing Committees and Senior Task Forces. Doing so would not silence any sector but rather would levelize the playing field for all stakeholder interests. It would encourage those submitting proposals to attract support across sectors earlier in the development process, when compromise can meaningfully affect their substance.

C. PJM voting should be transparent.
PJM should also disclose how individual member companies vote throughout the stakeholder process. Entities participating in PJM are voting on matters that affect more than their private commercial interests. They participate in developing rules affecting the reliability and cost of electricity service for approximately 67 million consumers.

Aggregate voting results provide insufficient accountability and can impede consensus- building. When a proposal narrowly fails, stakeholders should be able to identify the concerns preventing agreement and determine whether modifications could produce broader support. Transparency would also eliminate inconsistent disclosure obligations. Some utilities may already be required under state law to disclose PJM votes, while others are not. In contrast, public consumer advocates operate under public-records requirements. PJM-wide disclosure would establish a uniform standard that could easily be followed. Individual member votes should be publicly available at all material levels of the stakeholder process.

V. PJM BOARD INDEPENDENCE

PJM Board reform should increase public accountability while preserving board independence.

Any expansion of Board authority should be accompanied by greater Board
accountability.

PDF page 17

15
First, the Board’s required expertise should include meaningful experience with state regulation and consumer interests. PJM should include Board members who understand how wholesale market and transmission decisions ultimately appear on consumers’ retail electric bills.

Second, the process for selecting Board members should include greater state and consumer participation. The Nominating Committee should include representation designated by states as well as a statutory consumer advocate. Consumers should not depend upon occasional rotation within the End-Use Customer Sector for representation in selecting the individuals who ultimately exercise enormous authority over costs paid by those consumers. Third, Board decision-making should be substantially more transparent. Board votes on significant matters should be disclosed, and the Board should provide meaningful explanations for consequential decisions. Appropriate portions of Board meetings should also be open or otherwise made available to stakeholders and the public.

Transparency should not, however, come at the expense of independence. FERC should not relax appropriate restrictions on private communication between individual Board members and interested market participants. Greater transparency in Board deliberations provides a better means of increasing accountability than expanding private access.

VI. SECTION 205 FILING RIGHTS
FERC should not expand the PJM Board’s Section 205 authority without corresponding consumer and state safeguards.

The Commission should proceed cautiously before expanding or limiting the PJM Board’s unilateral filing authority under section 205 of the Federal Power Act. The relevant question is not simply whether other RTO boards possess broader section 205 authority. The question is whether expanding or limiting authority to PJM’s Board, under PJM’s existing

PDF page 18

16
governance structure, would produce decision-making that better protects consumers and results in just and reasonable rates.

More importantly the Commission has already spoken on the concept of limiting the Board’s existing filing rights related to transmission planning. In Docket Nos. ER24-2336, ER24-2338 and EL24-119, the Commission rejected efforts by the Transmission Owners to provide themselves with extensive, unilateral veto rights with respect to the transmission planning process. In those cases, PJM and the Transmission Owners proposed to move the planning process rules from the PJM Operating Agreement to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff and the Consolidated Transmission Owners’ Agreement. FERC found that such action would provide the Transmission Owners with undue influence over the transmission planning process.8 The D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the Commission’s rulings just two months ago.9 There is no reason to revisit these very recent decisions.

At a minimum, PJM’s governing documents should first be revised to establish affordability and the public interest as express Board responsibilities. States and consumer advocates should also receive meaningful “jump ball” filing rights that allow a sufficiently broad consensus to present an alternative proposal to the Commission.

Providing FERC with competing proposals would not displace the Commission’s
ultimate responsibility to determine what is just and reasonable. Instead, it would ensure that the Commission hears a meaningful alternative from governmental entities responsible for protecting consumer interests.

VII.  CONCLUSION
The Commission should evaluate PJM governance reform from the perspective of the consumers whom the Federal Power Act ultimately protects. The fundamental problem is not simply whether authority resides with PJM’s Members Committee, its Board, or another institutional actor. The problem is whether PJM’s governance structure produces transparent, accountable, cost-conscious decisions consistent with just and reasonable rates. At the present time, PJM’s governance structure is not doing the job.

The reforms discussed in these OCC comments would not guarantee that every PJM decision produces the outcome preferred by consumers, states, market participants, or any other constituency. The reforms would, however, make it substantially more likely that decisions affecting tens of millions of consumers are made through a process that gives weight to affordability, provides meaningful public accountability, and supplies the Commission with the information and competing perspectives necessary to fulfill its responsibility under the Federal Power Act to protect consumers from unjust and unreasonable rates.

1. The Need for Standardized Reporting
OCC encourages PJM and transmission owners to provide standardized reporting identifying: (1) projected load attributable to native load, ordinary growth, and large-load customers; (2) whether customer load is supported by executed service agreements or other binding commitments; (3) customer development milestones; (4) anticipated in-service schedules; (5) sensitivity analyses for delayed or cancelled projects; and (6) whether facilities can be phased as customer commitments mature.

2. The Need for Transparency
Customer-driven supplemental transmission projects differ in important respects from traditional reliability-driven transmission projects. Historically, many supplemental projects were developed to address aging infrastructure, equipment replacement, operational flexibility, or identified reliability violations based on existing system conditions. By contrast, customer-driven projects are often initiated in anticipation of substantial future load growth associated with one or more specific customers seeking new or expanded electric service. As a result, these projects frequently depend upon assumptions regarding the timing, magnitude, and certainty of future customer demand rather than existing system deficiencies. That distinction makes transparency regarding planning assumptions, customer commitments, forecast certainty, and project timing particularly important. Without sufficient information regarding those underlying assumptions, stakeholders cannot meaningfully evaluate whether the proposed transmission investment is appropriately timed, appropriately sized, or reasonably necessary to serve the anticipated customer load. The projects presented at the July 7, 2026 TEAC meeting illustrate this distinction. Three of the four Ohio projects were driven primarily by anticipated customer load growth, while one addressed an operational limitation on the existing transmission system. OCC's comments reflect those differences by applying a common set of planning principles while recognizing that the information necessary to evaluate customer-driven projects is not identical to that required for traditional reliability or operational projects.

3. The Value of Phased in Construction in Customer-Driven Transmission Planning As customer-driven supplemental transmission projects continue to increase in size and complexity, transmission owners should, where practical, evaluate whether proposed facilities can be developed in phases that correspond with demonstrated customer demand. Phased implementation is a well- established planning approach that allows infrastructure to be expanded as customer development progresses while maintaining the flexibility necessary to provide reliable electric service. Considering phased implementation where feasible does not delay necessary infrastructure or discourage economic development. Rather, it reflects prudent utility planning by aligning the pace of infrastructure investment with the pace of demonstrated customer demand, thereby reducing the potential for existing consumers to bear the costs of facilities constructed significantly in advance of need, or that may be excess capacity if the AI loads fail to show up as projected.

IV. Consumer Protection Principles
As supplemental transmission planning increasingly responds to requests from individual large-load customers rather than traditional reliability needs, several principles should guide the evaluation of proposed transmission investments.

First, transmission projects should be supported by a clearly demonstrated need based on objective and transparent planning analyses. Stakeholders should have sufficient information to understand the assumptions underlying projected customer demand, the degree of certainty associated with those forecasts, and the planning criteria giving rise to the identified need.  Without this information, stakeholders cannot meaningfully evaluate whether proposed facilities are necessary or appropriately sized.

Second, transmission investments should reflect prudent utility planning. Proposed solutions should be supported by reasonable engineering analyses, consideration of feasible alternatives, and where appropriate, opportunities for phased implementation that correspond to demonstrated customer commitments.  Particularly for projects involving substantial capital investment, prudent planning requires balancing the need to provide timely service to new consumers with the responsibility to avoid premature, unnecessary or potentially abandoned infrastructure investments that may ultimately be borne by existing consumers.

Third, transmission investments should be developed and implemented in a manner consistent with longstanding regulatory principle of cost causation. Consumers should have confidence that investments undertaken primarily to accommodate new large-load customers are planned and funded in a manner that appropriately assigns costs to those AI customers, rather than shifting those costs to existing residential and small business consumers.

Finally, meaningful stakeholder participation is essential to maintaining confidence in the transmission planning process.  The M-3 process should provide an opportunity for stakeholders to review proposed transmission needs and solutions before significant investment decisions are finalized.  That opportunity is meaningful only if stakeholders are provided sufficient information to understand the basis of proposed projects and if substantive stakeholder comments are thoughtfully considered as projects advances through the planning process.

These principles are not intended to impede or delay needed transmission investment. Rather, they are intended to ensure that customer driven transmission projects are supported by transparent planning assumptions, prudent engineering, meaningful stakeholder review, and appropriate consumer protections. As transmission planning continues to evolve in response to unprecedented large-load growth, adherence to these principles will strengthen confidence in the M-3 process and Attachment B

Increased transparency and continued stakeholder engagement will strengthen confidence in the supplemental planning process and improve the quality of transmission planning decisions for the benefit of all stakeholders.
