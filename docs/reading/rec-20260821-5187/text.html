COMMENTS OF THE EDISON ELECTRIC INSTITUTE 
Edison Electric Institute (“EEI”) respectfully submits these comments regarding 
governance and stakeholder reforms in PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).1   
EEI’s member companies are the nation’s investor-owned electric utilities.  
Together, we provide electricity to nearly 250 million Americans, operate in all 50 states 
and the District of Columbia, and support over seven million jobs in communities across 
the country.  EEI member companies work every day to provide reliable energy to their 
customers at the lowest cost possible.  EEI member companies also form the backbone of 
our nation’s electric power industry, which is essential to advancing our economic and 
national security priorities.  Over the next five years, investor-owned electric companies 
will invest more than $1.3 trillion to enhance the electric grid, building critically needed 
new generation and transmission and distribution infrastructure required to power 
economic prosperity. 
EEI commends the Commission for undertaking a structured effort to reform PJM’s 
governance and stakeholder processes.  This initiative is timely and necessary, arriving at 
a genuine inflection point in PJM’s history.  The issues being confronted in this proceeding

market reforms.  EEI members stand ready to tackle these interrelated challenges.

II. 
GOVERNANCE AND STAKEHOLDER REFORMS ARE NECESSARY BUT 
INSUFFICIENT TO MEET THE RESOURCE ADEQUACY CHALLENGE 
While governance and stakeholder reform is essential, resolving these challenges 
will not, by itself, solve the larger and more critical resource adequacy problems facing the 
region.  The core problem confronting PJM in 2026 is underinvestment in generation.  And 
there is a real risk that conditions could deteriorate before they improve.  Accordingly, 
governance reform is a necessary first step.  But governance reform must be in service of 
facilitating genuine reforms to address the underlying supply shortfall. 
A. 
The resource adequacy challenge threatens PJM’s fundamental charge 
to serve customers reliably 
PJM’s “core responsibility” is maintaining reliability.11  And PJM has long 
“operated on the principle that all customers share in a common standard of reliability and 
willingness to pay to meet it.”12  But current resource adequacy conditions are undermining 
PJM’s ability to meet these standards.  The problem is documented in PJM’s own 
“Powering Reliability Through Market Design” whitepaper and in comments filed in this 
docket.13  PJM explains that the current shortage is structural rather than cyclical—not a 
temporary supply gap that will close on its own, but the predictable result of several

The timing gap between when demand arrives and when supply responds lies at the very 
core of the shortage.  Compounding the problem, PJM observes that building new 
generation now costs roughly twice as much and takes twice as long as before.15  Capital 
costs for a new combined-cycle plant have roughly doubled and those for a combustion 
turbine have increased by more than fifty percent, even as supply chain backlogs, 
permitting timelines, and equipment scarcity show every sign of persisting.16  In short, the 
barriers to entry are far higher today than when the market was designed. 
The consequences of the resource adequacy gap are visible in the region’s core 
reliability metrics.  For example, the 2027/28 Base Residual Auction failed to procure 
sufficient capacity to satisfy the required reserve margin, clearing at an Installed Reserve 
Margin of 14.4 percent—5.6 percentage points below PJM’s 20 percent reliability target—
and leaving a physical deficit of more than 6,000 MW.17  That shortfall places direct 
pressure on the region's long-standing “1-in-10” loss-of-load standard, which, absent 
corrective action, risks eroding into a far weaker level of service for every customer.18

B. 
New supply in PJM has been slow to come online despite record-high 
capacity auction prices 
The existing PJM resource adequacy construct cannot address the current resource 
adequacy shortfall.  New supply has been slow to come online despite record-high capacity 
auction prices.  Recent Base Residual Auction results demonstrate the shortfall in new 
capacity.  The 2027/28 auction procured only 774 MW of new generation and uprates, 
consisting of 350.7 MW UCAP from new generation and 423.6 MW UCAP from uprates.19  
The 2028/29 auction procured even less—only 524.7 MW UCAP, consisting of 317.1 MW 
UCAP from new generation and 207.6 MW UCAP from uprates.20  In both instances, the 
auctions failed to procure sufficient capacity to meet the required reserve margin.  
Customers are paying drastically more for (largely) existing generation capacity.  
This shortfall persisted even though prices reached maximum levels21 in both 
auctions, meaning that customers are paying record prices—and incumbent generation
