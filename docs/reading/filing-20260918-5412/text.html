II. 
INTRODUCTION 
DCC appreciates MISO’s continued proactive engagement with the data center industry, 
and its efforts to develop reliability requirements that facilitate the integration of large loads while 
preserving the reliable operation of the transmission system.  DCC member facilities depend upon 
reliable electricity service, and DCC shares MISO’s objective of ensuring that the interconnection 
and operation of large load customers does not adversely affect reliability, system operations, or 
other customers.  DCC recognizes that large loads may present operational characteristics that 
warrant distinct reliability requirements and standards to maintain transmission system reliability.  
DCC is committed to working collaboratively with MISO, the North American Electric Reliability 
Corporation (“NERC”), and stakeholders to establish requirements that maintain reliability, 
protect customers, and avoid creating barriers to large load growth.  
The issue presented by MISO’s Filing is not whether transmission customers taking service 
for large loads should be subject to distinct visibility and operational requirements.  The 
Commission already resolved this issue in its June 2026 show cause order finding the MISO tariff 
unjust and unreasonable because it does not include provisions establishing ride-through, ramping, 
and other operational requirements to address the challenges of large load integration.5  Instead, 
the issue is whether the framework that MISO has proposed in this proceeding represents a just 
and reasonable means of mitigating these reliability risks.  While DCC appreciates MISO’s efforts 
to develop a compliance framework and recognizes the difficulty of the task, DCC respectfully

III. 
COMMENTS 
A. 
MISO’s Filing Is Inconsistent With The Show Cause Order, The FPA, And 
The Rule Of Reason    
1. 
MISO’s Proposal To Relegate The Substance Of Its Requirements To 
Its BPMs Is Inconsistent With The FPA 
Section 205 of the FPA requires public utilities to file the rates, terms, and conditions of 
FERC-jurisdictional service with the Commission.10  This obligation includes “classifications, 
practices, and regulations affecting such rates and charges[.]”11  Additionally, “no change shall be 
made” in any rate, term, condition, or practice without a Section 205 filing providing notice of the 
change to the Commission and the public, and only after the Commission determines that the 
change is just and reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential.12  These basic 
statutory requirements are reflected in the Filed Rate Doctrine, which prohibits public utilities from 
imposing any rates, terms, and conditions other than those that have been filed with and approved 
by the Commission.13   
Section 205 and the Filed Rate Doctrine are designed to ensure that customers receive 
advance notice of the rates, terms, and conditions of service and the Commission can exercise its 
authority necessary to maintain just and reasonable rates.14  As the courts have recognized, the

But the FPA and Filed Rate Doctrine are not satisfied merely because a public utility has 
filed a tariff with FERC.  The FPA, FERC’s regulations, and the Filed Rate Doctrine require that 
the tariff “clearly and specifically” set forth the rates, terms, and conditions, and practices affecting 
such rates, terms, and conditions, of FERC-jurisdictional service.16  A tariff that omits key terms 
and conditions of service in favor of vague or open-ended references to a public utility’s 
procedures and practices is inconsistent with the FPA and the Filed Rate Doctrine, and ultimately 
unjust and unreasonable.17   
MISO’s proposed tariff revisions do not satisfy these requirements.  While DCC recognizes 
that not every ministerial implementation detail must be included in the tariff, MISO’s tariff 
revisions do not include the substantive standards that MISO proposes to apply, leave key aspects 
of MISO’s proposed framework unaddressed, and grant MISO broad discretion to apply the 
standards to customers as it deems appropriate without any objective requirements to constrain 
this discretion.  Instead, the tariff repeatedly defers the substance of these standards and key aspects 
of how these standards will be applied to a new Large Load BPM that remains under development 
by MISO, including the performance standards and limits that customers will be held to,18 testing 
requirements and other procedures for demonstrating compliance with MISO’s standards, and the 
timelines for providing required information to MISO.19  The result is a tariff framework that

The Commission’s Rule of Reason does not justify MISO’s decision to leave the substance 
of its proposed requirements to the BPMs.  Instead, the Commission’s Rule of Reason requires 
public utilities to file those practices or provisions that “significantly affect rates and services”20 
while permitting only implementation details of “practical insignificance”21 to be included in 
BPMs or other manuals.  The Rule of Reason requires terms and conditions to be included in the 
tariff if they “affect rates and services significantly, . . . are realistically susceptible of specification, 
. . . are not so generally understood . . . as to render recitation superfluous,”22 and are not “clearly 
implied by the tariff’s express terms.”23  Rather than supporting MISO’s proposal, applying the 
Rule of Reason to the tariff provisions that MISO has filed in this proceeding highlights the 
problems with MISO’s approach.

v. 
MISO’s Approach Undermines Certainty, Predictability, And 
Commission Oversight 
The practical effect of the tariff revisions that have been proposed is that neither the 
Commission nor commenters have the information necessary to determine whether the substance 
of MISO’s proposed framework is just, reasonable, and not unduly discriminatory or preferential.  
This places the Commission and commenters in the impossible position of being asked to evaluate 
a reliability framework without any insight into the substance of the standards or other key terms 
and conditions of service. Without the operative standards and procedures before it, the 
Commission cannot determine whether the proposed requirements are supported by demonstrated 
reliability needs, reasonably tailored to the risks MISO seeks to address, capable of being applied 
consistently to similarly situated customers, or will achieve the objective of minimizing disruption 
to existing commercial arrangements.  
MISO’s proposed framework undermines the certainty that the Show Cause Order sought 
to promote.  The proposed tariff language does not provide customers with notice of the 
requirements that will apply, how compliance will be assessed, or even whether their facilities 
ultimately will be deemed subject to these requirements.  Large Load customers need to understand 
the scope of their obligations when making investment decisions and entering commercial 
arrangements.  And the tariff must provide customers with assurance that exemptions, once

granted, cannot be summarily revoked based on a subjective MISO assessment without regard to 
the stage of development of their facilities.  
 
MISO’s proposal also creates a risk of inconsistent standards and application.  
Transmission customers serving Large Loads or Computational Loads should be subject to a 
common set of standards and requirements, as applicable.  Yet, since the details of these 
requirements are not provided in the tariff, neither the Commission nor customers have any 
assurance that the requirements that ultimately are adopted will be applied in a manner that ensures 
consistent and non-discriminatory treatment of customers.  
Finally, the proposed framework would allow MISO to create through future BPM 
revisions the very disruption to settled expectations that the Show Cause Order directed MISO to 
avoid.  Placing the substance of MISO’s reliability requirements in the BPMs means that the

formal notice to customers or approval by the Commission.  As the Commission recognized in the 
Show Cause Order, the application of new reliability requirements to customers has the potential 
to disrupt the settled expectations of customers that have relied on existing market rules when 
developing their facilities.44  It was precisely for this reason that the Commission directed MISO 
to propose measures to minimize disruption to existing commercial arrangements.45  Yet, MISO’s 
proposal would allow it to revise these same requirements through the BPMs after customers have 
designed their facilities and entered commercial arrangements without making a Section 205 filing 
or seeking Commission approval—and without any obligation to take steps to protect settled 
expectations.

B. 
MISO Has Not Demonstrated That Its Proposed Definition Of Large Load Is 
Just, Reasonable, And Not Unduly Discriminatory Or Preferential 
MISO’s proposed definition of Large Load also creates ambiguity regarding the scope and 
application of MISO’s proposed requirements.  Because classification as a “Large Load” carries 
substantial regulatory and commercial consequences, it is critical that any definition of Large Load 
provide clear notice to transmission customers and the loads they serve of whether they will be

risks that the Commission sought to address in the Show Cause Order.  MISO’s proposed 
definition of Large Load does not meet these standards.

DCC appreciates MISO’s effort to recognize the potential impact that its proposed 
reliability requirements could have on existing large load customers that already have entered 
commercial arrangements based on existing market rules.  In practice, requiring customers that 
have already commenced the development of their facilities to comply with new ride-through,

customers to redesign their projects, modify protection and control systems, replace equipment, 
and implement other project-specific engineering modifications.  As a result, it is critically 
important that any new reliability requirements that are adopted include measures to respect the 
settled expectations of customers, including appropriate exemptions and transition periods for 
customers that are required to comply with these requirements.  
DCC believes, however, that MISO has not demonstrated that its proposal is appropriately 
tailored to avoid disruption to existing commercial arrangements that the Commission sought to 
address in the Show Cause Order.  MISO’s proposed grandfathering framework turns entirely on 
the status of an associated transmission project in the MTEP or EPR process rather than on the 
commitments or progress made by Large Load customers.  In practice, whether a transmission 
project satisfies the MTEP/EPR Condition may reflect little more than the timing or nature of a 
transmission upgrade rather than the maturity of the underlying project.  Indeed, a customer may 
execute utility service agreements, secure financing, procure major equipment, commit substantial 
capital, and make irreversible business decisions prior to when an associated transmission project 
reaches the applicable MTEP or EPR milestone.
