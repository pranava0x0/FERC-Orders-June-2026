I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The issue of co-location of large load and generation represents an emerging and strategically important development in the evolution of the electric grid. Data centers that hold important, sensitive data and support growing artificial intelligence capabilities run applications that need large amounts of power to fuel the economy and provide national security. Advanced manufacturing facilities that support economic development and onshoring of critical supply chains will also require access to reliable, affordable power to compete in a global economy. Co- location arrangements offer the potential for increased reliability and faster interconnection to the grid, but their rapid emergence and different configurations are raising concerns about the potential for compromised resource adequacy, reliability, and resiliency. Commission direction is critical to bring clarity to this pressing issue, and the Commission has placed appropriate attention and urgency on co-location through the Show Cause Order and the Technical Conference convened last November.4 United agrees with PJM that the industry needs guidance, and urges the Commission to act quickly to reduce regulatory uncertainty and allow large loads and generation to interconnect to the grid in a fast and efficient manner while avoiding cost shifts to other customers and preserving or even bolstering reliability.

The Commission should direct PJM to take further action in its evaluation of its Tariffs, which include the Open Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT”), Operating Agreement (“OA”), and Reliability Assurance Agreement (“RA”). III. PERSPECTIVES ON CO-LOCATION ISSUES
A. The Commission Should Act Within its Jurisdiction to Facilitate Fair Co-
Location Options While Preserving State Jurisdiction Over Retail Issues
As FERC recognized in its Show Cause Order, there is a shared responsibility between states and the Commission with respect to service to co-located load and generation. While FERC, PJM, or another RTO/ISO cannot infringe on a states’ ability to regulate retail sales of energy and distribution services to retail load, the Federal Power Act does grant FERC jurisdiction over transmission service and wholesale energy sales in interstate commerce.21 This jurisdiction gives FERC and PJM an important role in the co-location of large loads and generation.

States, along with RTOs/ISOs, are grappling with the issues of increasing demand and retirement of existing generation without new resources replacing them fast enough. Solutions to these issues must be weighed against state policies and goals pertaining to economic development, generation mix, energy affordability, and other priorities. Some states have opened proceedings, developed special tariffs, or are taking other steps to address issues related to the rapid rise in large load additions.22 These developments at the state level are emergent, and are subject to change at the discretion of the relevant state regulatory authorities. Given the evolving nature of this issue and the fact that each state has a different regulatory structure and faces different issues and circumstances, there must be flexibility in any tariff changes put forward by PJM to accommodate states’ different and evolving regulatory frameworks with respect to large loads. B. PJM Tariffs Regarding Co-Location Agreements Do Not Provide Sufficient
Clarity and Consistency
PJM states that its tariffs are just and reasonable26 while also acknowledging that clarifications and enhancements could be made to accommodate additional co-location configurations.27 PJM also recognizes that only three of the co-location eight scenarios it describes in its Answer are available under the current tariffs.28 PJM’s tariffs must be both clear and flexible to allow for a variety of fair and effective co-location solutions to emerge and evolve. Large loads looking to interconnect to PJM must have regulatory certainty that the proverbial rug will not be pulled out from under them after investing in a co-location arrangement. Clear tariff provisions are the basis for such regulatory certainty and will invite innovative solutions that bring about economic development while supporting reliability and fair and affordable rates for all customers.

As discussed further below, each of the eight co-location scenarios described in PJM’s answer may prove viable for certain customers, depending on their capabilities and needs, and all eight should be accommodated. PJM and the Commission must also keep an open mind to

1. All Eight Co-location Options Should be Accommodated, but
Require Additional Development
PJM lays out eight potential co-location options, of which three are currently accounted for in its tariffs and five are not. It is important to recognize and preserve the three existing configurations, but these scenarios fail to address the new kinds of co-location arrangements that large loads and generators are interested in pursuing (indeed, PJM freely admits that Option 1 is not an example of co-location at all). The five new arrangements may all be of interest to certain customers depending on their specific needs and capabilities, but they lack detail. Further development of these options, consideration of variants, and evaluation of additional options through an expedient but iterative stakeholder process is necessary to provide regulatory certainty and proactively address the variety of co-location arrangements that will likely come before PJM and the Commission. PJM and the Commission should also clarify that the options are not mutually exclusive, and that combinations or variations of the eight listed options may be allowable.

Option 1 describes an arrangement in which the load and the generator are treated entirely separately—they may share a contractual arrangement and a physical location, but do

Given the amount of innovation in new BTMG technologies, this option has potential to enhance grid reliability while lowering costs for end use customers, but it does not address demand for co-location opportunities for new large loads. The three existing co-location options allowed under PJM’s current tariff therefore fail to anticipate the different co-location arrangements that are likely to be pursued in the PJM region. Without an expedited process, customers seeking to bring new generation to serve their load under the BYOG option may be waiting several years to connect to the grid, defeating the purpose of this option. Further, keeping these projects waiting could cause cost allocation issues as well as reliability and resource adequacy concerns. There are also efficiencies, from both a study and a network upgrade perspective, from taking into account the intent for the new generation to serve new load when studying its impacts on the system. These efficiencies will save money and time and reduce the need for grid components that are subject to supply chain constraints. These efficiencies also differentiate projects interconnecting under a BYOG option such that a streamlined process to interconnect new generation under a BYOG arrangement is not unduly discriminatory.

An expedited interconnection process to facilitate new resources brought online via the BYOG option must be fair, transparent, and not unduly discriminatory. The expedited process must not harm projects in the standard interconnection queue, and must grant equal, nondiscriminatory opportunities for independently developed projects to participate. For new generation that is physically co-located with load and not exporting to the gird, the study itself could be similar to the recently reformed surplus interconnection service process in PJM.53 The new generation would be studied according to the intent to only serve the new load, thus avoiding the need for new network upgrades.

The Commission’s upmost concern is and should remain to be the reliability of the grid as co-located loads come online. In its response to the Commission, PJM noted that “[a]s a baseline, PJM does not believe any changes to capacity market rules are necessary to accommodate co-location arrangements.”54 United agrees that if co-location is pursued according to carefully developed, transparent, and flexible tariff provisions that account for the impact of different co-location arrangements on the grid, co-location should have “the same implications for resource adequacy as load that is not co-located.”55

However, given the pace, scale, and uncertainty of new load additions, some changes to PJM’s forecasting and planning processes are warranted. For example, the impact of large loads on the transmission system should inform changes to the planning process. For example, PJM’s Option 7 acknowledges that some loads do not necessarily need to be studied and planned for firm 24/7 continuous power, which will likely trigger transmission expansions in addition to generation additions. Further, improving the transparency and responsiveness of the load forecasting process will promote resource adequacy and reliability. F. Other Considerations
Much of the discussion regarding co-located loads is focused on the challenges and the need to avoid adverse impacts, but co-location also has the potential to bring various benefits to the grid. Many co-located loads can provide demand response and other services to benefit the transmission system and should be compensated for those services, as contemplated by PJM’s Option 8. Any load that brings its own backup (as many data centers do) in addition to co-located generation brings less risk to the system. Further, loads that bring with them new generating resources via the BYOG option are effectively zeroing out their impact on the system, helping to resolve some the resource adequacy concerns and reducing necessary network upgrades. In addition to backup generation, behind the meter generation should be utilized, and compensated, to help resource adequacy and reliability.

Ultimately, not all co-location arrangements are equal and should thus not be treated as such. Any tariff changes PJM must make, while increasing standardization of the process, must
