COMMENTS OF THE PUBLIC UTILITIES COMMISSION OF OHIO’S
OFFICE OF THE FEDERAL ENERGY ADVOCATE

The Office of the Federal Energy Advocate (Ohio FEA) of the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio, fulfilling its statutory duty to advocate on behalf of Ohio’s retail electricity consumers,1 submits the following in response to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC or Commission) February 20, 2025 Order instituting a show cause proceeding pursuant to section 206 of the Federal Power Act (FPA) regarding large loads co-located at generating facilities in the PJM Interconnection, LLC (PJM) region.

I. Background
The substantial grid challenges PJM is working to address are well known and a result of numerous simultaneous issues, including a changing generation resource mix, delayed capacity auctions, lengthy interconnection queue reform, and unprecedented forecasted load growth driven primarily by data centers. Given the current landscape of tight supply and increasing demand, interest in the topic of co-locating large loads with generating facilities in PJM has

escalated over the last two years. On November 1, 2024, the Commission convened a technical conference in Docket No. AD24-11-000 related to large loads co-located at generating facilities, followed by a request for post-technical conference comments.2 Separately, on November 22, 2024, Constellation Energy Generation, LLC (Constellation) filed an FPA section 206 complaint against PJM in Docket No. EL25-20-000 arguing that PJM’s Tariff is unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory because it does not contain rules for interconnected generators to follow when they seek to execute certain configurations and arrangements to serve co-located load.3 In the instant proceedings, the Commission consolidated the show cause proceeding with the technical conference and Constellation complaint proceeding for administrative efficiency and directed PJM and the Transmission Owners to respond to enumerated questions and concerns regarding co-location arrangements.

The Ohio FEA offers the following comments for FERC’s consideration.
II. Comments
A. Multiple Pathways Are Needed to Address Resource Adequacy Needs.
The PJM region faces the unprecedented challenge of simultaneously managing significant load growth, premature retirement of dispatchable thermal generation, and slow entry of new resource additions. Significant incremental capacity is needed, and the Ohio FEA appreciates PJM’s effort in working with stakeholders, state regulators, and the Commission to effectuate market, planning, and operations reforms to ensure that resource adequacy and grid reliability are maintained.

Given PJM’s existing market conditions and speed to market concerns, it is not surprising that various permutations of co-locating load with generation resources have recently come to the forefront. The Ohio FEA agrees that an “all hands on deck” approach is needed to meet supply and demand challenges in the region and appreciates PJM’s commitment “to facilitating and supporting co-location arrangements where permitted by applicable state law.”4 However, PJM must develop transparent processes to assess the grid impacts of co-location arrangements and to ensure that individual arrangements do not threaten grid reliability or worsen the resource adequacy concerns that exist across the region.

In its answer, PJM lays out eight potential options for large load co-location configurations, including the existing standard process for integrating large loads into the grid (regardless of whether co-located generation shares the same point of interconnection) and the traditional behind-the-meter configuration where generation that serves a primary customer is netted against that customer’s use of the electric grid. PJM goes on to describe five conceptual options that are not in its current Tariff but that might be of interest to states or entities contemplating alternative co-location arrangements and seeking clarity from PJM or the Commission.

Through its discussion of the range of options, PJM reiterates its recommendation and preference that large loads be designated as Network Load as opposed to configurations that propose to install off-system load behind an existing generator.5 “Network Load status offers the most robust reliability benefits and holistic planning efficiencies, minimizes the need for one-off

operational procedures (both interim and longer term), and offers a cost allocation framework that assesses charges for use of and reliance on the transmission system.”6

The Ohio FEA shares concern that installing large off-system loads behind existing generators adds complexity, including that the load would not be captured in the annual load forecasting and planning processes conducted by Load Serving Entities and PJM. Additionally, the Ohio FEA notes an important difference between new loads that are paired with new generation and co-location arrangements that propose to take existing generation off the system. The latter has broader implications because it would change the power flow across the transmission system and affect the mix of resources available to participate in PJM’s energy and capacity markets. Because of the wider range of impact, co-location arrangements that take existing generation off the system should be subject to robust analysis before they are allowed to proceed, including studying the impacts on the markets, grid reliability, and resource adequacy.

B. Bring Your Own Generation (BYOG) Should Be Encouraged.
In encouraging load additions to be Network Load, PJM acknowledges that any
additional load on the system requires either the addition of supply resources to serve that load or flexibility on the part of the load to curtail its consumption from the transmission system when necessary to maintain reliability.7 Three of the five conceptual options PJM outlines in its Answer could serve to bring new Network Load on faster without exacerbating resource adequacy concerns. The Ohio FEA supports further consideration of these options. In particular, co-located load that elects to be Network Load and brings its own generation, or BYOG, could augment the steps PJM is already taking, such as through the

Reliability Resource Initiative, to ensure that needed generation resources can be timely integrated onto the transmission system to serve significant future load growth. BYOG may provide a pathway in some states for new generation resources to support co-located load and also serve as PJM Capacity Resources. Under this option, various incentives could be explored, including faster processing of generation that enters the PJM interconnection queue to accommodate the co-located load. Importantly, under BYOG, the new Network Load would bring generation resources that could also serve as Capacity Resources, mitigating both local grid impacts at the point of interconnection and regional resource adequacy concerns.

C. Co-Located Load Must Pay for Grid Services Used.
The Ohio FEA agrees with PJM that “participants involved in co-location arrangements should pay the costs of any grid services they consume and the arrangements must be reliable and operationally manageable.”8 Regardless of the co-located load configurations that may be approvable by the Commission and under state laws, PJM must establish transparent processes to determine the extent to which individual co-located load relies on the bulk power system (for Ancillary Services or otherwise) and a means by which to allocate those transmission costs accordingly so that co-located load arrangements do not shift costs to other customers.

III. Conclusion
The Ohio FEA appreciates the Commission’s thorough investigation into issues surrounding co-located loads to assist PJM and state regulators in meeting the resource adequacy needs of the region without compromising reliability.
