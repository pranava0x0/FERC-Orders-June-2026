LIMITED COMMENTS OF BUCKEYE POWER, INC. 
 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s 
(“Commission”) Rules of Practice and Procedure,1 Buckeye Power, Inc. (“Buckeye”)2 
respectfully submits these limited comments on PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) 
proposed revisions to its Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) and the Reliability 
Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities (“RAA”) to establish the Interim 
Resource Adequacy Service (“IRAS”).3 In order to eliminate a potential unjust and 
unreasonable result, Buckeye seeks clarification as to how PJM intends to measure the 
MW quantity it directs Electric Distributors to reduce in an IRAS Action.  
Specifically, Buckeye seeks to avoid the possibility of PJM directing an Electric 
Distributor to drop a specified amount of MW based on registered peak loads of New 
Large Loads that exceeds the amount of actual New Large Load demand in real time, 
which would require the Electric Distributor to drop existing customer load. Such an 
outcome would be contrary to PJM’s intent to address the reliability challenges caused by 
New Large Loads unsupported by new capacity, and it would not be just and reasonable. 
PJM should therefore identify what in its proposed Tariff language or forthcoming PJM

COMMENTS 
I. 
Introduction 
PJM is proposing IRAS as an operational tool to address the reality that New 
Large Loads are developing more quickly than generation and transmission can be built, 
making it difficult for PJM to maintain a reliable electric grid while allowing these New 
Large Loads to interconnect at their desired pace.4 The IRAS framework is intended to 
allow PJM to identify the amount in MW of New Large Loads that exceeds capacity and 
order load reduction based on that amount during potential grid emergencies. This 
approach is intended to protect existing customers, including residential customers, from 
bearing the reliability impacts caused by New Large Loads, particularly to the extent 
New Large Loads are not supported by Bring Your Own New Capacity (“BYONC”) or a 
share of Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”).  
As detailed in the proposed Tariff language, PJM may initiate an IRAS Action, 
“prior to calling on Pre-Emergency Load Management Reductions Actions after 
determining that available economic real-time supply may not be sufficient to maintain 
reserves to meet applicable Reserve Requirements and/or Interconnection Reliability

Additional clarification is needed, however, as to how IRAS Actions will be implemented 
in real-time to ensure that the mechanism does not unintentionally require that existing 
customers, including residential loads, be unnecessarily curtailed.  
As detailed below, PJM should clarify how the MW quantity that PJM directs 
Electric Distributors to drop in an IRAS Action will be determined in a manner that 
ensures that Electric Distributors are not required to reduce more load in real-time than is 
being operated by New Large Loads at that time. Buckeye is concerned that directing the 
Electric Distributor to reduce a specific amount of MW quantity based on registered peak 
load of New Large Loads determined for the Delivery Year will not match the actual 
amount of load being used by the New Large Loads in real-time. If PJM were allowed to 
direct IRAS quantities in real-time that exceed the real-time demand of New Large 
Loads, this mismatch between the peak load reported by the New Large Load in the 
registry and the amount of load the Large Load is using in real-time could require the 
Electric Distributor to reduce existing customers, including residential loads, to make up 
the difference. 
For example, assume the Large Load Registry for an Electric Distributor 
zone/area shows 3,000 MW of peak load attributable to three New Large Loads for the 
applicable delivery year with 1,000 MW of BYONC. That would result in 2,000 MW of 
Eligible New Large Load Amounts subject to IRAS. PJM issues a real-time IRAS Action 
that requires the Electric Distributor to reduce 2,000 MW of load based on the Electric 
Distributor’s maximum Eligible New Large Load Amount for that Delivery Year.

However, in real-time, the demand of the three New Large Loads is only 1,500 MW 
(perhaps because one reduced usage in response to earlier alerts, or because one or more 
of the New Large Loads are not yet fully operational). 
Importantly, because the real-time load of the New Large Loads is just 
1,500 MW, the Electric Distributor would have no ability to meet a 2,000 MW reduction 
obligation from just the New Large Loads—even though those New Large Loads are the 
sole reason the IRAS obligation exists. As a result, in this scenario, the Electric 
Distributor would be forced to reduce 500 MW of load for existing customers, which 
may impact residential loads and other non-New Large Load end-users. Moreover, that 
additional 500 MW of curtailment from non-large load customers would be unnecessary, 
as an IRAS quantity equal to the New Large Load’s real-time load should be sufficient to 
address the reliability impacts of New Large Loads unsupported by new capacity. 
This outcome is clearly contrary to PJM’s intent, which is to base the IRAS load 
reduction obligation specifically on the extent to which New Large Loads have failed to 
cover their capacity obligations with new generation. Nothing in PJM’s desire to provide 
flexibility to states and Relevant Electric Retail Regulatory Authorities (“RERRAs”) 
suggests an intent that Electric Distributors would be forced to reduce existing customers’ 
load as a result of New Large Loads. Indeed, PJM’s emphasis that load curtailment is the 
last resort, with IRAS Actions coming well before that, confirms that existing customers 
are not intended to be forced to bear the burden of IRAS reductions. 
Buckeye asks that PJM clarify how the MW amounts Electric Distributors will be 
asked to curtail will be determined in a real-time IRAS Action, and whether this 
determination will consider the actual demand of the New Large Loads in real-time to

avoid requiring impacts to existing customer load described above. Specifically, PJM 
should ensure that the amount of MWs the Electric Distributor is required to drop in 
response to an IRAS Action will not exceed the amount of MWs being used by the New 
Large Loads in real-time.14 This clarification will still allow the Electric Distributors, 
working with its applicable state or RERRA, to determine how the MW drop will be 
assigned to individual loads, while at the same time ensuring that Electric Distributors are 
not put in the position of being forced to drop existing load (the action of last resort to 
maintain grid integrity) to comply with an IRAS Action. 
To the extent that the proposed Tariff language must be revised to make this 
clarification, PJM should do so. To the extent PJM does not voluntarily do so, the 
Commission should direct it to make these changes in order to protect existing customers 
from reliability impacts caused by New Large Loads and to ensure just, reasonable, and 
not unduly discriminatory rates. At minimum, if PJM does not clarify this issue 
consistent with Buckeye’s limited comments herein, the Commission should issue a 
deficiency letter directing PJM to explain how the existing proposal is just, reasonable, 
and not unduly discriminatory.  
PJM has already consented to make modifications “consistent with PJM’s 
overarching objectives in this filing,”15 which the clarification described above would be.

CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons stated above, the Commission should (to the extent PJM does 
not do so on its own) require PJM to clarify how it will calculate the IRAS Action 
reduction quantity in real-time, including whether that amount could exceed New Large 
Load in real-time such that an Electric Distributor is forced to curtail other, existing 
customers. To the extent Tariff revisions are required to prevent this problem, the 
Commission should direct PJM to make those further revisions to the extent it does not 
do so voluntarily.
