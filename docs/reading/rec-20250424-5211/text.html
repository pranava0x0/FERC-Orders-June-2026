INTRODUCTION
Pursuant to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“Commission”) February 20, 2025 Order (“Order”), the Center for Biological Diversity (“Center”) submits the following response to the parties’ March 24, 2025 filings. Feb. 20, 2025 Order at 57. The Center appreciates the opportunity to comment on the Commission’s Order Instituting Proceeding concerning co-location of large loads at generating facilities. The Center is a national, non-profit conservation organization with more than 1.8 million members and online activists who care about the country’s urgent need to expedite the renewable energy transition and protect human health, the natural environment, and species from the ravages of the climate emergency, extinction crisis, and environmental degradation.

As discussed below, in addressing the energy needs of data centers and other large load facilities that are rapidly proliferating across the country, it is vital that the Commission address both the challenges and opportunities these facilities provide. Regarding challenges, to fulfill its statutory obligations—as detailed in the Addendum below—it is critical that the Commission take into account the environmental implications of these facilities, including both the climate change threats they pose and their other environmental impacts. As for opportunities, the Commission should consider how these facilities can help address grid and load needs through

demand response and load flexibility, and how to maximize reliance on renewable energy to address the facilities’ energy demand needs.

In addition, the Center also offers comments below more specifically addressing the issues raised in the Commission’s Order and initial comments. First, our comments detail steps the Commission should take to ensure that any tariff or other measures protect consumers from being forced to subsidize data centers and related facility and infrastructure costs that should be borne by private corporations benefiting from these expenditures. Second, we address the issue of ensuring these facilities do not escape regulation by being mischaracterized as either behind- the-meter or offline. At bottom, the Commission must ensure that these facilities—and the load growth the facilities are bringing to the Nation’s energy system—is both fair to consumers and consistent with FERC’s statutory mandates.

DISCUSSION
I. Threshold Issues the Commission Should Consider in Addressing the Matters Raised in This Proceeding.

A. The Commission Should Consider the Environmental Implications of Any Tariff That Encourages the Growth of Data Centers and Other Large Load Facilities.

As reflected in the comments already in the record, the growth of data centers for Artificial Intelligence (“AI”) and other purposes, as well as of cryptomining facilities, are key drivers of the effort towards co-location arrangements. Before turning to the Commission’s specific questions, therefore, it is vital to address the risks data centers and cryptomining facilities pose to the climate and the environment.1

1. Data Centers and Cryptomining Threaten the Climate.

Data centers, AI, and cryptomining facilities demand enormous amounts of energy, which is why they are prime candidates for co-locations arrangements. However, the load growth these facilities are projected to bring is driving more fossil fuel generation, taking the Nation in the completely wrong direction to address the climate emergency. The Commission has an obligation to take into consideration this strong connection between these facilities and fossil fuels as it considers appropriate regulatory structures.

a. Data Centers, AI, and Crypto-Facilities Demand Seismic-Levels of
Energy.

Data centers are highly energy- and water-intensive. These facilities host vast computing infrastructure—such as server racks, networking equipment, backup power systems, physical security features, and advanced cooling technologies—to process massive amounts of data and support services like artificial intelligence, cryptocurrency mining, photo sharing, and video streaming.

Cloud Service Providers, largely owned and operated by major technology corporations, currently control approximately 67 percent of global cloud infrastructure.2 This dominance, coupled with Big Tech’s strategic prioritization of the AI and cryptocurrency sectors, is fueling a rapid proliferation of conventional and AI-specific data centers, as well as cryptocurrency mining operations, across the United States.

b. Data Centers, AI, and Crypto-Facilities’ Reliance on Climate-Killing
Fossil Fuels Is Driving Growth in the Fossil Fuel Power Sector.

Today, data centers, artificial intelligence, and cryptocurrency operations predominantly depend on energy sourced from fossil fuels, resulting in substantial emissions of carbon dioxide (CO₂) and other greenhouse gases. For example, each Bitcoin transaction is estimated to generate approximately 535 kilograms of CO₂, equivalent to the emissions from 1,186,233 VISA transactions or 89,203 hours of video streaming on YouTube.8

The immense electricity demand from data centers, AI, and cryptocurrency mining— largely met by fossil-fueled energy—not only accelerates climate change but also drives the continued operation and revival of gas and coal power plants, posing serious environmental risks. For instance, this year, Southern Company, a major U.S. utility, announced that it would be extending the operational lifespan of three coal plants in Georgia and Mississippi to address the growing energy demands of data centers.9 This decision permits the continued operation of a combined 8.2 gigawatts (GW) of fossil fuel-based, environmentally harmful energy capacity.10

Similarly, in February, NRG Energy Inc., GE Vernova, Inc., and The Industrial Company announced their collaboration to build four new fossil gas power plants, totaling more than 5 GW, to provide energy to data centers supporting AI and GenAI operations in the ERCOT and PJM markets.11

c. Energy Demand from Data Centers, AI, and Crypto Is Projected to
Grow Exponentially.

The energy footprint of these industries is expected to grow greatly in the coming years and decades, posing significant risks to the stability of the electric grid and potentially undermining national efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

Current trend analysis shows that global demand for data center capacity could grow by 19 to 27 percent annually through 2030, reaching as much as 171 to 298 GW per year by the end of the decade.12 This is a sharp increase from today’s estimated demand of 60 GW and signals the risk of a major supply shortfall.13 Avoiding this gap will require building at least twice the total data center capacity constructed since 2000, but in less than a quarter of the time.14 The demand for AI-compatible data center capacity alone is anticipated to grow at an average annual rate of approximately 33 percent through 2030.15 By the end of the decade, it is projected that roughly 70 percent of the total demand for data center capacity will be directed toward facilities specifically designed to accommodate advanced artificial intelligence workloads.16 Generative AI—currently the most rapidly expanding application of advanced AI— is expected to represent approximately 40 percent of the total demand.17

2. These Facilities Also Have Numerous Other Adverse Environmental
Impacts.

Beyond greenhouse gas emissions, data centers result in numerous adverse environmental impacts, including impaired air quality, increased noise, high water demand, and harm to special- status species and habitat.

First, data centers degrade air quality in surrounding communities, largely due to diesel backup generators. Since 2017, more than 1 GW of diesel-based energy—enough to power at least 161,969 households for a year—has been approved as emergency power for California data centers alone.21 These generators not only add to greenhouse gas impacts, but they harm public health through emitting nitrogen oxide and particulate matter. Researchers at UC Riverside and Caltech found that an increase in permits for diesel generators at data centers in Virginia since 2023 may have resulted in 14,000 asthma symptom cases and caused as much as $300 million in health care costs.22 By 2030, researchers predict the health burdens of data centers could amount to $20 billion.23

B. The Commission Should Maximize Opportunities For These Facilities To
Facilitate Renewable Energy Alternatives.
As noted, on the current trajectory data centers, AI and cryptomining facilities threaten to undermine progress on addressing the climate emergency. However, as we discuss next, the Commission should also consider the ways in which these facilities could instead help to promote load flexibility, renewable energy, and distributed energy resources.

1. These Facilities Can Help Address Load Demand Issues Through Demand
Response and Load Flexibility Programs.
A recent study from the Duke University Nicholas Institute for Energy, Environment & Sustainability demonstrates how using data centers as flexible load results in tremendous grid reliability benefits and critically reduces the need for new generation. Remarkably, if data

Simple shifts in data center operations would not only prevent the construction of new fossil fuel generation, and thus the creation of new greenhouse gas emissions which drive extreme weather and worsen public health, but it would also save money for the millions of residential customers who otherwise would have subsidized the costs for this new generation. Practically, load flexibility through demand response allows high-energy users like data centers to reduce energy consumption during periods of peak demand by adjusting operations, shifting workloads, and utilizing on-site back-up generation. According to the Nicholas Institute study, if data centers limited load a mere 0.5 percent (less than 44 hours out of the year), the grid could handle 98 GW of new load—enough to power 70 million homes. Increasing curtailment by another half-percent means the grid could handle an additional 28 GW of new load. Load flexibility does raise questions about whether demand response programs could be universal across the data center industry, such as for AI operations that require model training and around-the-clock power. In these cases, curtailment can still occur with “work” shifting geographically to other data centers that are available (i.e. not participating in a curtailment window).

The global average atmospheric carbon dioxide in 2024 was 424.61 parts per million (ppm), a level not seen for millions of years.97 The last time CO2 in the Earth’s atmosphere was at 400 ppm, global mean surface temperatures were 2 to 3°C warmer and the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets melted, leading to sea levels that were 10 to 20 meters higher than today.98 Global carbon emissions over the past 15 to 20 years have tracked the highest emission scenario used in IPCC climate projections, the RCP8.5 scenario,99 which is projected to lead to devastating impacts.100

B. The Commission Has a Mandate to Facilitate the Nation’s Clean Energy
Transition.
Congress has directed that FERC make the Nation’s transition away from fossil fuel power sources a high priority. In the Energy Act of 2020 Congress set forth a national goal “to reduce emissions and meet 100 percent of the power demand in the United States through clean, renewable, or zero emission energy sources … ”107  Given FERC’s vital role in overseeing the Nation’s power sector, this provision demonstrates a Congressional expectation for FERC to incorporate decarbonization into all its decision-making.

This is not a new Congressional priority. More than forty years ago, Congress passed the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA), directing FERC to reduce the nation’s reliance on fossil fuels, and charging the agency with administering new programs to foster the development of renewable energy resources.108 Thus, as the Supreme Court has explained, in considering the use of the term “public interest,” FERC is called upon to consider the objective to “reduce reliance on fossil fuels,” based on the fundamental premise that the country benefits from renewable energy development and the “resulting decrease in the Nation’s dependence on fossil fuels.”109
