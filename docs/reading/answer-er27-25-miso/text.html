II. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
MISO proposed LARS as part of its response to the Commission’s Show Cause Order,9
and as a standalone procedure that will immediately improve the study process for large loads looking to connect to MISO’s system and create significant opportunities for lowering the costs of mitigating the impacts of integrating the largest of large loads onto the MISO Transmission System.  As highlighted in many of the comments filed in this docket, the energy industry is changing quickly, and many states are experiencing significant new load additions in the near- term.10  LARS is one of MISO’s proposed solutions to more effectively address this problem.  It is a standalone process that enables the optimization of transmission solutions by allowing MISO to study both the large load and the generation that will serve it at the same time, promoting process efficiency, cost responsibility, and affordability.  The tool offers a vast improvement over the status quo leveraging available efficiencies in other MISO processes.

MISO appreciates the opportunity to respond to the protests and comments in support it received in response to its LARS filing.  In particular, MISO appreciates the opportunity to clarify misconceptions and concerns surrounding cost impacts.  LARS, in fact, provides an opportunity to better mitigate cost impacts of new Large Load integration over the status quo for LSEs that choose to leverage the tool. LARS Responds to the Commission’s Show Cause Order
First and foremost, LARS responds to several of the concerns raised in the Commission’s Show Cause Order.11  LARS addresses the Commission’s concern regarding the speed at which large loads are seeking to interconnect,12 by enabling the generation associated with the most resource intensive (i.e., the largest) large loads to be aligned and studied on a similar timeline to that currently experienced for Expedited Project Reviews (“EPRs”) of local transmission projects associated with large load additions.  LARS also enables the potentially-significant cost saving efficiencies that the Show Cause Order sought for electrically proximate large loads and associated generation by allowing for a more complete picture of the localized Transmission System and the identification of an optimized set of Network Upgrades to address the impacts of both the load and the generation, creating opportunities for more efficient, and potentially less costly Network Upgrades that could be identified in the separate load and generation studies that occur today.13

A. LARS is Responsive to the Commission’s Show Cause Order
The Electrically Proximate Requirement
Many of the protesters suggested that how MISO defined “electrically proximate” is not responsive to the Commission’s Show Cause Order.19  The protesters emphasize that the Commission defined “electrically proximate” as a “large load that is located no more than two substations away from the generating facility.”20  The protestors argued that MISO’s proposal of allowing the generation and load pair to be located in the same Local Resource Zone (“LRZ”) to meet the electrically proximate requirement fails to meet the Commission’s expectations.21  But the Commission expressly stated that it would “consider proposed revisions to this definition that ensure it is appropriately tailored to MISO’s transmission system.”22  MISO is expanding upon the Commission’s definition to fit its needs and resource adequacy construct.  LARS does not limit the electrical or physical distance between the load and generator within the same LRZ to provide maximum flexibility for the generation/load pairs.  MISO determined that requiring the generation and load to be located within the same LRZ enabled flexibility while still allowing it to identify optimized Network Upgrade solutions. D. LARS Application and Eligibility Requirements
i. LARS is Not Preferential to LSEs.
A common complaint among protestors was that LARS enables Load Serving Entities (“LSEs”) to gatekeep who can participate in the process.42  MISO disagrees with this assessment for several reasons.  First, under MISO’s current processes, only Transmission Owners may submit EPR requests – Interconnection Customers have never been allowed to submit these kinds of requests to MISO.  LARS does not change this dynamic.  LARS is a load-driven process, and an Interconnection Customer cannot require a Transmission Customer to scope their Transmission Service request around a generator they have not chosen to pair with.  Thus, it is logical to require the Transmission Customer to act as the applicant.  To the extent that an Interconnection Customer has developed an agreement to work together with a Transmission Customer, the requirement that the Transmission Customer initiate the application provides no barrier to participation.  If no such agreement exists, then the pairing of load and generation in a coordinated study process would be premature and speculative. LARS does not prevent Independent Power Producers (“IPPs”) from competing to supply energy to large loads under applicable state and or utility-led RFP processes. However, for an accelerated study process to have the level of certainty needed to function, the pairing must be established prior to submitting a LARS Application. iii. The 15-Year Pairing Commitment is Necessary for Certainty
The 15-year minimum pairing of the generation and load pairs in LARS is a necessary aspect of ensuring certainty for MISO and ensuring that LARS is not used as a queue-jumping process by Interconnection Customers.  MISO seeks to prevent either the generator or the load from using LARS to connect quickly and then operate without their partner.  Requiring a 15-year minimum commitment is a key part of ensuring that pairing is a formal, long-term commitment. MISO envisions that LARS pairs will perform concurrently even though MISO is not requiring a generating facility’s output to be matched to the demand of the electrically proximate large load. This is another requirement that will reduce speculative generation or load proposals that are not ready to connect to MISO’s transmission system.
