II. 
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
MISO proposed LARS as part of its response to the Commission’s Show Cause Order,9 
and as a standalone procedure that will immediately improve the study process for large loads 
looking to connect to MISO’s system and create significant opportunities for lowering the costs of 
mitigating the impacts of integrating the largest of large loads onto the MISO Transmission 
System.  As highlighted in many of the comments filed in this docket, the energy industry is 
changing quickly, and many states are experiencing significant new load additions in the near-
term.10  LARS is one of MISO’s proposed solutions to more effectively address this problem.  It 
is a standalone process that enables the optimization of transmission solutions by allowing MISO 
to study both the large load and the generation that will serve it at the same time, promoting process 
efficiency, cost responsibility, and affordability.  The tool offers a vast improvement over the status 
quo leveraging available efficiencies in other MISO processes.  
MISO appreciates the opportunity to respond to the protests and comments in support it 
received in response to its LARS filing.  In particular, MISO appreciates the opportunity to clarify 
misconceptions and concerns surrounding cost impacts.  LARS, in fact, provides an opportunity 
to better mitigate cost impacts of new Large Load integration over the status quo for LSEs that 
choose to leverage the tool.  While LARS was well-received by a wide variety of stakeholders,

LARS Responds to the Commission’s Show Cause Order 
First and foremost, LARS responds to several of the concerns raised in the Commission’s 
Show Cause Order.11  LARS addresses the Commission’s concern regarding the speed at which 
large loads are seeking to interconnect,12 by enabling the generation associated with the most 
resource intensive (i.e., the largest) large loads to be aligned and studied on a similar timeline to 
that currently experienced for Expedited Project Reviews (“EPRs”) of local transmission projects 
associated with large load additions.  LARS also enables the potentially-significant cost saving 
efficiencies that the Show Cause Order sought for electrically proximate large loads and associated 
generation by allowing for a more complete picture of the localized Transmission System and the 
identification of an optimized set of Network Upgrades to address the impacts of both the load and 
the generation, creating opportunities for more efficient, and potentially less costly Network 
Upgrades that could be identified in the separate load and generation studies that occur today.13

LARS Does Not Adversely Impact Projects in the Existing Interconnection Queue 
Third, MISO has structured the LARS process to not harm projects in the existing 
generation interconnection queue.  LARS projects are not displacing current projects in the 
Definitive Planning Phase (“DPP”) queue and claims that LARS projects will take the transmission 
headroom capacity of projects in future queue cycles are speculative.  LARS projects will identify 
optimized transmission solutions that could ultimately create more headroom on the transmission 
system with their potentially larger Network Upgrades, and any LARS projects and their 
associated upgrades may be used to help alleviate constraints observed in queue cycle studies.  
Moreover, maintaining LARS as a process separate from the DPP better insulates Interconnection 
Customers from bearing the cost of Network Upgrades needed to accommodate generation serving 
a LARS Associated Load.   
 
LARS Is Appropriately Scoped to Address the Unique Challenges and Opportunities 
Associated with the Largest Large Loads and Generation Pairs  
Fourth, the LARS Application and strict eligibility requirements are designed to ensure 
that only genuinely shovel-ready generation-and-load pairs participate in LARS.  MISO structured 
LARS to permit participation by all developers and resource types.  The process requires 
Transmission Customers to collaborate with other parties before submitting a LARS Application. 
Rather than making Transmission Customers (commonly LSEs) gatekeepers of the LARS process, 
this requirement recognizes the essential role Transmission Customers play in integrating large 
loads onto the grid.  Limiting loads to a minimum of 200 MW and requiring commercial 
agreements will act as a natural barrier to speculative projects and will result in only truly

LARS is Responsive to the Commission’s Show Cause Order  
The Electrically Proximate Requirement 
Many of the protesters suggested that how MISO defined “electrically proximate” is not 
responsive to the Commission’s Show Cause Order.19  The protesters emphasize that the 
Commission defined “electrically proximate” as a “large load that is located no more than two 
substations away from the generating facility.”20  The protestors argued that MISO’s proposal of 
allowing the generation and load pair to be located in the same Local Resource Zone (“LRZ”) to 
meet the electrically proximate requirement fails to meet the Commission’s expectations.21  But 
the Commission expressly stated that it would “consider proposed revisions to this definition that 
ensure it is appropriately tailored to MISO’s transmission system.”22  MISO is expanding upon the 
Commission’s definition to fit its needs and resource adequacy construct.  LARS does not limit 
the electrical or physical distance between the load and generator within the same LRZ to provide 
maximum flexibility for the generation/load pairs.  MISO determined that requiring the generation 
and load to be located within the same LRZ enabled flexibility while still allowing it to identify 
optimized Network Upgrade solutions. In developing LARS, MISO concluded that defining a 
maximum distance based on substations would impede the flexibility of large loads and

LARS Heeds the Call for Speed to Power and Supports Resource 
Adequacy  
The immensity and rate of large load additions across the United States is requiring unique 
proposals.  While MISO had already considered creating a process to study load with generation 
at the same time, the Commission’s Show Cause Order underscored the need to establish the study 
process sooner rather than later.  LARS advances that objective within the MISO region by 
providing a process through which significant new large loads and generation intended to support 
that load can be studied on an accelerated and coordinated basis.  LARS is another complementary 
tool that harmonizes with MISO’s large load framework including its recently proposed ZGIA 
process for co-located load.  Each of the proposed processes seek to address different challenges 
posed by large load growth and LARS provides a pathway for paired load and generation to be 
studied and commence service together.  
The Commission recognized in its Show Cause Order that “the relative speed and 
concentration at which large loads are seeking to interconnect appears to be outpacing the addition 
of new generation, which in turn heightens reliability risks and drives up costs to ratepayers” and 
stated it was “concerned that MISO and load serving entities are unable to plan adequate generation 
to serve all load at the pace such load seeks to connect.”27  MISO developed LARS in the context 
of this dramatic large load growth and is proposing the LARS process to help address that growth.  
The strict entry requirements for LARS both support its intended benefits and underscore that 
LARS is not, and should not be, a replacement for the DPP.28  Rather, it is intended to be a narrowly 
scoped but powerful process.  LARS speeds up the interconnection process for a select number 
and kind of project where the large load has formally partnered with generation for a period of 15

D. LARS Application and Eligibility Requirements  
i. LARS is Not Preferential to LSEs.  
A common complaint among protestors was that LARS enables Load Serving Entities 
(“LSEs”) to gatekeep who can participate in the process.42  MISO disagrees with this assessment 
for several reasons.  First, under MISO’s current processes, only Transmission Owners may submit 
EPR requests – Interconnection Customers have never been allowed to submit these kinds of 
requests to MISO.  LARS does not change this dynamic.  LARS is a load-driven process, and an 
Interconnection Customer cannot require a Transmission Customer to scope their Transmission 
Service request around a generator they have not chosen to pair with.  Thus, it is logical to require 
the Transmission Customer to act as the applicant.  To the extent that an Interconnection Customer 
has developed an agreement to work together with a Transmission Customer, the requirement that 
the Transmission Customer initiate the application provides no barrier to participation.  If no such 
agreement exists, then the pairing of load and generation in a coordinated study process would be 
premature and speculative. LARS does not prevent Independent Power Producers (“IPPs”) from 
competing to supply energy to large loads under applicable state and or utility-led RFP processes.  
However, for an accelerated study process to have the level of certainty needed to function, the 
pairing must be established prior to submitting a LARS Application.  LARS takes generation and 
Transmission Customer pairings as it finds them.  It is not intended as an avenue for exploring

iii. 
The 15-Year Pairing Commitment is Necessary for Certainty 
The 15-year minimum pairing of the generation and load pairs in LARS is a necessary 
aspect of ensuring certainty for MISO and ensuring that LARS is not used as a queue-jumping 
process by Interconnection Customers.  MISO seeks to prevent either the generator or the load 
from using LARS to connect quickly and then operate without their partner.  Requiring a 15-year 
minimum commitment is a key part of ensuring that pairing is a formal, long-term commitment.  
MISO envisions that LARS pairs will perform concurrently even though MISO is not requiring a 
generating facility’s output to be matched to the demand of the electrically proximate large load.  
This is another requirement that will reduce speculative generation or load proposals that are not 
ready to connect to MISO’s transmission system.  Requiring this commitment to remain in place 
for a 15-year period is a balanced approach that ensures the pairings remain in place for a long

MISO is requesting an independent entity variation for LARS, which is a unique process

compared to the traditional DPP queue.  Consistent with Order Nos. 2003 and 2023, MISO may

available transmission capacity.  MISO agrees that in most instances cluster studies are the most

optimized transmission solutions in the MISO footprint.
