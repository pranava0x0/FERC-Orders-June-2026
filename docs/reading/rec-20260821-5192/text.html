COMMENTS OF THE NEW JERSEY BOARD OF PUBLIC UTILITIES 
The New Jersey Board of Public Utilities (“Board”) files these comments in response to 
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) revisions to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff 
(“Tariff”) and the Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities to establish the 
Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”).  The Board appreciates the haste with which this 
proposal was filed and underscores the importance of moving forward with this proposal amid 
significant regional resource adequacy concerns.  While there are certain elements to the RBP that 
the Board would have pursued differently, the Board overall supports PJM’s proposal. 
I. 
The RBP Addresses System Reliability Needs 
First, the Board supports the Tariff revisions to add the targeted one-time procurement RBP 
starting in 2026, rather than relying on existing Tariff language which states that the Reliability 
Backstop Auction (“RBA”) will be triggered only after three Base Residual Auctions (“BRA”) 
clear sufficiently short of capacity.  The BRAs for the 2027/2028 and 2028/2029 Delivery Years 
(“DY”) have cleared short by 6.61 and 6.8 GW,2 respectively, and this deficit is only predicted to

effective way to protect ratepayers from costs and risks associated with load growth from data 
centers.  Therefore, the Board appreciates how PJM’s RBP proposal encourages bilateral contracts 
between incoming large loads and qualifying new supply resources.  This includes PJM’s retention 
of Charles River Associates to facilitate confidential and bilateral matchmaking, the offset of new 
large loads that have procured capacity from the initial RBP target, coupled with an allocation of 
RBP costs only to those that have not procured their own capacity, and the broader eligibility of 
resources that qualify for bilateral contracts compared to the RBP.  The Board hopes the bilateral 
matchmaking process underway meaningfully reduces the procurement target and subsequent 
costs from the RBP, as these contracts help insulate customers from the risks and costs attributable 
to these new large loads.   
The RBP’s centralized procurement terms, which provide a price commitment of up to 15 
years for qualified new supply resources, will also help attract new supply resources.  The Board 
supports PJM’s proposed Tariff revisions to conduct this backstop before waiting for an additional 
BRA to clear short.  The Board also supports assigning the costs of this procurement to zones in 
proportion to their expected large load additions.  This is necessary to ensure large loads can be 
made responsible for the costs, while recognizing that states have ultimate authority over retail 
cost allocations.  This RBP effort is a step in the right direction today – but, in the long term, PJM 
must continue to coordinate with stakeholders to develop robust reforms in its Powering Reliability 
Through Market Design workstream.

Board understands PJM’s reasoning for basing the central procurement target amount on the

a significant portion of this load growth is due to data center demand, the Board thinks that a 
subscription-based or “buy-bid” central procurement amount would have been better able to 
protect ratepayers from costs and risks associated with load growth from data centers and their 
uncertain load forecasts, as the procurement amount would be directly attributable to distinct data

costs would be allocated with more accuracy to these incoming large loads.  However, the Board 
realizes that this would require additional time before the backstop could be initiated and 
understands the importance of running this backstop procurement promptly.  The Board will work 
closely with other state entities and utilities to ensure that these costs are properly allocated to 
incoming data centers.   
Furthermore, while the Board appreciates many of the requirements and the standardization 
of eligible supply resources for the RBP, the Board would have liked to see more flexibility for the 
participation of demand response (“DR”) and distributed energy resources (“DER”).  DR and DER 
resources are intrinsically very different from generating resources, and requiring these resources 
to provide “customer agreements or binding commitments with identified locations and customer 
names” that can serve the MW for 15 years is very onerous.8  DR and DER resources build their 
business models expecting customer turnover; it is not reasonable to expect these resources to pre-
specify customers for the RBP contract period.  The Board thinks PJM should have pursued more 
flexible options for these resources – allowing these resources, for instance, to replace contracted 
customers to satisfy the RBP obligation.  The Board recommends more flexibility for DR and DER

The Board also has concerns about the opportunity for market power to arise in PJM’s 
selection process.  Specifically, projects with an earlier Delivery Year may be overly favored over 
cost concerns.  In PJM’s centralized procurement, resources with earlier Delivery Years are chosen 
first, up to the maximum willingness-to-pay cap of $555/MW-day UCAP; however, if these 
resources lead to a higher average price in early DYs and don’t initially clear, they can clear later 
in the process if lower-cost resources selected in later DYs create “room in the budget” to select 
these higher-cost, earlier-DY offers.  PJM, stakeholders, and the Federal Energy Regulatory 
Commission (“FERC”) should monitor the results of the RBP to ensure it produces an efficient 
outcome with just and reasonable results.  Similarly, the Board has concerns that the pay-as-bid 
market structure, compared to PJM’s uniform clearing price in the capacity market, would create 
inefficient market outcomes or allow for market manipulation.  PJM and FERC must monitor, as 
much as possible, how this structure affects auction results.9   
Finally, the Board has concerns about the collateral requirements for LSEs.10  The Board 
appreciates PJM’s efforts intended to protect consumers from the risk of stranded RBP costs.

for LSEs to secure the required collateral.  In theory, an LSE would pass on the collateral costs to

II. 
The RBP Opt-Out and Needed Clarification 
The Board appreciates PJM’s opt-out provisions that complement certain state laws.  New 
Jersey recently passed the Data Center Fair Share Act (“Act”) to help protect customers from the 
load impacts of new data centers in the state.11  The Act aims to provide data centers the 
opportunity to offset their load contributions through voluntary load reduction agreements with 
third parties.  Under one such available program specifically mentioned in the Act, the peak 
shaving adjustment (“PSA”), as detailed in PJM’s tariff under Attachment DD-2, a zone’s load can 
be offset by committed load reduction programs.  The Act notes that data center participation in 
such programs should relieve the data center from RBP costs and other related obligations. 
 
PJM’s RBP proposal provides an opt-out that allows electric distribution companies 
(“EDC”) to decrement their associated MW quantity of load that participates in PSA programs.  
This opt-out appears aimed at complementing and working with the Act, and the Board appreciates 
PJM’s consideration.  However, the Board thinks clarifications are needed to ensure the option is

workable within the RBP timeline.  Namely, it is currently unclear whether the RBP opt-out 
submission requires full compliance with Attachment DD-2 in time for the centralized 
procurement or whether program details under Attachment DD-2 can be accomplished later.  PJM 
should clarify that planned PSAs can qualify for the opt-out – MW for MW – if they are being

needed under Attachment DD-2, which would be September 2027.  New Jersey looks forward to 
participating in this effort and hopes that participation will be workable within the RBP deadlines. 
These types of efforts can help unlock efficient ways to incorporate large loads into the grid, and 
the Board truly appreciates PJM’s efforts to accommodate these state efforts. 
III. 
Conclusion   
The Board thinks PJM’s proposal strikes a good balance between prioritizing both least-
cost resources and those that can interconnect to the grid most quickly.  Granted, if the Board had 
designed the RBP to its own specifications, it may have made some reasonable changes. 
Nonetheless, the Board ultimately supports PJM’s RBP proposal and the Board recommends 
approving it so PJM can run the central procurement in 2026.
