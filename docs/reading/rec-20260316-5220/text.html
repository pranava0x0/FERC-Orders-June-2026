Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“Commission” or “FERC”), 18 C.F.R. § 385.211, the Digital Power Network (“DPN”) respectfully submits these comments on the February 23, 2026 compliance filing submitted by PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) in the above-captioned proceeding.1

DPN, an affiliate of the Digital Chamber, is the largest coalition of Bitcoin miners and digital infrastructure providers, representing over 80% of the U.S. public Bitcoin mining hashrate, with a significant compute presence in the PJM region that is increasingly evaluating co- located and behind-the-meter configurations. PJM’s February 23 compliance filing is therefore of substantial importance to DPN’s membership. As PJM explains, the filing was submitted pursuant to section 206 of the Federal Power Act in response to the Commission’s December 18, 2025 Order in Docket No. EL25-49, et al., which directed PJM to revise its behind-the-meter generation tariff provisions, establish new transmission service pathways for co-located load, and revise its necessary studies process.2 DPN appreciates the Commission’s and PJM’s efforts to bring greater clarity to the treatment of co-located load arrangements. Greater tariff specificity is needed. At the same time, the Commission should ensure that PJM’s compliance revisions do not inadvertently eliminate the operational flexibility that makes many emerging large loads valuable to the grid. Flexible loads, including many Bitcoin mining facilities, can rapidly curtail, respond to system conditions, and participate in demand response and related reliability-supporting

I. BACKGROUND
On February 23, 2026, PJM submitted tariff revisions to comply with the Commission’s December 18, 2025 Order in Docket No. EL25-49, et al.3 PJM states that the filing addresses three principal matters: revisions to retail behind-the-meter generation (“Retail BTMG”), the establishment of new transmission services for eligible customers taking service on behalf of co-located load, and revisions to the necessary studies process for existing interconnection customers seeking to serve co-located load.4 PJM further explains that the filing is paired with a separate initial brief concerning the rates, terms, and conditions associated with those new transmission services.5

As relevant here, PJM’s filing proposes a new definition of Retail Behind-the-Meter Generation that would impose a 50 MW materiality threshold for new Retail BTMG, subject to transition and legacy treatment for certain existing arrangements.6 PJM also proposes that facilities qualifying as Retail BTMG would be excluded from co-located load configurations.7

PJM further proposes new service pathways for co-located load, including Interim Network Integration Transmission Service (“Interim NITS”), Firm Contract Demand Transmission Service, and Non-Firm Contract Demand Transmission Service.8 PJM explains that Interim NITS would allow an eligible customer seeking NITS for co-located load to receive service on a non-firm, interruptible basis while required network upgrades are completed.9 Further, PJM noted that certain arrangements may subject generators to capacity interconnection right (CIR) delisting.10

DPN agrees that tariff clarity and more pathways for load flexibility are needed. But the Commission should ensure that PJM’s compliance revisions do not reduce the economic incentives for flexible large loads to curtail and hinder new generation deployment.

A. The Commission should ensure that PJM’s new co-located load transmission service framework preserves the operational flexibility of flexible loads.

PJM’s proposal to create formal service pathways for co-located load is an important step toward providing the certainty that project developers need. For years, the lack of clear tariff treatment for co-located load arrangements has created uncertainty for generators and large loads seeking to develop innovative on-site configurations. DPN therefore supports the Commission’s effort to require PJM to establish defined service options for co-located load and maximize current grid use.11

However, the Commission should also ensure that these new service pathways do not come at the expense of the flexibility that makes certain large loads valuable to the system. Many DPN members, including Bitcoin mining facilities, are large flexible loads that often exceed 50 MW and can respond rapidly to grid conditions. Their ability to curtail load, manage operations dynamically, and participate in demand response is central both to their business model and to the reliability value they can provide to the grid. The final tariff should therefore preserve, rather than diminish, these capabilities.

In particular, the Commission should ensure that the final framework does not unnecessarily undermine the economic and operational value of generators paired with flexible co-located load. PJM’s proposed necessary studies and interconnection revisions contemplate that serving co-located load may require reductions in Capacity Interconnection Rights (“CIRs”).12 DPN is concerned that, absent further clarification, those requirements and the structure of the new service pathways could reduce the incentives for flexible load projects to preserve curtailment-capable operating models that benefit the grid.

More broadly, DPN is concerned that the service constructs proposed here, while directionally responsive to the Commission’s order, impose uncertainty regarding the economic viability for flexible loads to partake in non-firm or load-flexible operation models, either through firm or non-firm contract demand services. The Commission should ensure that PJM’s compliance framework does not unintentionally limit benefits from demand response options that are integral to many loads’ operating models and reduce system stress.

B. The Commission should ensure that the final framework continues to support demand response and other reliability-enhancing behavior by flexible loads.

Flexible loads do not merely consume electricity; they can also support reliability when market rules and tariff structures preserve the incentives to do so. Many Bitcoin mining facilities curtail during periods of grid stress and can function as highly responsive demand- side resources. That operational flexibility can reduce system strain, particularly during

periods of high demand or transmission congestion. However, it is crucial to recognize that this flexibility is largely due to the price-sensitive nature of mining operations, in addition to their interruptibility, making the financial benefits of demand response critical. The concern for DPN is that overhauling the existing co-location framework may risk minimizing the benefits of operational flexibility associated with demand response. If the Commission adopts a framework that reduces the ability of flexible loads and their paired generators to maintain the economic attributes that make curtailment rational, the result may be less, not more, operational flexibility on the system.

Accordingly, the Commission should clarify that PJM’s compliance revisions must be implemented in a manner that preserves appropriate opportunities for flexible loads to participate in demand response and other reliability-supporting arrangements as both a business and reliability activity, consistent with the technical capabilities of those resources and the applicable market rules.

C. The Commission should require further consideration of PJM’s proposed 50 MW cap on new Retail BTMG.

DPN is also concerned with PJM’s proposed new 50 MW threshold for Retail BTMG. PJM explains that, under its proposed definition, new Retail BTMG would be limited to facilities with a cumulative nameplate rating of no greater than 50 MW, excluding certain qualifying backup generation, and that facilities qualifying as Retail BTMG would be excluded from co- located load configurations.13

That proposal may be too restrictive for many digital infrastructure projects. Many digital infrastructure developments operate at scales well above 50 MW. For those projects, behind-the-meter configurations can provide a viable pathway to speed-to-power, particularly where developers seek to pair load with generation at existing industrial sites, brownfields, or other locations with legacy infrastructure. A categorical 50 MW threshold risks foreclosing that pathway for a substantial segment of large computing infrastructure development in the PJM region, through imposing additional barriers to utilization.

DPN recognizes that the Commission directed PJM to establish a materiality threshold for Retail BTMG and that PJM has attempted to implement that directive in a clear and administrable manner. But the Commission should carefully consider whether PJM’s proposed threshold is broader than necessary to address the concerns identified in the underlying section 206 proceeding.14 In particular, the final framework should not eliminate practical development pathways for large loads without a sufficiently developed record showing that such a restrictive threshold is necessary.

DPN also urges the Commission to further consider whether a higher interim threshold would be more appropriate given present market conditions. As PJM itself notes in its filing, parties in the underlying proceeding argued that a higher threshold, such as 200 MW, could better preserve existing investments while the region develops additional information about the scale and impacts of large load growth.15 To the extent the Commission finds that additional transition or refinement is warranted, a higher interim threshold would be more consistent with current uncertainty regarding large load growth and could avoid prematurely forcing projects into less workable frameworks.

D. The Commission should not require projects to rely on a framework that may compound PJM’s already significant queue and study delays without greater justification. DPN is particularly concerned that PJM’s proposed treatment of large BTMG resources above 50 MW may force more projects into interconnection and study pathways at a time when PJM’s interconnection queue and associated study timelines remain a major barrier to infrastructure deployment. PJM’s filing confirms that existing interconnection customers seeking to serve co-located load will be subject to the necessary studies process and may need to enter superseding Generator Interconnection Agreements reflecting study results and facility modifications.16

The Commission has explained that these reforms are intended to address reliability and resource adequacy concerns.17 DPN does not dispute the importance of those objectives. But it is equally important to recognize that load forecasting for emerging large load sectors remains highly uncertain. Load modeling in this area is still developing, and forecasts can fluctuate materially as technologies, project timelines, and market conditions evolve. In that environment, a framework that categorically channels a larger set of projects into already- burdened study and queue processes may be premature, particularly where the underlying forecast assumptions remain unsettled.

For many projects, the practical effect of the 50 MW cap will be to eliminate a potentially viable behind-the-meter pathway and require reliance on co-located load or broader interconnection processes that are already slow and resource-intensive. The Commission should not assume that this shift is costless. For fast-moving computing infrastructure projects, delay itself can determine whether development occurs in PJM or elsewhere. The Commission should therefore require PJM to better justify why 50 MW is the appropriate threshold at this stage and whether a higher threshold, at least on an interim basis, would better accommodate current uncertainty while PJM and the Commission continue to refine their understanding of large load impacts.

III. CONCLUSION
DPN appreciates that PJM’s filing creates a clearer framework than the status quo. Formal service pathways for co-located load, recognition of non-firm service options, grandfathering for certain legacy BTMG arrangements, and the exclusion of certain backup generation from the 50 MW threshold all reflect an effort to respond to the Commission’s directives in a practical manner.18 These features may create useful pathways for some projects, particularly those capable of operating under non-firm or curtailment-based arrangements. Nonetheless, the Commission should not treat the existence of a more formal framework as sufficient in itself. The question in this compliance proceeding is whether PJM’s revisions are just, reasonable, and workable as applied to the broad range of large load configurations that will increasingly seek to interconnect and operate in PJM. On that question, DPN respectfully submits that the Commission should require further refinement. At minimum, the Commission should require PJM to: (1) clarify that the new co-located load service framework will preserve meaningful opportunities for flexible loads to participate in adequately incentivized demand response and related reliability-supporting arrangements;

(2) further justify or revisit the proposed 50 MW threshold for new Retail BTMG, including consideration of a higher interim threshold; and (3) ensure that implementation of the new framework does not unnecessarily exacerbate queue-related delays or foreclose commercially viable pathways for large flexible loads and data centers seeking timely deployment in PJM.

DPN respectfully requests that the Commission accept PJM’s compliance filing only to the extent it provides needed tariff clarity, while requiring further clarification and modification to ensure that the final framework preserves the operational flexibility of large flexible loads, maintains appropriate incentives for demand response and reliability-supporting behavior, and does not unnecessarily restrict viable behind-the-meter and co-located development pathways for Bitcoin mining, AI infrastructure, and other large computing facilities in the PJM region.
