III. 
BACKGROUND 
The concept of a multi-year forward period for RPM first emerged in PJM’s August 
2005 Initial RPM Proposal,24 and was designed to address one of several core deficiencies with 
PJM’s then-existing capacity construct—namely that, as PJM explained, the construct did “not 
look far enough into the future to secure capacity in time to meet reliability needs.”25  In 2005, 
this defect was glaring.  At the time, PJM’s capacity construct only required that Capacity 
Resources be committed for one day, and there was little incentive to commit for longer 
periods.  Moreover, Capacity Resources were permitted to “opt out” of their Capacity Resource 
status with as little as 36 hours’ notice.  PJM administered capacity credit markets only twelve 
months at a time, which were designed to accommodate short-term competitive load-switching

PJM’s current capacity accreditation methodology (ELCC) bears no 
resemblance to the eFORd model considered by the Commission in 
2006, and the three-year forward period forces PJM to integrate 
speculative assumptions into its ELCC modeling. 
At the time of the Commission’s 2006 approval of RPM’s multi-year forward period, 
PJM used a capacity accreditation methodology known as Equivalent Forced Outage Rate - 
demand, or “eFORd.”  This methodology focused on historic, unit-specific metrics that 
measured the percentage of time that a specific unit was forced out of service or unable to run 
when called upon.  For various reasons, including the nature of the then-prevailing generation 
mix and the types of operational risks faced by the PJM Region, eFORd was supportable when 
RPM was accepted.  Additionally, because eFORd relied on a resource’s own historical 
performance rather than on system-wide, forward-looking modeling of the resource mix, it had 
a high level of predictability, stability, and replicability for resource owners. 
On July 30, 2021, the Commission accepted PJM’s proposal to transition from eFORd 
to a new capacity accreditation methodology, known as Effective Load Carrying Capability  
(“ELCC”).95  Unlike eFORd, ELCC considers the simultaneous reliability contribution of all 
resources, and recognizes the complementary and antagonistic interactions among the

The current three-year forward period undermines the accuracy of 
other BRA planning parameters, including the assumed resource mix in 
each Locational Deliverability Area used to calculate CETO/CETL 
values, leading to unjust and unreasonable results.  
The planning parameters used in a given BRA play a critical role in the auction’s 
outcome.  For example, RPM utilizes an administratively set demand curve—the Variable 
Resource Requirement Curve (“VRR Curve”)—which represents the prices that consumers 
should be willing to pay for varying quantities of capacity.   In developing the demand curve 
for a given BRA, PJM forecasts the expected peak load for a particular Delivery Year and then 
sets a target level of capacity needed to reliably serve the load—also known as the Reliability 
Requirement—given the expected resource portfolio.  The auction’s supply curve is based on 
Sell Offers for capacity that Capacity Market Sellers submit at a specific price and, together, 
the offers comprise the auction’s supply curve.  RPM is a locational market, meaning there are 
defined areas within the PJM Region that have limits on the amount of capacity they can 
import.  Those areas are LDAs, which recognize and quantify the locational value of capacity

the import limit of an LDA (CETL) with the amount of capacity that needs to be imported into 
a LDA to meet the reliability criterion (CETO).  Sell Offers are located in specific LDAs based 
on where those resources are located. 
The accuracy of the planning parameters used in a given auction are only as good as 
the data upon which they are based.  As Dr. Nicholson explains, the three-year forward period 
undermines the accuracy of certain planning parameters, including CETO and CETL, because 
these values depend on assumptions about the resource mix and transmission topology that 
will actually exist in each LDA during the Delivery Year.104  As with the ELCC and load

and a half years in advance than when set closer to the Delivery Year.  These inaccuracies and 
uncertainties introduced into the CETO and CETL parameters can, in turn, lead to capacity 
prices that do not reflect market fundamentals in the Delivery Year by misrepresenting the 
transmission system’s ability to transfer capacity.105  This can lead to unjust and unreasonable 
outcomes.  Indeed, the idea that inaccurate planning parameters can warp the outcome of a
