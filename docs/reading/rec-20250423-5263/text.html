I. INTRODUCTION
In most aspects, NRDC agrees with the conclusions in the PJM Answer: the jurisdictional framework suggested by the Commission in the Order is correct; co-located loads’ use of the transmission system and wholesale services is a function of the specific configuration; co-located loads are best addressed by fitting them into existing market structures.  However, in some areas PJM’s Answer appears to suggest unduly preferential treatment for co-located loads or their associated generation, and the Answer also misses several specific issues that if left unaddressed could lead to unjust cost shifting or reliability violations.

II. COMMENTS

B. Certain Proposals in the PJM Answer Would Result in Unduly Preferential
Treatment
1. Creating an expedited interconnection process for generation dedicated to
co-located loads is unduly discriminatory.
The PJM Answer proposes one option in which co-located load elects to be network load and bring its own generation—either in the form of new generation or arrangements with existing generation that has or is scheduled to deactivate. PJM envisions that one way this option could be facilitated is through “a potentially faster interconnection path for generation resources supporting co-located loads.”7 This would entail a further erosion of open access principles beyond those recently approved by FERC as the Reliability Resource Initiative (“RRI”). In their concurrence to the RRI Order, Commissioners Phillips and Rosner noted that they accepted RRI because “it is a one-time, extraordinary measure.”8 If FERC were to approve

2. To avoid undue discrimination, flexibility of co-located loads must be
treated on the same terms as any other demand response.
Recent work has highlighted that flexibility on the part of large loads can dramatically reduce their impact on system reliability.11 Reflecting this, the PJM Answer includes an option for a new non-capacity backed service offering.12 Essentially, PJM proposes offering interruptible service to co-located loads.

However, PJM already has an interruptible service offering, in the form of its existing and well-developed demand response rules. Demand response in PJM’s capacity market is, at its core, a mechanism for customers to specify the price at which they are willing to designate a portion of their load as interruptible, along with detailed provisions for measuring and enforcing compliance with commitments to take interruptible service. PJM also offers a little-used Price Responsive Demand product that enables load serving entities to reduce their capacity obligations to reflect load that is automatically curtailed based on market conditions.13 The proposed non-capacity backed service would simply be a new third type of demand response. That is a perilous path, as it would be difficult to design such a product that did not amount to being simply an unduly preferential method for favored loads to provide demand response. For example, most load resources in PJM cannot offset their full capacity charge even if they commit to dropping to zero consumption on request,14 but PJM’s proposed non-capacity

If the history of demand response is any indication, it would likely take several iterations over many years before all the kinks were worked out of such a product. The region would be much better served by allowing co-located loads to represent their flexibility through the existing demand response products. If shortcomings are identified in those products that prevent realization of the full value of co-located load flexibility, they should be remedied in the existing products so that all flexible load has non-discriminatory opportunity to avoid capacity costs.

PJM’s discussion of this option reveals shortcomings in how demand response is treated in planning. Currently, PJM’s transmission planning simply assumes that the amount of demand response on the system will remain constant. Straightforward improvements to this unreasonable assumption would be appropriate and potentially avoid large unnecessary costs. Currently, utilities notify PJM of expected future large loads, which PJM then treats as adjustments to load forecasts. There seems to be no barrier to allowing the utilities, or the loads themselves, to also provide PJM with information about the future loads’ demand response capability as part of this same process. That would enable PJM’s planning to properly consider benefits from load flexibility, realizing the same value as the proposed non-capacity backed service without creating a new product.

C. The PJM Answer Fails to Address Important Planning and Reliability Issues
1. Co-located loads that rely on the wholesale grid for backup create a need
for capacity that is not adequately captured under PJM’s Tariff.
The PJM Answer presents options where a co-located, non-network load is primarily served by its own generator but may draw upon PJM when needed.15 Under current rules, such load would generally have no capacity obligation, provided that the on-site generation was running during the coincident peak hours PJM uses to allocate capacity costs. If done at scale this will create a reliability gap: when the co-located load’s generator is off-line, PJM will have load that it has not procured capacity for. Should this occur during a time of system stress, reliability may be threatened. This is a matter of particular concern because of the prominence of correlated or common-mode generator outages in recent reliability events. If outages of the generator serving the co-located load are correlated with outages of PJM’s fleet generally, PJM runs a risk of seeing an unplanned for increase in load just when supply is falling short. It is well established that utilities may charge for on-demand backup power service.16 However, PJM’s tariff currently contains no provisions for this. To maintain reliability, capacity must be procured for any significant load with the possibility of withdrawing from PJM’s grid during emergencies, such as would occur for a co-located load if the associated generator fails. Large loads that use backup generators may be somewhat similarly situated to co-located loads in this respect, provided that they use their generators to reduce load during coincident peaks. In this case, capacity is procured for those loads,17 but the peak shaving load is not allocated a share of capacity costs. Since PJM procures capacity based on these loads’ full year

2. Costs incurred on the basis of utility speculation of future loads may not be allocated to other utilities.

 This Section 206 proceeding raises very important issues, but NRDC also urges the Commission to be proactive in addressing the other ways in which PJM’s tariff and planning procedures need to be updated to the reflect the marked load growth—predominantly driven by data center development—currently being experienced across its footprint. Specifically, PJM should explore increasing the certainty and discipline in its load forecasting methodologies with respect to new large loads. Accurate load forecasts are essential to inform PJM’s transmission planning, interconnection procedures, and investment decisions by existing and new generation, storage, and demand response resources. Inflated load forecasts could drive a potential costly overbuild of the system that would ultimately be paid for by other rate classes other than these
