Introduction 
 
FERC’s July 23rd Technical Conference was a Constitutional Convention for PJM, the need for 
which we have seen evinced time and again in this most recent year of upheaval. Indeed, the 
first-of-its-kind Multi-state Summit on the State of PJM, held last September at the National 
Constitution Center, featured calls for such an event from across the political and stakeholder 
spectrum. We applaud the Commission’s timely attention to the problems plaguing PJM’s 
current governance structures, and focus on identifying substantive solutions, fast.  
 
As we celebrate 250 years since our nation’s founding, we must credit the institutional ideals 
and governing documents that underpin America’s longevity. Today, with electricity such a 
critical part of a functional, modern society, our grid governance requires a similar North Star to 
guide us. Nowhere is that need more evident than in PJM, our nation’s largest and 
longest-standing regional grid, which, as Chair Swett well noted, is facing “a grave legitimacy 
crisis.”  PJM is also approaching a momentous anniversary – its 100th birthday – and we must 
ensure the RTO sets itself up to thrive for the new century to come. We are ready to work with 
PJM and stakeholders to do so. 
 
Our organizations – RMI, Sierra Club, Earthjustice, Natural Resources Defense Council, 
and Southern Environmental Law Center (collectively “Public Interest Organizations”) – 
represent a coalition of regular participants in the PJM stakeholder process. Many of the 
undersigned organizations are members of the PJM Public Interest Environmental Organization 
User Group (PIEOUG). While other signatories are not members of the PIEOUG, all of the 
undersigned organizations are participants in PJM stakeholder processes and are affected by 
PJM’s governance practices. For all of us, PJM governance issues have been a long-standing 
concern that have impacted our efforts, and we value the opportunity to respond to the specific 
issues raised at the conference.1 We are encouraged to see the issues so clearly identified by 
the Commission for further public comment. We look forward to continued dialogue in the 
Dispute Resolution Service process and building on PJM’s proposal. 
 
Before addressing the questions individually, we introduce our principal concerns and remedies 
for PJM’s governance:

First, PJM must embed the public interest within its governance structure. By public 
interest, we are thinking expansively about the interests of the 67 million people who reside and 
pay electric bills in the PJM region, as well as the region’s businesses – large and small. Making 
a commitment to the public interest explicit is key, and underpins many of the other 
contemplated changes. Three specific reforms would help achieve this end: 
 
1.​ PJM’s governing documents should clearly state that the RTO has an obligation to 
serve the public interest. This idea is reflected in several pre-conference comments, 
including those of former FERC Chair Pat Wood.2 What’s more, other RTOs already do 
this, as Abe Silverman noted during the July 23rd event: MISO is set up as a 501(c)(4) 
organization “operated exclusively to promote social welfare,” while CAISO is set up as a 
“nonprofit public benefit corporation.”3 
2.​ It is equally critical that the PJM Board, leadership, and staff maintain an 
orientation towards serving the public interest, and not merely PJM members. The 
Independent Market Monitor, Joe Bowring, emphasized this in his conference remarks, 
noting not only the importance of the Board acting in the public interest, but also their 
directing PJM leaders and staff to do the same.4 Pat Wood suggests adopting public 
annual performance metrics, and tying compensation to their achievement, as in MISO. 
We agree that performance metrics linked to ensuring that PJM leadership serves the 
public interest would offer value in the form of both clearly defined outcomes and 
accountability in pursuing them.  
3.​ Finally, explicit inclusion of the public interest in PJM’s mission would necessitate 
a more substantive and formal role for states and consumer advocates within the 
stakeholder process, as representatives of the public. The breadth and diversity of 
state voices in the PJM region presents both challenge and opportunity in this regard, 
but the need for a stronger voice is real. Pat Wood notes that, among the four multi-state 
RTOs, he views PJM states’ voice within stakeholder and decision-making processes as 
“the most muted.” We therefore see merit to the idea of a PJM States’ Committee on 
Electricity (PJM-SCOE), with defined rights that could include issue identification and 
prioritization via a direct channel to the Board, consultative authority (particularly around 
matters regarding resource adequacy, over which states have significant 
responsibilities), and jump ball filing rights.5  
 
Centering the public interest is urgently needed to address the growing energy 
affordability concerns affecting many American families. Polling by Third Way recently 
found that one in four Americans say they already struggle to pay their utility bills, and another 
40 percent said they would have difficulty paying if costs increased further.6 Meanwhile, in July, 
the average US residential electric bill hit a record of $217 – with some of the biggest price

of financial strain for the majority of Americans, or PJM residents. To address this growing 
issue, PJM should adopt cost impact analyses to evaluate many of the proposed rule changes 
and reforms it is contemplating today, to ensure they do not further exacerbate (and ideally

Second, PJM must remedy the dysfunction of its current stakeholder process. We have 
participated in enough PJM committee and task force meetings to be able to say with certainty 
that they leave much room for improvement, as DOE Deputy Secretary Danly similarly raised in 
his opening remarks.8 Meetings are frequently side-tracked and bogged down by unwieldy 
question/comment queues, and lack the requisite procedural and decision-making structures to 
promote meaningful dialogue. Instead, obfuscation and grandstanding proliferate. Unresolved 
disputes, outstanding questions, and lack of clarity on future direction are common at meetings’ 
end, even if the meeting has taken up an entire day. The end result of this is an inability to act, 
even when issues demand immediate attention.  
 
The CIFP process (which literally has “Fast Path” in the name) initiated by the PJM Board last 
August is a case in point: set up to yield a decision by the end of 2025, it instead resulted in a 
dozen or so solution proposals, an inconclusive vote at year’s end, and a second round of CIFP 
stakeholder discussions initiated early this year, continuing into summer 2026. After PJM 
stakeholders debated – inconclusively – what to do for nearly a year, the issue of large load 
integration is just now approaching resolution, with serious implications for the capacity prices 
and reliability threats the region is facing. To fix this, PJM must address two tandem types of 
reform: structural changes and administrative/operational enhancements. 
 
Structural changes 
1.​ At the highest level, this entails a shift to an advisory stakeholder structure. The 
stakeholder process has become dominated by incumbent interests and far too prone to 
inertia and delay. By removing decision-making authority from the collective stakeholder 
body, the incentive to stonewall or slow-walk needed changes (especially insofar as they 
preserve the current status quo or protect incumbents) is lessened, and the whole 
process stands to become more streamlined and collaborative. Former FERC Chair 
Mark Christie recently noted that “those who stand to profit from the rules should not be 
writing them.” 9 We agree. We acknowledge that this shift might entail corresponding 
reforms to ensure efficacy, for which we provide more detailed thoughts in our response 
to question 11 below.  
2.​ Streamline the committee structure and meeting schedules. PJM has altogether too 
many meetings, and an overhaul could facilitate more cohesive, transparent, and 
accountable decision-making around a set of prioritized issues for stakeholders to be

3.​ PJM should refresh and rebalance its stakeholder sectors, to correct for imbalances

number of sectors should be expanded to reflect the growing diversity of PJM’s

provisions for the creation of new sector(s) to reflect specific stakeholder member 
groups. For example, a “public interest sector” should be created that offers an 
accessible, expanded participation role for organizations such as ours, many of which 
have contributed meaningfully to stakeholder discussions for years.10 Additionally, 
distributed energy resources and demand response providers, as a unique type of 
market participant, could benefit from their own sector. In ISO-NE, NEPOOL’s industry 
sectors include “Alternative Resources,” designed to provide a voice for distributed 
generation and other emerging technologies. There is also precedent for an expanded 
number of stakeholder sectors in MISO, which has 11 sectors, including one specifically 
for environmental organizations. ​
 
Administrative and operational enhancements 
1.​ Issue prioritization must become a core feature of the stakeholder process, as 
several noted during the Technical Conference. This will support both making most 
effective use of PJM staff’s valuable time, as well as giving PJM stakeholders the clarity 
and discipline they need to participate most effectively in informing issue resolution. The 
recently proposed stakeholder process for the Regional Organization for Western 
Energy (ROWE) –  the result of collaborative discussion and a look across the existing 
RTO landscape in search of pitfalls and best practices – offers a compelling model.11 The 
process they describe for the annual development of an Issue Catalogue and Roadmap 
could support more focused stakeholder discussions for the PJM region. 
2.​ The time is ripe to reconsider how input is solicited in the stakeholder process – in 
what form, and by whom.  
a.​ To the question of form, greater reliance on notice and comment, as exemplified 
by CAISO, may be appropriate at times in PJM.12 PJM staff seem to be trialing 
this with the call for white papers recently announced as part of the Powering 
Reliability Through Market Design workshops, which we find to be a more 
compelling way of gathering informed input on this topic from an array of

In addition, to support transparency and accessibility, PJM should post meeting 
transcripts or detailed summary notes, recordings, and votes on a better-organized PJM 
website.  
 
The work that PJM is undertaking, and that FERC and a wide array of stakeholders now aim to 
shape, is critical to the ongoing success and ultimate functionality of the RTO in a rapidly 
changing world. As plainly stated by panelists at the technical conference, both sitting and 
former FERC commissioners, and across the stakeholder membership at PJM, things cannot 
continue as they are. We are grateful for the opportunity to weigh in as a collaborative of 
multiple members within a key PJM sector on how these reforms should take shape, and are 
excited for swift transition from strategy development to implementation this fall.  
 
Responses to Individual Questions​
 
Topic: PJM Board Independence 
 
1) Should Board members’ terms be extended to five or more years? Yes 
 
Comments: 
We support five-year terms, but not longer. Longer Board terms will help Board members 
remain more independent, in the sense they are not subject to as frequent “re-election 
pressures,” and allow the Board to serve as a more stabilizing, decisive force. However, if the 
term extends too long, members may become too divorced from accountability, and the stakes 
of individual Board member elections too large. A five-year term would already put the PJM

Board term as the longest of any RTO; the vast majority of RTOs cycle on a 3-year Board 
term.14 The term length cannot be considered in isolation, however. Individual members should 
be elected in a staggered fashion, to ensure some degree of continuity for serving Board 
members, and to prevent wholesale turnover and loss of institutional experience during the 
onboarding of any new Board member. Per the subsequent question, Board members should 
also be limited to a maximum service term, and a maximum of two (2) consecutive terms.  
 
It is also important to set rules for the removal of Board members for cause, to mitigate against 
the risk that a longer Board term engenders. The Board should be set up to be self-policing in 
this way, such that a super-majority vote could remove a sitting Board member, and we also 
believe that a unanimous vote of the Nominating Committee should be able to effect a Board 
member’s departure.  
 
2) Should there be a maximum number of years that a Board member may serve (e.g., 10 
years)? Yes​
 
Comments: 
Setting a maximum number of years for Board service helps ensure the Board keeps up with 
the changing times. Too long a term duration could dampen the incentive to act quickly, and lock 
in perspectives that could become myopic. The change to the overall service limit should reflect 
the specifics of the extended term length. For example, if five years is chosen for a Board term, 
a reasonable limit would be ten (10) years, reflecting two consecutive terms. This is consistent 
with the proposal put forth by PJM themselves.15 
 
In extenuating circumstances, Board members should be able to apply for an exemption to a 
term limit. The criteria for extenuating circumstances should be defined in coordination with PJM 
staff. Examples could include their ability to contribute unique or extraordinary perspective, 
unique insight into a PJM geography or subregion, or unique insight into a key aspect of 
business operations. A Board member should only be eligible for one application and approval 
for exemption. If a Board member applies for an exemption and is not approved, they are not 
eligible to apply again. Unless another alternative is brought forward, the approval should 
remain within the purview of the Nominating Committee, which currently oversees Board 
appointments. Further discussion is necessary on whether the Nominating Committee should be 
able to approve the exemption with or without the Member vote that typically follows a 
Nominating Committee appointment determination.

4) Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off period for

Comments: 
The conflict-of-interest rules are one of the few precautionary protections in place to ensure that 
PJM Board decisions are unencumbered by bias and prior loyalties. We see no justification or 
advantage to reducing the cooling off period. ​
 
5) Should restrictions on Board communications be relaxed to enable more 
communication between the Board and stakeholders? Yes 
 
Comments: 
While some restrictions make sense and were enacted for a reason – to prevent undue 
influence – we worry the PJM Board has become too aloof to be able to discharge its duties 
optimally. Thus, while “relaxed” is a somewhat vague term to convey the nature of necessary 
reform, reform to current communications between Board and stakeholders offers potential 
benefit. As inspiration, we can look to examples from other RTOs: 
 
1.​ MISO: MISO heavily funnels communication through an Advisory Committee and 11 
distinct stakeholder sectors. Board communication is restricted outside of structured 
public updates or explicit board retreats. Under its updated governance guides, decorum 
and official channels are tightly policed by committee chairs. 
2.​ SPP: SPP features a built-in Regional State Committee (RSC) made up of state utility 
commissioners. Because state regulators hold primary authority over cost allocations, 
SPP’s board communications are uniquely exposed to state political/regulatory 
viewpoints, requiring high transparency to ensure individual market participants are not 
frozen out of multi-state negotiations. 
3.​ ISO-NE and NYISO: Both New England and New York utilize highly structured, 
formalized rules where the independent board acts as a separate entity from the 
stakeholder committees. Private communication regarding pending market rules or 
active appeals is generally prohibited; stakeholders must rely on formal, scheduled joint 
board-stakeholder symposia to air grievances collectively. 
4.​ CAISO: Because CAISO manages a single-state grid but operates a multi-state Western 
Energy Imbalance Market (WEIM), it balances strict California state transparency 
requirements with broader western stakeholder engagement. Ex parte protocols are 
deeply influenced by whether a topic involves a CPUC docket or a FERC market filing.

These demonstrate that there are ample ways that the communication between the Board and 
stakeholders could be different. One specific improvement we recommend is that the PJM 
Board annually invites sector(s) to sit down and discuss general sector concerns or consider 
educational materials. These regular-cadence meetings can offer an incredibly informative and 
important space for dialogue and relationship building. The PJM Board has historically and 
should continue to host these, however, they should be revised so that all sector-specific 
meetings are open to attendance by observers, including the media.  
6) In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the Board be 
required to explain its reasoning? Yes 
 
Comments: 
If the Board is to be granted more decision-making authority, corresponding transparency and 
accountability enhancements are warranted. As the saying goes, with great power comes great 
responsibility, and stakeholders must be able to monitor and evaluate the extent to which the 
Board is discharging their responsibilities well. However, this question asks two things.  
●​ First, should Board votes be made public: Yes, as a general rule, Board votes should be 
made public. This is a useful transparency measure, and, if Board members’ 
re-nomination worries are lessened due to longer term lengths, there should be little to 
fear from making votes on the record.  
●​ Second, should the Board be required to explain its reasoning? Yes. Regardless of the 
public nature of the vote, the Board should be required to explain its reasoning; this is 
core to good governance and a reasonable requirement to ask of PJM’s leaders. 
7) In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or available 
to the public? Yes 
 
Comments: 
Yes, as above, increasing transparency into Board proceedings offers meaningful improvements 
that will help (re)build trust in PJM leadership and the stakeholder process. Board meetings 
should be public, with limited exception for discussion on sensitive or confidential items, 
and any related private votes. This hybrid model is not new. PJM can look to other RTOs to 
see models of where this works. SPP MISO, ERCOT, and CAISO all practice a combination of 
closed and public Board meetings. At present, only NYISO and PJM have closed meetings. 
However, efforts to increase Board transparency cannot stop here.  
PJM can go one step further and in adopting a primarily open (or hybrid) model, enhance some 
of the inefficiencies seen at other RTOs with similar practices. For example, in SPP, meetings 
may be characterized as open, and any stakeholder is allowed to register and attend to a point 
(i.e., when discussing confidential or sensitive information). However, the transition from open to 
closed meeting is not always clear on the meeting agendas, and without attending every 
meeting, one may not know that a portion of the meeting was closed at all. Explicit labeling 
(public/closed) in meeting agendas, and potentially creating a “hybrid” meeting designation in 
the PJM stakeholder event center would offer significantly improved clarity on what parts of a 
meeting are open (though again, the default should be most).

8) Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the 
responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest? Yes 
 
Comments: 
This is a linchpin reform, which could flow through to an array of additional governance and 
stakeholder process enhancements, and deliver materially better outcomes for the region. As 
noted in our introductory remarks, by public interest, we are thinking expansively about the 
interests of the 67 million people who reside and pay electric bills in the PJM region, as well as 
the region’s businesses – large and small. Making the obligation to serve the public interest 
(defined in this way) an explicit feature of PJM’s governing documents is key; it cannot merely 
be implied. Doing so is essential for enabling some of the other, complementary reforms 
envisioned, such as developing performance metrics for the Board and leadership, which could 
include accountability to public interest provisions. There is support for this idea in several 
pre-conference comments, including those of former FERC Chair Pat Wood. What’s more, other 
RTOs already do this, as Abe Silverman noted during the July 23rd event: MISO is set up as a 
501(c)(4) organization “operated exclusively to promote social welfare,” while CAISO is set up 
as a “nonprofit public benefit corporation.”  
 
This should also manifest as another attempt to update the PJM mission. An attempt was 
made to update the mission statement on January 27, 2026 that would have explicitly 
incorporated affordability and public interest language. The draft language read:  “As the primary 
task, to ensure the safety, reliability and security of the bulk electric power system. [In addition], 
understand customer needs and deliver valued service to meet those needs in an affordable 
and cost-efficient manner. Create and operate robust, competitive and non-discriminatory 
electric power markets. Achieve productivity through the efficient union of superior knowledge 
workers and technology advances.” 16 
 
While that update to the mission was not adopted, it serves as a blueprint for the type of thinking 
that needs to be encouraged to ensure the mission of PJM reflects not only affordability, but also 
the public interest. An example of a further updated mission statement that explicitly integrates 
both could be the following: “As the primary task, to ensure the safety, reliability, affordability 
and security of the bulk electric power system on behalf of the public interest…” 
9) Should state representatives have at least one voting member on the Nominating 
Committee? Yes 
 
Comments: 
State representatives and consumer advocates should have a presence on the Nominating 
Committee. Specifically, we support the consumer advocate proposal to have one seat for a 
state executive branch appointee, one for a state regulator, and one for a consumer advocate. 
This is another reform that would help make the public interest a living value within PJM.

10) Should PJM adopt public performance metrics and tie management compensation to 
their achievement? Yes 
 
Comments: 
Tying executive leadership compensation directly to a series of performance metrics is industry 
best practice for many of the businesses and organizations that comprise PJM’s membership. 
Some combination of inability, lack of incentive, or unwillingness of current leadership to execute 
amidst complicated stakeholder headwinds has made itself evident. Without a clear nexus 
between PJM’s performance and direct consequences for leadership, it is unlikely that change 
will organically emerge.  
Development of the public performance metrics should follow the work done in devising the 
FERC Order 1920 benefit metrics, which sought to holistically capture the value that wholesale 
markets offer the region. PJM (either at the Board or leadership level) should similarly be 
empowered to articulate performance metrics that recognize the holistic set of roles articulated 
in their own updated mission statement, in which they aim to address four objectives:  
1.​ To ensure the safety, reliability and security of the bulk electric power system. 
2.​ Understand customer needs and deliver valued service to meet those needs in an 
affordable and cost-efficient manner. 
3.​ Create and operate robust, competitive and non-discriminatory electric power markets.  
4.​ Achieve productivity through the efficient union of superior knowledge workers and 
technology advances.”17 
Building on our response to Question 8, we would like to see inclusion of the public interest in 
this mission statement, and thus tied in to PJM’s performance metrics.

12) Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over the energy market and ancillary

13) Should PJM have section 205 filing rights over Regional Transmission Expansion 
Planning (RTEP) unencumbered by the requirement to obtain approval of two-thirds of 
the Members Committee? Yes 
 
Comments: 
PJM should have section 205 filing rights over the RTEP process. Effective regional 
transmission planning is essential to PJM’s mission to provide reliable and affordable electric 
service to the region. However, as explained in response to other questions, PJM’s independent 
exercise of section 205 filing rights should be balanced by providing jump-ball filing rights to 
OPSI and to PJM Members where those entities achieve super-majority support for an 
alternative filing. 
 
In addition, we should reconsider which section 205 filing rights are retained by the PJM

14) Should the Organization of PJM States Inc (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights over

17) Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing 
committees, also apply to lower-level committees? Yes 
 
Comments: 
PJM’s stakeholder process is overly captured by incumbent interests, and reforms such as this 
– in tandem with expanded stakeholder sectors (more on this below) – would help to re-weight 
stakeholder representation in a manner more reflective of the growing, diversifying range of 
perspectives seeking insight and input into PJM rules. PJM should also consider how to 
regularly assess and refresh its mechanisms for responding to evolving needs of stakeholders, 
recognizing that voting sectors and member types may evolve, and representation structures 
should not be allowed to become outdated or ineffective. Examples of these types of durable 
process reforms include:  
●​ Make votes at every level public 
●​ Introduce a process for new sector designation, including any criteria or threshold that 
such a sector might be required to meet  
●​ Create clear rules for attendance for “Open” versus “Closed” meetings 
●​ Establish standard expectations for participation during meetings 
●​ Expand report-out requirements from working group decisions to clarify the composition 
of the vote 
●​ Establish protocols for representation of meeting outputs in subsequent stakeholder and 
decision-making bodies 
A number of additional reform opportunities, at both the structural and administrative levels, 
exist to address the stakeholder process. As noted in the introduction, these include:

●​ Streamline the committee structure and meeting schedules. PJM has altogether too 
many meetings, and an overhaul (including elimination of some lower level committees)

19) Should state representatives have a formal voting role in the stakeholder process 
(limited to certain topics, such as resource adequacy and regional transmission cost 
allocation) in addition to the consumer advocate votes? No 
 
Comments:​
State representatives are not ordinary stakeholders in the PJM process and should not be 
limited to expressing their views through voting in the PJM stakeholder process. Instead, state 
representatives should have jump ball section 205 filing rights where they reach super-majority 
support for a proposal that differs from one that PJM wishes to advance. Providing state 
representatives with jump ball filing rights better recognizes the important role of the states, 
especially with regard to issues such as resource adequacy that are in large part subject to 
state jurisdiction. In contrast, limiting state representatives to a voting role within the PJM 
stakeholder process creates inappropriate risks that state perspectives may be ignored and that 
the Commission will not have the opportunity to evaluate and approve policies that states in the 
PJM region support.
