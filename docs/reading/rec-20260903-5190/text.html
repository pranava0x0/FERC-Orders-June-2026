OFFICE OF THE FEDERAL ENERGY ADVOCATE 
 
On August 13, 2026, PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) filed, pursuant to section 205 of 
the Federal Power Act, revisions to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff (OATT) and the 
Reliability Assurance Agreement (RAA) to establish a new Interim Resource Adequacy Service 
(IRAS). PJM proposes IRAS as a condition of transmission service for New Large Load (NLL), 
as defined in the filing, to be used during periods when there are insufficient resources to reliably 
meet demand in real time operations starting with the 2027/2028 Delivery Year (i.e., June 1, 
2027). When triggered by system conditions, IRAS will enable PJM to prioritize load reduction 
in zones/areas where NLLs have not brought their own new capacity or had their capacity needs 
covered through the Reliability Backstop Procurement (RBP).1  
To support the IRAS framework, PJM proposes numerous revisions to the OATT and/or 
RAA to include, among others, 1) incorporating definitions of Large Load, New Large Load, and 
Eligible New Large Load; 2) clarifying a Load Serving Entity’s (LSE) obligation to bring new 
capacity in an amount equal to or exceeding the peak load of the NLL it serves or be subject to 
load curtailment prioritization during emergencies; 3) establishing a Large Load Registry and 
requiring LSEs and electric distribution utilities (EDUs) to provide information to the Registry as 
necessary for PJM to determine the megawatt (MW) quantity of IRAS responsibility by

I. 
Background 
PJM’s capacity market, called the Reliability Pricing Model (RPM), is designed to ensure 
long-term grid reliability by procuring the appropriate amount of supply needed to meet 
predicted peak load, plus an adequate reserve margin, three years in the future. A primary 
objective of the capacity market is to match (or balance) power supply with future demand, 
thereby creating long-term price signals to attract needed investments to ensure resource 
adequacy. Under the RPM, capacity resources are procured by PJM through an annual, 
competitive Base Residual Auction (BRA). 
In December 2025, the BRA conducted for the 2027/2028 Delivery Year represented the 
first time in recent history that large load additions significantly increased PJM’s load forecast 
which, in turn, significantly increased the PJM Region Reliability Requirement, in megawatts, 
PJM sought to procure in the auction. The auction resulted in a capacity procurement that was 
6,517 MW short of the PJM Region Reliability Requirement and 5.6 percent below the target 
20% Installed Reserve Margin (IRM).3 Similarly, in July 2026, the BRA conducted for the

II. 
Comments 
A. 
Data Centers Are the Large Loads Driving Historic Demand.  
As an initial matter, the Ohio FEA maintains that data centers are the large loads driving 
historic demand in the PJM footprint and, therefore, PJM should define Large Load to be 
specific to data centers, not all loads of a designated size. It has become obvious to most that data 
centers are unique large loads that warrant distinction from other commercial or industrial 
customers. Ohio was one of the first states to establish data center tariffs with a defined data 
center customer class, and the Ohio FEA recommends PJM do likewise.8  
PJM goes on to define New Large Load for purposes of applying a Bring Your Own New 
Capacity (BYONC) construct, and Eligible New Large Load for purposes of determining an 
IRAS obligation based on whether NLL fulfills its BYONC requirement. Eligible NLL is 
calculated as the difference between a NLL’s registered peak load in MW and its BYONC 
arrangements and/or Allocated RBP Unforced Capacity in MW, as reported in the Large Load 
Registry.9 Again, no distinction is made between the computational loads that are directly 
responsible for the current state of resource adequacy – such as hyperscale data centers and 
cryptocurrency miners – and traditional manufacturing loads that drive American industry. As 
this nation, under the current administration’s leadership, works to onshore and expand domestic

B. 
BYONC Is Necessary to Avoid Curtailment and Maintain Reliability.  
The Ohio FEA supports a BYONC framework and PJM’s proposal to incent LSEs to 
bring new capacity resources to bear commensurate with the addition of NLL, which PJM 
defines to mean new or incremental large loads that go into service after June 1, 2027. We agree 
that bringing new capacity to match new large load is the optimal solution to address resource 
adequacy concerns and is strongly preferred to IRAS. However, given the challenges of bringing 
new capacity resources online to meet new demand in the near term, we find PJM’s BYONC 
proposal to be reasonable. PJM proposes that, starting on June 1, 2027, any NLL that has not yet 
brought new capacity would be subject to priority curtailment during emergency grid conditions 
(i.e., IRAS Action). As the electric grid is and will continue to be stressed by an imbalance 
between demand and supply, IRAS Action is a critical operational tool for PJM to use when 
system conditions threaten reliability in real time. As further discussed below, this tool also 
protects other non-large load customers from curtailment. To avoid potential IRAS Action, NLL 
must contract with new capacity that meets the BYONC gating criteria, including that the new 
capacity will be available for at least 10 years.  
C. 
Registry Is Key to Successful Implementation. 
The Ohio FEA appreciates PJM’s proposal to establish a Large Load Registry, which is 
fundamental to the IRAS framework and necessary for successful state implementation. As 
described in the filing,  
PJM will use the registry to determine the quantity of [NLL] by zone/area 
and allocate IRAS responsibility to each zone/area accordingly, starting 
with the 2027/2028 Delivery Year. States may leverage the Large Load 
Registry to inform the implementation of retail constructs including load

The Ohio FEA agrees that the registry will be extremely helpful in assisting states with 
standardizing data around large loads generally and is a necessary component for administering 
the IRAS framework. Details around the proposed registry highlight the significant and ongoing 
collaboration of many parties that will be necessary to execute a connect-and-manage program. 
Consistent with state public record laws and administrative procedures, state commissions 
routinely receive and maintain confidential information from regulated utilities and suppliers, 
parties to legal proceedings, auditors, project developers, competitive auction bidders, etc. Thus, 
there should be no concern about data protection standards for state commissions with access to 
the registry. On the contrary, the only way that states, LSEs, EDUs, and PJM can reasonably be 
expected to accurately co-manage a program like IRAS is through a centralized repository like 
the Large Load Registry.   
D. 
IRAS Actions Must Precede Other Emergency Actions to Protect Other 
Customers. 
 
PJM’s filing describes the current practices set forth in PJM Manual 13 for when capacity 
shortages give rise to emergency procedures, including three levels of NERC Energy Emergency 
Alert Levels (EEA1 through EEA3), with EEA3 being the highest alert level. PJM also describes 
the way it would incorporate IRAS Actions into those emergency procedures.11 While PJM 
explains that grid operators have the flexibility to implement emergency procedures in whatever 
order is required to ensure system reliability, the Ohio FEA strongly supports PJM’s proposal to 
generally sequence IRAS Actions first at the beginning of an EEA1.12 The electric grid is

increasingly constrained by the addition of large loads; therefore, it is reasonable for large loads 
that do not play a role in remedying the supply and demand imbalance to be curtailed prior to 
other load. This is an essential element of PJM’s proposal. Deploying IRAS Actions prior to 
deploying Pre-Emergency Load Management Reductions will protect other customers from a 
wide range of pre-emergency (EEA1) and emergency (EEA2) actions that otherwise would 
impose significant financial impact and shift reliability risk to other customers. Further, 
prioritizing IRAS Actions is a necessary step to comply with the Statement of Principles 
Regarding PJM, as signed by the National Energy Dominance Council and the 13 PJM 
governors, and the Ratepayer Protection Pledge.  
Should PJM fail to prioritize IRAS Actions, such as re-sequence them to occur just prior 
to the issuance of manual load shed under an EEA3, damage to retail ratepayers will already 
have been done. During emergency system conditions, the dispatch of Emergency Demand 
Response Resources (generally under EEA2) will cause energy prices to rise dramatically for all 
loads. Non-essential building loads will be curtailed. Emergency back-up generators will be 
dispatched. These costly and harmful scenarios could all be avoided (or at least minimized) by 
deploying IRAS Actions first, at the beginning of an EEA1, as PJM proposes. 
Also importantly, as PJM notes, if PJM were to call IRAS Actions after Demand 
Response Resources have been exhausted, this would eliminate the incentive of NLL to contract 
for new capacity because they would be insulated from curtailment by Demand Response.13 
Putting IRAS in the privileged position of being deployed after Emergency Demand Response 
would serve as a perverse incentive for NLL to actually prefer IRAS over bringing new capacity 
to the system as required to ensure resource adequacy and reliability.

E. 
States Must Have Flexibility to Determine IRAS Action Compensation. 
The need for curtailment, and therefore compensation, would not exist but for Eligible 
NLL connecting to the system before adequate supply is available. The foundation of PJM’s 
IRAS proposal, if approved by the Commission, is that Eligible NLL will be able to connect to 
the grid prior to meeting the BYONC requirement. Thus, the Eligible NLL will benefit from the 
capacity market and appropriately pay capacity costs. Under IRAS, the capacity market will still 
serve as the primary vehicle to incent resource adequacy in the PJM region, and most capacity 
resources will still be obligated to make a cost-based bid into the energy market. And as 
recognized by PJM’s Effective Load Carrying Capability accreditation methodology, capacity 
will continue to have value across all hours and seasons, not only during critical peak hours. 
Accordingly, Eligible NLL will derive capacity market benefits most of the time (i.e., all 
intervals during which they are not called upon to curtail pursuant to an IRAS Action). 
However, the Ohio FEA recognizes that Eligible NLL subject to priority curtailment may 
not receive the full benefit afforded to other capacity backed loads. While allowing Eligible NLL 
to connect to the system and benefit from the capacity market without bringing new supply could 
be considered a form of compensation, some stakeholders in the CIFP process asserted that a 
level of compensation may be appropriate. To address the issue, PJM proposes a wholesale 
compensation rate equal to 50% of its existing Non-Performance Charge Rate but asserts that the 
wholesale rate is flexible and can be reduced to less than 50% of the Non-Performance Charge 
Rate based on retail rates.14 In establishing the wholesale compensation rate, PJM specifically 
states: 
Nothing in this Tariff, Attachment DD-4, section X shall interfere with any 
Eligible New Large Load’s election to (i) accept in its retail rates a sum less 
than 50% of the Non-Performance Charge Rate as compensation for the

While offering no opinion on whether the proposed 50% of the Non-Performance Charge 
Rate is just and reasonable, the Ohio FEA appreciates the deference shown to states to further 
determine IRAS compensation through retail ratemaking.16 States may find it necessary or 
appropriate to consider whether compensation is consistent with the Ratepayer Protection Pledge 
and/or other commitments to insulate other customers from cost shifting that could arise if 
Eligible NLL is compensated for IRAS Action. Accordingly, as PJM describes, states may 
determine, through their retail tariffs or other regulatory mechanisms, whether IRAS 
compensation should be 50% of the Non-Performance Charge Rate or lower, even zero. Should 
the Commission find there to be a lack of clarity on PJM’s proposed compensation framework, 
we request the Commission affirm in its Order that states have optionality to determine the level 
of IRAS compensation at the retail level. 
III. 
Conclusion 
PJM’s IRAS is a timely effort to confront reliability concerns caused by significant data 
center load growth in the region. The proposed BYONC construct, the Large Load Registry, and 
the curtailment prioritization of certain loads when necessary to ensure reliability during 
emergency conditions will all require massive new coordination effort by PJM, states, EDUs, 
and LSEs. Together we must ensure that new resources are secured to meet new demand while 
mitigating any compounding effect on PJM grid operations, markets, or other ratepayers.
