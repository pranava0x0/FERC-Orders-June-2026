I. 
 
BACKGROUND 
As indicated in the July 31 Filing, both the 2027/2028 BRA and the 2028/2029 BRA 
cleared at the market price cap of $325/MW-day and over 6 GW short of the applicable PJM 
Regional Reliability Requirement.6  In the July 31 Filing, PJM proposes the RBP to procure

in the 2028/2029 BRA for commitment periods of up to 15 years ending, in all cases, with the 
2042/2043 Delivery Year.7  PJM describes the RBP as “a critical safety net designed to address 
current resource adequacy shortfalls”8 and explains that the 15-year term is informed by the 
statement of principles agreed between and among governors of PJM states and the National 
Energy Dominance Council,9 “which seeks ‘[r]evenue [c]ertainty to [n]ew [g]eneration’ through 
‘15-year price certainty for new capacity resources.’”10

A. 
Any Soft Cap Should Be Set at a Level Reflecting Current Costs of Resources Whose 
Capacity Could Be Procured through the RBP 
PJM fails to carry its burden to demonstrate that setting the RBP soft cap at $555/MW-day 
is just and reasonable.  PJM asserts that its “proposal to limit the maximum willingness to pay is 
necessary,” because “[t]he RBP is not disciplined by direct buy bids that explicitly express 
willingness to pay.”27  According to PJM, “it is not reasonable to assume that load should be 
obligated to pay an unlimited amount for supply.”28  The $555/MW-hour figure was calculated 
based on outdated estimates of the cost of new entry (“CONE”) and the energy and ancillary 
services revenues for the reference combustion turbine (“CT”) in PJM’s most recent quadrennial 
review.  As such, it is not representative of the current CONE for such a unit, much less of the 
costs of other resources whose capacity could be procured through the RBP, including resources 
whose technology or circumstances do not match those of the reference CT.

considerations”29 that have prompted PJM’s decision to impose a cap, it bears emphasis that the

Commitments to the Extent the Load Prompting the RBP Does Not Materialize

offer as price-takers into RPM Auctions for the associated Delivery Years “is antithetical to

undermines it.”55  After interventions that produced “unsustainably low price levels for three 
delivery years,”56 Dr. Morris states, “the market is waiting for PJM – and implicitly, the 
Commission – to make credible moves that they will not allow capacity prices to crash to levels 
that make new investments likely unprofitable over the life of the assets.”57  In this environment,

I. 
The “Soft” Price Cap of $555/MW-Day Can Only Serve to Raise Costs to 
Residential Customers 
10. The Soft Cap is pitched in the RBP Proposal as a just and reasonable limit on the 
willingness to pay for new capacity.  (RBP Proposal, at 62.)  We can all hope that 
sufficient capacity at low enough prices is available so that the Soft Cap will not 
become binding.  But if the Soft Cap is binding—that is, PJM does not purchase the 
full RBP Target because the Soft Cap requires PJM to reject offers from willing 
sellers—then the main effect of the Soft Cap is to raise capacity prices in general, 
and indirectly to residential customers.  Accordingly, I recommend that the 
Commission does not accept the proposed Soft Cap; or, at a minimum, the 
Commission raises the Soft Cap to reflect increases in costs and uncertainty for new 
construction since PJM’s last Cost of New Entry (“CONE”) study. 
11. The Soft Cap is a mechanism by which PJM will procure capacity only if the quantity-
weighted average prices of the accepted pay-as-bid prices are less than $555/MW-
day.  (RBP Proposal, at 63.)  This is a “soft” price cap because it does not limit every 
accepted price to be equal or less than $555/MW-day, but PJM can pay more than 
$555/MW-day for one or more contracts if the prices for other RBP contracts are 
sufficiently low so that the overall average of purchases is less than or equal to 
$555/MW-day. 
12. Although the RBP Proposal couches the Soft Cap as a “willingness to pay” (RBP 
Proposal, at 61–64.), it is not based on any studies of the actual willingness to pay.  
For energy, the willingness to pay is measured in the Value of Loss Load (VOLL).  I

A Zero Must Offer Price Is Inconsistent with Returning PJM to Market

25. The RBP Proposal also has a requirement that the RBP Capacity must offer that 
capacity into the BRAs at a price of zero to ensure that it clears the auctions.  In their 
protest, the NRG Companies propose an offer floor of $175/MW-day (UCAP)—the 
same price as the floor incorporated into the price collar applied in the capacity 
auctions for the 2026/2027, 2027/2028, 2028/2029 and 2029/2030 Delivery Years—
apply to the extent the forecast load prompting the RBP does not materialize.  For 
the reasons discussed below, requiring capacity to be offered into the BRAs at zero 
is antithetical to creating a normal capacity market.  Accordingly, I recommend that 
the Commission adopt alternative measures to protect the buyers and consumers in 
PJM. 
26. A must offer requirement is reasonable given that the purpose of the RBP is to 
procure capacity to serve PJM for 15 years.  (RBP Proposal, 56–57.)  It is also 
reasonable to require that the capacity be offered into PJM and not to be allowed to 
sell RBP Capacity into markets outside of PJM.  (RBP Proposal, at 57.)  Finally, it is 
reasonable that load will receive the benefit of the capacity it will pay for under the 
RBP, if all the capacity procured is truly needed to meet the reliability requirement.  
(RBP Proposal, at 57.)   
27. Markets evolve and 15 years is a long time.  Instead of specifying a zero offer price 
for the full 15 years, contracts could be structured so that any uncleared capacity has 
its strike price set to zero for that BRA and all future BRAs until the end of the contract.

the floor provides greater certainty on cost recovery and hence less risks for the 
owners of new generation.
