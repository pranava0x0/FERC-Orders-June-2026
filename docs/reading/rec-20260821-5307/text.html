PROTEST OF NORTHERN VIRGINIA ELECTRIC COOPERATIVE, INC.
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC” or the “Commission”),1 Northern Virginia Electric Cooperative, Inc. (“NOVEC”) respectfully submits this protest to aspects of the July 31, 2026, filing by PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) to revise its Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) and Reliability Assurance Agreement Among Load Serving Entities (“RAA”) to establish the Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”).2 For the reasons set forth herein, the Commission should reject the RBP as unjust and unreasonable. If the Commission does not reject the RBP, it should require revisions on compliance to eliminate or substantially reduce the nearly $2 billion collateral requirement that the RBP Proposal would impose on NOVEC, and to eliminate the resulting subsidization of the PJM capacity market by NOVEC and a relatively small number of the other Load Serving Entities (“LSEs”) that would be primarily responsible for providing financial security for the contracts that PJM would enter into with RBP Resources.

B. The RBP violates the Commission’s cost-causation principle because it does not recognize the structural causes of the capacity shortfall beyond load growth and improperly assigns all RBP costs based only on projected load growth of large-load customers.

Section 205 requires rates to be just and reasonable.13 That standard incorporates “a ‘cost- causation principle’—the rates charged for electricity should reflect the costs of providing it.”14

The cost-causation principle “add[s] flesh to the[] bare statutory bones” of the just and reasonable standard.15 But while cost allocation is not a matter for the “slide-rule”16 and does not require “exacting precision,”17 the cost-causation principle does require the Commission to have “an articulable and plausible reason” to believe that the benefits are roughly commensurate with the costs.18 A rate that assigns all the costs to a cause only partly responsible for the costs does not satisfy the cost-causation principle because as a “kind of equity,” the principle matches burden with benefit.19

The RBP violates the Commission’s cost-causation principle because it assigns the costs of the RBP Charge and the collateral requirements to one cause without recognition of other significant causes of the capacity shortfall, and because it results in an improper subsidy of PJM’s Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”) auction by RBP buyers for the benefit of other Market Participants that are not RBP buyers.

The RBP process is a one-time mechanism designed to address resource-adequacy challenges arising from the capacity shortfall that occurred in the 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction (“BRA”).20 Ms. Croop explains that the “RBP will function as a safety net to address the shortfall MW from the 2028/2029 BRA that has not been addressed by new load bringing their own new capacity through bilateral agreements or otherwise.”21

C. The RBP improperly concentrates the incremental costs of RBP Resources on RBP buyers while allowing the resulting capacity-market benefits to accrue more

broadly.
PJM’s proposed RBP framework does not adequately account for the market-wide benefits that RBP Resources may provide. RBP buyers would bear the incremental RBP Charges and associated credit obligations attributable to the RBP Resources allocated to them. Yet those resources would participate in the same RPM Auctions that establish capacity prices paid by load throughout the relevant capacity market. To the extent RBP Resources increase available supply and reduce RPM clearing prices, those benefits are not confined to the LSEs assigned RBP cost responsibility. They accrue to other capacity purchasers as well.

The RBP framework requires RBP Resources to offer their committed capacity into the RPM Auctions as price takers at $0. PJM explains that this requirement is appropriate because the RBP Resources are already being paid for by the responsible LSEs and because it ensures that an RBP Resource clears the RPM Auction, thereby avoiding the potential for load to pay twice for the same capacity. NOVEC does not dispute the objective of preventing duplicative payment obligations, but PJM’s solution has an important cost-allocation consequence that its proposal does not adequately address requiring RBP-supported capacity to offer at $0 can affect RPM clearing outcomes for market participants that bear no corresponding share of the incremental RBP costs. The significance of that requirement follows from the operation of RPM. Adding RBP Resources at a mandatory $0 offer increases the quantity of capacity offered at the bottom of the supply curve. Depending on market conditions, locational constraints, and the position of the marginal resource, those offers can reduce the RPM clearing price that otherwise would have resulted. Where that occurs, the resulting reduction in capacity prices benefits capacity purchasers generally, not merely the RBP buyers that financially support the RBP Resources.

PDF page 16

16

The incremental financial obligations associated with those resources, however, remain concentrated on the LSEs assigned RBP cost responsibility. Those LSEs must fund applicable RBP Charges and support those obligations through PJM’s proposed collateral requirements, while other market participants may receive lower capacity prices resulting from the same resources without bearing a corresponding share of those incremental costs.

The RBP’s contract-for-differences settlement structure compounds this mismatch. RBP Resources receive RPM revenues that offset their RBP commitment payments. If the mandatory $0 offers of RBP Resources contribute to a lower RPM clearing price, those resources receive less RPM revenue and the corresponding RBP settlement obligation increases. RBP buyers therefore may bear a larger RBP Charge at the same time that the lower RPM price produces savings for capacity purchasers more broadly. In that circumstance, the same market effect that benefits non- RBP buyers increases the incremental obligation imposed on RBP buyers.

This allocation raises concerns under the Commission’s cost-causation principles. Although cost allocation need not correspond precisely to benefits received, the Commission has required costs to bear a reasonable relationship to the burdens imposed and benefits received and has recognized that substantial departures from that principle can create inappropriate cross- subsidization. PJM has not adequately demonstrated why the incremental costs and substantial credit obligations associated with RBP Resources should be concentrated on designated RBP buyers without accounting for material capacity-market benefits those resources may provide to other PJM load.

At a minimum, the Commission should require PJM to demonstrate that its proposed allocation of RBP costs is roughly commensurate with the benefits attributable to the RBP Resources. If PJM’s mandatory $0 offer requirement produces material market-wide capacity-

price benefits, PJM should explain why those benefits do not warrant broader allocation of at least some portion of the incremental RBP costs or another mechanism that recognizes the value received by non-RBP buyers.

The potential effect of such offers on RPM clearing prices is not merely theoretical. In considering the operation of PJM’s capacity market in another context, the Third Circuit recognized that artificially low capacity offers can “crowd out other capacity that is priced at a higher, cost-based rate” and thereby produce a lower clearing price.40 NOVEC does not contend that RBP Resources are similarly situated to the state-supported resources at issue in New Jersey BPU. The relevant point is narrower: PJM’s requirement that RBP Resources offer at $0 can affect the RPM clearing price, and any resulting price reduction benefits capacity purchasers beyond the LSEs required to bear the incremental costs of supporting those resources.

III. STAKEHOLDER PROCESS

Rather than attempt to salvage the RBP Proposal, the Commission should exercise its authority under NRG to implement the voluntary subscription model the stakeholders approved as part of the CIFP process. PJM’s CIFP process is intended to expedite resolution of issues critical to PJM. As such, while decisions from it are ultimately the Board’s to make, PJM members and other stakeholders play a key advisory role by signaling solutions they agree with through a four-stage proposal-development and voting process. Indeed, members have been criticized in past iterations of the CIFP for not coming to clear consensus. Members sent the Board a clear message in this CIFP by supporting one proposal from more than a dozen

considered. That proposal included an interconnected package of reforms that were carefully balanced to address the issue while maintaining mitigation options for LSEs, all headlined by a version of the RBP auction that would have required LSEs to “subscribe” to obligations, i.e. they had control to mitigate their forecasted growth by committing to curtail it instead of accepting a capacity obligation and resulting costs. NOVEC supported that proposal and still does.

The Board clearly appreciated members’ message by adopting portions of the proposal that it found favorable but rejecting those it did not, including the key tool for LSEs to mitigate their potential obligations in the auction: the subscription-based auction. By pulling out that component of the proposal, PJM effectively stripped LSEs of their control over accepting RBP obligations. In NOVEC’s view, that control was critical to the success of the RBP process, which should embody a bottom-up, rather than a top-down approach. The bottom-up process gives truly interested developers the option to subscribe to pay the RBP costs rather than accept an interruptible form of service which PJM has proposed in Docket No. ER26-3515 (the Interim Resource Adequacy Service or “IRAS”). Contrary to the PJM Board’s determination, the voluntary subscription framework would provide sufficient assurance that capacity equal to the identified near-term shortfall would actually be procured.75 Paired with IRAS, committed load is adequately incentivized to participate to avoid an interruptible service—their business models rely on reliable service.

Although NOVEC rejects the premise that the voluntary subscription process would fail, even if it procured less than the total shortfall, that should not be considered a failure when considered in the context of the package of reforms the stakeholders proposed.
