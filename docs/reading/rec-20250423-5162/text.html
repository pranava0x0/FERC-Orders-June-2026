Executive Summary
The Utility-Data Center “seam” is bearing much of the brunt of tightening regional grid reserve margins and rising customer costs. Mainspring is providing solutions to utilities, large energy users and grid operators to rapidly bring new capacity. Mainspring’s Linear Generator can be configured as a new traditional grid-scale generator, new generators at retail customer sites behind the meter (BTMG), and non-retail BTMG deployed rapidly by public power entities. Other key takeaways:

●​ Upon close review, PJM’s tariff is not just and reasonable because it lacks explicit details on how legacy generation can transition to become BTMG.

●​ PJM’s detailed analysis of Option 3 describes a new class of BTMG. What PJM describes as Option 3 is not currently addressed in the tariff in a coherent fashion. This is a meaningful omission in the Tariff as many large generators are considering transitioning to this configuration to serve large loads.

●​ In addition, we also respectfully urge the Commission to preserve the existing and historic BTMG classes for resources that enter service behind a customer’s meter (primarily under retail jurisdiction).

●​ Next, we also recommend eliminating the cap on Non-Retail BTMG.
●​ We offer detailed recommendations on black start, ancillary service and specifically, reserve cost allocation.

Introduction
Mainspring Energy is actively developing on-site and co-located projects in the PJM region and anticipates operating projects under one or more of the eight “Options” described in PJM’s March 24 Answer. We appreciate the vital work undertaken by the Commission, PJM, states, utilities and other stakeholders to incorporate large loads into the grid quickly while preserving reliability and minimizing cost. We are regularly speaking with customers across the country about the “seam” that requires creative solutions. The high load factor, data center - utility seam, is a major area of focus for us and Mainspring’s Linear Generator was built for this moment.

PJM is up for this challenge. PJM has been a global leader at integrating large loads. However, new co-located generation is likely needed to manage the ramp in new loads over the next 5 to 10 years. As such, we are concerned that PJM’s comments and Tariff do not adequately distinguish the range of planning, markets and operational steps involved in re-purposing legacy, front of the meter PJM generation into behind the meter generation. The reliability challenge is indeed tremendous; PJM’s Board recently observed that, “Taking the anticipated 2025 load forecast into account, the PJM system could see a capacity shortage as soon as the 2026/27 Delivery Year.”1 As Mainspring describes in the enclosed whitepaper, Bring Your Own Power (BYOP), data center availability is low while demand for capacity is incredibly high, including in PJM’s footprint.2 PJM should not overlook the opportunity for help from new retail and non-retail generation to offset the growth in large loads. Data centers can not afford delays in interconnection. As we uncovered in the BYOP whitepaper, many new loads are receiving 3-5 year estimates for grid delivery of power. For a 100 MW data center, three years of delay translates to over $500M in lost revenue. In response to these pressures and the need to protect other captive ratepayers, PJM and its member states should take extra effort to enable all forms of new generation: front of the meter PJM resources, retail resources at customer sites (BTMG), and non-retail BTMG deployed quickly by public power entities. We urge all stakeholders to think constructively about how new, dispatchable behind the meter generation can be expanded within today’s existing paradigms. PJM’s tariff is currently insufficient because it lacks details that explain the detailed process and costs to re-purpose legacy generation into behind the meter generation. The

In 2025, Mainspring plans to construct several data center generation projects, including in a PJM state. We foresee implementing various configurations across these projects and emphasize that the Commission should preserve operational flexibility and opportunities for phased development.

For instance, in Phase 1, we can deliver a power solution within 9 months to serve the data center as a prime power source. In Phase 2, once grid interconnection is feasible, configurations may vary: the linear generators could supplement the grid on a continuous basis (operating as “behind the meter generation” to PJM). Alternatively, in Phase 2 linear generators could be used only to manage peak on-site loads and/or grid peaks via an “interruptible program” (also as “behind the meter generation” to PJM). Or, as a third option in Phase 2, they could serve as full backup in a diesel-free “2N” configuration (again, to PJM this would classify as “behind the meter generation”).6

In Phase 3, as cleaner fuels become more available, our generators can transition to biogas, green hydrogen, ammonia, or fuel blends—without major capital reinvestment. These phases are described in more detail in the enclosed 2024 whitepaper Bring Your Own Power (BYOP), co-authored by Stanford University Fellow, Susanna Kass, and Mainspring CEO Shannon Miller. Another option and phase could involve Mainspring’s units transitioning into PJM front of the meter capacity (such as under PJM Option 1 or 2).

Behind the Meter Generation (BTMG)
Before making specific recommendations, we highlight key attributes of BTMG. First, BTMG is a suitable configuration moving ahead in line with PJM preferences. Grid limitations have led to growing reliance on “bridge to grid” models, where retail customers, in consultation with their local utility, bring their own capacity at least for Phase 1. Once the load customer is connected to PJM’s system, it becomes “network load” subject to the terms of the entity receiving transmission service. Therefore, the BTMG models should satisfy PJM’s stated preference for co-located configurations to retain the load as network load. That said, the concept of “network load” may need to evolve.

Second, reducing customer demand has equivalent if not better results than adding formal PJM-qualified generation capacity. While PJM references “bring your own capacity” (Option 6) as formally qualified resources that add formal MWs on the supply side, adding BTMG reduces the demand on the PJM grid and can be done much faster. In addition, having more generation on the distribution system reduces transmission and distribution losses. Therefore, at Mainspring we think of BTMG as “bringing” new capacity, also.

Two Separate BTMG Classes are in the PJM Tariff and Manuals Today:
Retail and Non-Retail BTMG
“BTMG” refers to customer-sited generation not registered as a wholesale PJM
resource.8 PJM described BTMG in 2004 at a single customer site. Later, “this definition was subsequently expanded to permit inclusion of municipals and EDCs (“Non-Retail”) that use distribution facilities to serve loads, with the consent of the owners or lessee of the distribution facilities”.9 Mainspring agrees with PJM that these are in many important ways, separate constructs. There exists a large body of rules, captured under PJM Manual 14D that pertain only to Non-Retail Behind the Meter Generation.

As such, why does PJM’s Answer often lump these two classes together into its Answer? PJM’s Answer often obscures their unique regulatory and operational characteristics. Any future analyses and reforms should more carefully distinguish between these two forms. Further, it is critical that PJM retain these classes of BTMG as separate from existing PJM generation, such as legacy nuclear units, that transition to become BTMG.

Tariff Language Needed: Repurposed Generation Capacity into BTMG ​

Clearly, repurposing existing assets to become behind the meter generation adds complexity, time and costs. Such steps should be available and transparent. These PJM-identified requirements described in PJM’s Answer under “Existing Option 3 “ should be added to the PJM Tariff given their importance to all market participants. The Commission should require PJM to add this tariff language describing this list of required studies and approaches to allocate study and interconnection upgrade costs appropriately for this class of existing generation repurposed into BTMG.

Recommended Language:
●​ Definition. Repurposed Behind the Meter Generation (BTMG) is generation that is located behind a retail customer meter that was previously a Generation Capacity Resource.

●​ Study Process for Becoming a Repurposed BTMG. The process is multi-staged and includes the following phases10:

○​ Complete Transmission Owner retail load integration study,
○​ Present solutions at the Transmission Expansion Advisory Committee (traditionally, Supplemental Projects),

○​ PJM will perform a “Do No Harm Study” to evaluate how a Supplemental Project will impact the reliability of the Transmission System.

■​ If as a result of the do no harm study, system upgrades are found to be required such upgrades will be considered part of the Supplemental Project and are the responsibility of the Transmission Owner sponsoring the Supplemental Project

■​ Supplemental Projects are cost allocated to the local Transmission Owner’s zone pursuant to individual Transmission Owner cost allocation frameworks,

○​ The generation owner will request a “Necessary Study” to change the interconnection.

■​ PJM will conduct a Necessary Study
■​ Transmission Owners review PJM’s study analysis to verify that analysis and/or perform additional transmission owner analysis pursuant to their processes or requirements

○​ An aggregation of large load additions could trigger regional (as opposed to local) transmission system needs, which would be identified as part of the Regional Transmission Expansion Planning process in Schedule 6 of the Operating Agreement.

■​ That load could rise to regional RTEP needs when determined during the annual load forecasting process in collaboration with LSEs

■​ With the updated load forecast, the RTEP process may incorporate the impact of the transition of the Generation Capacity Resource.

Black Start and Ancillary Services Cost Allocation
Indeed, proper cost allocation is critical. To that point, we also highlight PJM’s assertion on page 36 that all co-located data center configurations “appear to require” grid ancillary services and black start. To PJM’s credit, the RTO acknowledges that this conclusion is uncertain. In our experience, technology matters: linear generators, unlike nuclear units, can provide their own ancillary services and black start capabilities for on-site loads. Most likely, behind the meter generation configurations with nuclear units will desire to leverage grid services differently than those configurations with linear generators.

For a hypothetical situation, imagine a 200 MW data center. The data center is served 100 MW from standard grid power and 100 MW with MW BTMG nuclear generation. Operationally, the data center is sized for 200 MW interconnection to the PJM system but only uses 100 MW at 99% of the time. Charging customers for grid services the 1% of the time needed to supplement the 100 MW co-located generation is appropriate when those services are necessary. In addition, from a planning perspective, the customer has already secured sufficiently sized interconnection to supplement its nuclear generation on-site, so that cost issue has already been addressed.

Reserves Cost Allocation
PJM also erroneously suggests that all load with BTMG requires the use of PJM reserves above a reasonable threshold. In other words, PJM suggests if  the customer is generally receiving 100 MW of service from PJM and 100 MW from behind the meter generation, the customer should actually be paying for more than only 100 MW of reserves. This issue is nuanced and the answer changes with the exact scenario. Configurations could be designed to entirely avoid relying on reserves for the extra 100 MW. For instance, some loads are incredibly flexible and could drop immediately with the loss of on-site generation.

Further, the interconnection could be sized appropriately for this situation. A 200 MW data center with a 100 MW load interconnection (and 100 MW BTMG) is different from a 200 MW data center with a 200 MW load interconnection (also with 100 MW BTMG). In the first case, only 100 MW could ever flow from the grid to the customer. Whereas in the second case, the customer could technically draw upon 200 MW if controls were not in place. 9

Mainspring acknowledges that reforms may be needed to ensure BTMG benefits extend beyond long-term resource adequacy to system reliability needs in real-time. Non-Retail BTMG Cap Should be Lifted
​
Public power entities have a unique opportunity to move quickly to develop to site and build new generation and implement new customer tariffs to address the “seam”. The Non-Retail BTMG model allows public power to operate generation on the distribution system, rather than only behind an individual customer meter. Therefore, we encourage public power entities to leverage the Non-Retail BTMG pathway to introduce new generation quickly to the system.

Mainspring knows first-hand the advantages of public power entities to solve this problem. Mainspring has been contracted by a public power entity to deliver 36 MW of generation on the distribution system to offset new build of a data center. While the project is outside of PJM, many of the same lessons apply. We are eager to bring our experience to the municipal and cooperative utilities in the PJM footprint.

PJM’s Answer emphasizes that innovation may be needed in the Non-Retail BTMG space. Mainspring acknowledges that certain elements of the Non-Retail BTMG framework should also be re-examined such as the fact that these resources “do not carry reserves equal to the installed reserve margin”.12 Any further action should also appreciate the potential for expansion of Non-Retail BTMG, given the tremendous promise of this model to solve the capacity gap quickly. The current limit poses a risk to the grid by limiting the amount of new capacity available to add to the system. The Commission should ask PJM to lift the cap on Non-Retail BTMG above the current 3 GW limit.

Support for Exploring Options 6 and 7
Mainspring strongly supports further exploration of Options 6 and 7, recognizing the potential of each to offer complementary pathways for integrating large loads. However, these are not yet viable alternatives for near-term deployment. BTMG remains the most reliable near-term solution and should be welcomed as “bridge to grid” pathways, capable of maturing into, and working alongside Options 6 or 7.

Option 6 appears best suited to a range of use cases, from baseload or prime power use cases to peaking; Option 7 to intermediate applications; and Option 8 to peaking needs. Detailed Observations
Option 6 – Bring Your Own Capacity (BYOC)
●​ We are intrigued by this model, where customer-owned generation becomes a PJM resource serving both system needs and the load.

●​ However, PJM’s reluctance to create an expedited interconnection path for co-located generation is one reason among others that customers may default to BTMG.

Option 7 – Interruptible Service
●​ Mainspring supports non-capacity backed service models in which loads can self-supply or curtail in exchange for avoided capacity and transmission charges. ●​ PJM should explore whether a new “non-firm” load category is needed in its planning models, as current frameworks do not account for this at the transmission level.

Option 8 – Demand Response with Diesel Gensets
●​ We agree on the value of generation-based demand response but caution that diesel units are unsuitable for long run times. This approach faces regulatory uncertainty and concerns over local air quality impacts.

●​ PJM should instead throw its weight behind preparing for the future. As discussed in the enclosed BYOP whitepaper, “we need to examine if the concept of primary and backup systems is sustainable, in comparison to a resilient system that can operate as both”.

●​ Linear generators are better suited due to lower emissions and longer runtime capabilities, and they allow for larger installations without breaching emissions limits. Mainspring’s Linear generator produces 97% fewer nitrous oxides than a typical diesel generator.

Forecasting Needs
PJM’s load forecasting processes are robust but must evolve to integrate customer flexibility, including the ability to help local utilities and end-use customers to ascertain the financial value of accepting non-firm service. Specifically, PJM in concert with utilities, should allow customers to compare costs and time horizons for powering large loads. Customers should be able to compare a range of wired and on-site generation solutions prior to committing to a final infrastructure build. This has important implications to other rate classes, as well, who may be saddled with the costs of infrastructure for decades into the future. Further, large loads should be equipped to communicate evolving data center build phases and operations to PJM in real time.

Conclusion: Mainspring’s Linear Generator and Key
Recommendations to the Commission
As PJM faces rising loads and tightening reserves, flexible technologies are critical. Mainspring’s Linear Generator offers:

• Rapid Deployment: Modular, scalable, and available now.
• Operational Flexibility: Reliable 24/7 operation with load-following.
• Fuel Agility: Ready for natural gas, hydrogen, biogas, propane, and ammonia.
• Environmental Leadership: Substantial emissions reductions.
• Resilience: Redundant design and multiple fuel pathways.
We urge the Commission to affirm the reliability value of new retail and non-retail behind the meter generation and require PJM to file tariff language that clarifies the rules around “transitioned” or “repurposed” legacy generation into BTMG generation (Option 3). We support PJM’s exploration of Options 6–7. We also encourage states to pursue policies enabling local, dispatchable generation for large loads. These tools will help ensure that the region remains a global leader in digital infrastructure and grid reliability at least cost.
