LIMITED PROTEST OF VISTRA CORP. 
Pursuant to Rule 211 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy 
Regulatory Commission (“Commission”),1 Vistra Corp. (“Vistra”) submits this limited protest of 
PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) proposed Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) 
mechanism.  This proceeding is the latest in PJM’s efforts to address the resource adequacy 
challenges it faces as supply struggles to keep up with forecasted demand.  Vistra generally 
supports the proposal to use a backstop mechanism to address near-term the resource adequacy 
needs in the PJM system, even as Vistra believes it is critically important to ensure that the 
“ordinary” tools for ensuring resource adequacy, including the Reliability Pricing Model (“RPM”), 
remain the principal mechanisms by which PJM meets that critically important goal.  Short-term 
fixes have a tendency to beget further short-term fixes.  Vistra urges PJM—and the Commission—
to ensure that RBP does not follow that well-trod path.      
In that vein, Vistra appreciates PJM’s efforts to narrowly tailor the RBP to the immediate 
resource adequacy need in front of it.  During the stakeholder process, PJM narrowed the 
procurement target from 15 GW to 6.8 GW,2 which brings the procurement amount in line with

I.
LIMITED PROTEST 
Notwithstanding Vistra’s general support of the proposal, Vistra submits this limited 
protest to address certain discrete issues and ensure that the RBP proposal is consistent with cost 
causation principles and is not unduly discriminatory.  PJM explicitly “consent[ed] to the 
Commission’s exercise of authority to modify and/or sever any element of the proposed Tariff and 
RAA language to the extent necessary and permitted under section 205 of the FPA and NRG Power 
Marketing, LLC v. FERC, where such modification and/or severance is consistent with PJM’s 
overarching objectives in this filing.”3
First, PJM’s proposal to allocate RBP costs to all load-serving entities (“LSEs”)—for a 
locked-in fifteen-year period—regardless of whether they contributed to the current resource 
adequacy shortfall is unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory.  PJM proposes to assign 
RBP costs to each Electric Distributor zone based on the zone’s share of the regional MW target, 
then to LSEs within the zone based on a state-determined allocation methodology.  If a state does 
not set a methodology, PJM will default to allocating costs based on a LSE’s peak load

contribution.4  But a LSE’s peak load contribution has no inherent relationship with its contribution 
to the need for the RBP.  While a state may well propose a methodology that addresses Vistra’s 
concerns, there is no requirement that they do so, and PJM’s default methodology is fundamentally 
flawed. 
The just and reasonable standard in the Federal Power Act (“FPA”) requires that costs be 
allocated in a manner that is “roughly commensurate” with benefits.5  As applied here, that 
principle means that the costs of incremental investments needed to ensure reliable service should 
be borne by the parties that cause the need for those investments.6  And the Commission may not 
approve a scheme requiring a party to pay for facilities from which it derives “benefits that are 
trivial in relation to the costs.”7
The resource adequacy shortfall that caused PJM to pursue the RBP is the result of certain 
LSEs agreeing to serve load in excess of their available supply.  It is the cumulative effect of those 
shortfalls that led to PJM’s resource adequacy deficit.  By the same token, LSEs with sufficient 
supply to cover their load obligation did not cause the need for RBP.  Although PJM contends that 
the resource adequacy shortfall is simply the subsequent product of growth among new large

Second, PJM’s proposal is unduly discriminatory because it excludes Fixed Resource 
Requirement (“FRR”) entities from RBP cost allocation based on the very cost causation principle 
described above, while not applying the principle to other entities to which it equally applies.11
PJM proposes to exclude FRR entities principally because they have already paid for sufficient 
capacity to meet their needs, such that “[a]llocating RBP Charges to FRR load would, in essence, 
require that they pay twice for capacity it has already procured.”12  Vistra takes no issue with that 
treatment of FRR entities in isolation.  But, as explained above, the same principle—excluding 
entities that procured sufficient capacity from RBP cost allocation—applies equally to competitive 
retail suppliers that similarly have procured sufficient generation to meet their load needs.  
As a result, it would be textbook undue discrimination to exclude FRR entities from RBP 
because they have already paid for the capacity they need while not excluding competitive retail

Both have secured capacity to cover their load.  Neither entity contributed to the shortfall, so

PJM offers no reasoned basis for treating these entities differently.  Nor can it.  The fact 
that FRR entities opt out of the RPM does not justify the differential treatment.  RBP is designed 
to cure PJM’s resource adequacy shortfall.  The material questions at hand, then, is who caused 
that shortfall and who benefits from additional procurement?  On those questions, FRR entities 
and competitive suppliers with balanced supply and demand books are similarly situated.  The 
mechanism by which each entity accomplished the goal of covering its own capacity needs is 
irrelevant to the question of who should pay for the procurement necessary to cover the shortfall.  
Neither FRR entities nor competitive suppliers with sufficient supply to serve their demand 
contributed to the shortfall, so neither should be required to pay to remedy it. 
To ensure RBP is just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory, the Commission 
should not permit PJM to impose RBP costs on competitive retail suppliers that already have 
balanced books.  This result is also in line with the PJM Governors’ principles that PJM repeatedly 
invokes.  Those principles direct that RBP costs be allocated “to LSEs with new data centers that 
have not self-procured new capacity or agreed to be curtailable,” with “any remaining costs . . .

Finally, Vistra is also concerned with PJM’s fixed approach for allocating RBP costs.  The 
proposed Tariff language provides that each “zone/area’s assigned share of the Final RBP Target 
MW shall remain constant throughout the fixed 15-year term.”15  If there is anything that the last 
five years have shown conclusively in PJM, it’s that load growth can be hard to anticipate in 
advance.  Over the 15 years following the RBP, load will grow more than expected in some places 
and less than expected in others, but the RBP cost allocation will be frozen in time.  That static 
approach all but guarantees that costs will not be allocated in a manner that is roughly 
commensurate with benefits.  Although PJM claims that the 15-year contract term will provide 
financing certainty, it is not clear why the zonal allocation needs to be frozen to accomplish that 
goal.  The principal concern for financiers should be that project costs can be recovered, not the 
ultimate retail customers from which that is derived.   
Neither of these issues requires the Commission to reject RBP altogether.  Rather, the 
Commission can and should require PJM to modify its proposal by allocating RBP costs to LSEs 
based on their net short positions.  PJM explicitly consented to the Commission’s modification of 
the proposed Tariff as permitted under section 205 of the FPA and NRG.  These are discrete issues 
that require necessary modification, consistent with NRG, and preserve the overarching goal of

II.
CONCLUSION 
As this Commission considers PJM’s proposal, it is important not to miss the forest for the 
trees.  RBP is intended to be a one-time, narrowly tailored solution to incentivize new resource 
additions to meet short-term load growth.17  This is not PJM’s first stop-gap measure.  The cascade 
of “one-time” interventions in PJM’s capacity market risks creating a negative feedback loop that 
divorces the market from economic fundamentals, jeopardizing its ability to attract much-needed 
infrastructure investment.  While there are important near-term challenges to address, in the long 
term, price signals based on market fundamentals are critical to ensuring resource adequacy and 
affordability.  Without market signals based on accurate supply and demand dynamics, PJM will 
continue to struggle to incentivize and retain sufficient generation to meet demand.
