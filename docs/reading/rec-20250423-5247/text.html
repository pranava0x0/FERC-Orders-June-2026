BACKGROUND

II. 
COMMENTS 
 
NOVEC appreciates the Commission’s thoughtful approach to the question of co-location 
arrangements and respectfully submits these comments to supplement its protest in Docket No. 
EL25-20-000. NOVEC has no particular objection to co-location arrangements in principle 
provided: (a) that the co-located load obtains and pays for Network Integration Transmission 
Service (“NITS”) or firm Point-to-Point Transmission Service (“PTP Service”); or (b) neither the 
co-located load nor the generator to which it is tied are in any manner connected to the bulk 
electric system, either directly or via interconnection to distribution-level facilities that in turn 
interconnect with the bulk electric system. 
 
To summarize NOVEC’s comments below, NOVEC first addresses the question of 
jurisdiction. Next NOVEC explains why the specific configuration of the co-located load and the 
generator upon which that load is relying are of paramount importance to the Commission’s 
questions. Although there may be a variety of potential configurations, in the end the 
determination of whether co-located load or the supplier to such load should be required to 
obtain and pay for transmission service turns on whether the load and the generation serving it

B. 
PJM’s Existing Tariff is Just and Reasonable 
 
 
Implicit in the Commission’s question as to whether the PJM Tariff is just and reasonable 
is whether the Tariff should permit co-located arrangements in configurations that relieve co-
located load of transmission service costs or remove generation from the capacity and energy 
markets. On the contrary, the PJM Tariff is just and reasonable precisely because it does not 
permit that result. As discussed below, there are three reasons that support NOVEC’s position.

PDF page 9

9 
 
1. Co-located load relies on the transmission system and should pay the 
same costs as other transmission customers unless it and the supplying 
generator are physically islanded. 
 
  
“Fully Isolated Co-Located Load” is not fully isolated if any physical connection to the 
distribution or transmission grid exists with the co-located load or with the generator to which 
the co-located load is connected. If there is any physical tie at all, even if there is switchgear in 
place to isolate it, the co-location arrangement benefits from and relies upon the transmission 
system. The benefits of, and the need for, the transmission system is the very reason co-located 
loads seek to maintain that connection to the transmission system and do not island themselves.22 
 
With respect to large generation assets interconnected to the grid, regardless of whether 
the unit is nuclear, fossil-fueled, or renewable, the grid necessarily responds to all power-quality 
issues caused by the generator or any load behind the generator, including but not limited to 
frequency response, reactive needs, transient issues, and voltage control.  Co-located load is akin 
to parasitic load because the transmission grid cannot differentiate between energy that is 
injected into the market and energy that is injected to serve co-located load. Additionally, nuclear 
powered generation units are not operated as flexible or partially loaded assets but instead 
operate at full load between fueling cycles to maximize the value of their fuel rods. As such, 
nuclear powered generation assets depend upon other generators on the grid to provide all 
ancillary service and other power-quality needs.  
The reliance is clear from the proposed configurations. As the Indicated PJM 
Transmission Owners (“Indicated PJM TOs”) explained in their answer to the Commission’s

Moreover, in a recently submitted co-location arrangement and in the arrangements 
proposed and discussed in the Complaint, the co-located load depended upon 
another nuclear unit at the same facility for its backup supply of real power—which 
is to say, a PJM capacity resource that is interconnected with, serves, and relies 
entirely upon the grid. The proposed backup arrangement, including any 
“replacement capacity” mechanism, depends upon the delivery of energy from 
other locations on the network through PJM’s transmission system.27 
 
 
Co-location arrangements indisputably rely on the transmission system and its benefits. 
And, as the Indicated PJM TOs point out, all load, including co-located load, is used to plan the 
transmission system under the PJM Tariff.28 Co-location proposals that exempt co-located load 
from the costs of transmission service effectively give them free use of the transmission system 
and cause other load to subsidize that use. This is inconsistent with the Commission’s cost-
causation principle; the Commission should not approve free-ridership. The existing PJM Tariff, 
which requires co-located load to procure transmission service, is just and reasonable because it 
matches the cost of the transmission system with the co-location arrangements’ benefit from and 
use of the transmission system. The Commission should not order changes to the PJM Tariff, or 
if it does to clarify the provision of co-location services, it should still require co-located load to 
acquire firm transmission service. 
2. Certain of the proposed co-location arrangements have involved the 
removal of significant generation from PJM’s markets, which would 
cause an unreasonable and unsustainable increase in costs for customers 
even as the load causing those cost increases benefits from the markets’ 
planning functions.

generation to co-located load. The Commission should not permit any co-location paradigm that

customers’ investments and risk-bearing that helped make those plants now attractive to load that

D. 
If the Commission is Going to Incorporate Any National Security Concerns Into 
Its Analysis, It Needs to Explicitly Define National Security and Consider 
Countervailing National Security Interests 
 
 
The Commission encouraged “parties to address any national security implications 
associated with co-location arrangements, including with respect to the specific issues discussed 
in this show cause order, and what actions, if any, the Commission should take in response.”55 
NOVEC has several observations to offer. As a concept, “national security” has not been well 
defined in this proceeding. As Constellation used it in its complaint, national security is 
implicated because the failure to speedily adopt co-located loads will delay the development of 
artificial intelligence (“AI”) because data center generation will not be able to interconnect fast 
enough otherwise. But national security in the general sense is broader than AI: other industries 
are vital to national security — in particular, large industrial concerns. The price increases that 
co-located load could cause have the potential to harm their interests. Could that constitute a 
countervailing national security interest? One would think. One would also wonder about the 
existing data centers: they serve a vital function — does a cost increase to their operation harm 
their long-term viability and threaten national security? In an even broader sense, does the 
increase in costs to customers harm the general prosperity of the United States? If the 
Commission chooses to consider national security, it should consider it more systematically. 
Moreover, the Federal Power Act, in the context of sections 205 and 206, casts the 
Commission’s role as the arbiter of what is “just and reasonable.” Any consideration the 
Commission gives to national security must be as a component of a just and reasonable rate, and 
it cannot be a prime mover for the Commission in assessing rates.
