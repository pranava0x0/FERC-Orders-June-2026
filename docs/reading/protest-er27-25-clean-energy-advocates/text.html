I. Introduction
As the Commission has recognized, integrated planning processes can help ensure that generation and transmission can be arranged to supply nearby large customers reliably, at lower cost, and with faster engineering study processes, ultimately resulting in more just and reasonable rates without harming other existing or future customers or suppliers. Better load and generation interconnection processes are clearly needed. MISO’s LARS proposal does not achieve that end, nor does it improve other interconnection processes. Instead of improving the coordination of generation and transmission planning, MISO’s LARS proposal is a new, permanent parallel interconnection study process that will shift costs from large loads to other existing and future transmission customers and resource owners, create and worsen reliability concerns, and allow for unchecked and harmful anti-competitive discrimination. The Commission should reject LARS because it is not just and reasonable and is unduly discriminatory or, in the alternative, issue a deficiency letter requiring MISO to demonstrate how LARS can be made just and reasonable.

D. The Stakeholder Process
MISO developed LARS in eleven weeks. It first presented the concept to its Large Load Working Group (“LLWG”) on July 16, 2026.55 After presenting revisions at different meetings, MISO presented final language on September 23 and filed on October 2.56

Stakeholders raised, and MISO declined to address, several of the concerns presented in this protest. The Environmental Sector objected to the Transmission Customer’s gatekeeping role, to the LRZ-wide scope of the pairing requirement, and to LARS’s failure to meet the Show Cause Order’s electrically proximate standard, and asked that generators and loads be jointly liable for upgrade costs if a pairing ends early. Energy Futures Group presented the Generator and Load Interconnection Process (“GALIP”), which would study large loads and their generation together within the DPP, with full cost-causation-based allocation of upgrade costs and both firm and non-firm service options for load.57 MISO declined to pursue GALIP, stating that it “does not believe in a ‘one size fits all’ approach to interconnection” and that GALIP “aimed to shoehorn a parallel generation/load study process into the existing DPP process.”58

III. Arguments
A. LARS creates a cost shift from large loads and their generation to other
customers
It is a long-standing principle of Commission ratemaking that “all approved rates reflect to some degree the costs actually caused by the customer who must pay them,” and that compliance is measured “by comparing the costs assessed against a party to the burdens imposed or benefits drawn by that party.”59 In the Show Cause Order, the Commission preliminarily found that MISO’s Tariff violates that principle because it “lacks adequate mechanisms to mitigate the risk of cost shifting among transmission customers” when large loads drive network upgrades.60 LARS does not supply those mechanisms. By MISO’s own account, LARS adopts no cost allocation methodology, and “[t]he cost shifting issues raised in the Commission’s Show Cause Order will be addressed in future filings.”61

Instead, LARS builds cost shifting into the process in four ways. First, it assigns the network upgrades that a generator-load pair requires to the transmission pricing zone rather than to the pair. Second, it studies LARS generators in models that exclude the DPP queue, and resolves the resulting double-allocation of transmission capacity through MTEP projects paid for by zonal load and through curtailment of DPP generators that paid for their own upgrades. Third, it allows either half of a pair to leave the system after fifteen years and the generator to fail at any time, leaving zonal load to pay for whatever transmission must then be built. Fourth, it defers the cost recovery agreement, financial security, and transparency measures the Commission identified as necessary. The Commission should reject LARS or, at a minimum, decline to make

1. LARS assigns upgrade costs to the pricing zone
As explained above, LARS sorts network upgrades identified in its load and generation studies into three categories: violations in only the load study, violations in only the generation study, and violations in both studies. Violations that occur only in the load study or in both the load and generation studies are assigned the BRP or Other project types and their costs are allocated to all loads in the Transmission Owner’s pricing zone. Only when a violation occurs in the generation study alone will MISO assign the costs of the mitigations to the Interconnection Customer as a GIP upgrade.62 MISO calls this a “cost allocation hierarchy—transitioning from GIP to BRP when appropriate.”63 This hierarchy represents impermissible cost shifts. When the costs of mitigation are assigned to BRP or Other project types, those costs are allocated to the Transmission Owner in whose pricing zone the project is located. Those costs are then recovered through the Transmission Owner’s rates, paid for by every transmission customer in the zone. MISO explains that the costs will fall on “the load within the transmission pricing zone where the transmission upgrade is required.”64 In contrast, when the same generator interconnects through the DPP, the Interconnection Customer funds the Network Upgrades its interconnection requires. 2. LARS shifts costs to interconnection customers in the DPP
LARS shifts costs to interconnection customers in the DPP, to the disadvantage of generators in the DPP compared to those related to LARS projects. In the DPP, MISO models the system headroom by assuming that all higher-queued generating facilities will enter operation.78 By contrast, in LARS, MISO proposes to model only DPP projects with a signed GIA.79 These differing approaches risk MISO allocating transmission headroom twice.80 Rather than reconcile the double-allocation, MISO proposes that “[b]oth [projects] will be allowed to proceed, and neither will be required to pay additional costs due to the overallocation.”81 Failing to coordinate DPP and LARS shifts costs onto DPP customers.

LARS generators are studied as though the DPP queue does not exist. They are assigned none of the upgrades that their injections require in combination with the queued projects ahead of them. Those upgrades are left to the DPP customers, who are assigned them on the assumption that the headroom in their models would still exist when they reach commercial operation. When LARS and DPP generators both come online and the system cannot deliver to both, the result is a reliability gap. E. LARS threatens reliability and efficient planning
LARS would be at least the fifth separate path onto the MISO transmission system, alongside the DPP, ERAS, the pending Zero-Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement (“ZGIA”) process, and EPR for load. A sixth, the Large Load Project Review (“LLPR”) is currently under development.150 MISO’s addition of another parallel interconnection process takes the region further away from efficient transmission planning. MISO’s statement that any constraints that are not addressed in the LARS study due to overallocation would be reconciled in the MTEP process does not promise that these solutions would be minimal upgrades.151 It promises only that they would be reconciled through the MTEP process. There is a risk under MISO’s current proposal that unresolved reliability contingencies identified in a subsequent MTEP process could require mitigation through backbone upgrades or other high-cost solutions resulting from the cumulative impacts of LARS and other interconnection requests, the costs of which non-LARS transmission customers would ultimately have to pay. Due to the permanent nature of MISO’s LARS proposal, there is a risk that non-LARS transmission customers could be permanently disadvantaged by the process and bear the costs of transmission investments needed to resolve issues caused, at least in part, by a LARS request, or series of interconnection requests. Each new path to interconnection takes MISO further from efficient planning.

1. Parallel independent studies produce unreliable results and reliability risks Interconnection studies assign network upgrades on the basis of cumulative impacts. By putting some generators in one model and other generators in another, MISO cannot capture those impacts. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation’s (“NERC”) model validation guidance states that future-year planning cases “should contain the same component representations as the most recently validated model, unless there is a specific reason for the data to be different,” and should represent “the cumulative planned changes to the system” through the period studied.152 By excluding the DPP, LARS does the opposite of NERC’s procedures. The consequences of this mutual blindness of the models are not limited to the cost shifts described above.
