COMMENTS OF OLABANJI JOHN OGUNYEMI
Olabanji John Ogunyemi submits these comments in the above-captioned proceeding. On June 18, 2026, the Commission directed PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (PJM) and the PJM Transmission Owners to show cause why PJM's Open Access Transmission Tariff remains just and reasonable without provisions addressing (a) the application process, study procedures and operational requirements for transmission service on behalf of large loads; (b) transparency of Network Upgrade costs and a pro forma cost recovery agreement; (c) transmission services for flexible large loads; and (d) the rates, terms and conditions of service applicable to interconnection customers serving electrically proximate large load or co-located load.  PJM Interconnection, L.L.C., 195 FERC ¶ 61,211, at P 3 (2026) (Show Cause Order). The Commission also directed PJM to report on how it intends to ensure that adequate generation will be available to serve existing and new large loads. Id. P 4.

These comments address one question that runs through each of those issues: whether the generation built to serve large loads can be financed. My comments are offered from the perspective of the lenders, tax equity investors and tax credit buyers whose capital that generation depends on.

I. INTEREST OF THE COMMENTER
I am a project finance professional working on solar and battery storage projects in the United States. My work includes sizing construction and term debt for solar projects, preparing the documentation lenders and tax credit buyers require before they commit capital, and managing operating solar assets and the reporting their lenders rely on. My experience is with distribution- 1

II. SUMMARY
Much of the new generation that will serve large loads, including data centers, will be solar and battery storage, because those resources can be built fastest. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reports that developers planned to add 43.4 GW of utility-scale solar and 24 GW of battery storage in 2026, out of 86 GW of planned additions.[1] Whether that generation is built depends in large part on whether it can be financed. Capital providers commit only against terms they can underwrite: a known path to interconnection and service, a cost exposure they can quantify, and a schedule that fits the project's other deadlines. I support reforms that give large loads, and the generation serving them, those three things, and I urge the Commission to direct PJM to set them out in its Tariff.

III. COMMENTS
A. Study procedures should have defined timelines, because federal law now fixes a completion deadline for solar (Show Cause Order P 3(a)).

Public Law 119-21 ends the clean electricity investment and production credits for wind and solar facilities placed in service after December 31, 2027, where construction begins after July 4, 2026.[2] For a solar project financed against the credit, a delay in interconnection or service studies is no longer only a delay. It can cost the project its credit, and with it a large part of the capital that makes the project financeable. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory reports that the median project built in 2025 took 61 months from interconnection request to commercial operation, and that only about 13 percent of the capacity that requested interconnection from 2000 to 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025.[3] The Commission has already accepted, in Southwest Power Pool's tariff, a process it found "reasonably provides a flexible, 2

expedited,  and  separate  serial  interconnection  process  that  will  facilitate  the  prompt interconnection of generating facilities that are limited to serving" a high impact large load.[4] I encourage the Commission to require that the procedures PJM adopts for large loads, and for the generation serving them, include defined study timelines of that kind, so that projects can plan against the statutory deadline with confidence.

B. Cost transparency should extend to estimates that capital providers can rely on (Show Cause Order P 3(b)).

I support the Commission's focus on transparency of Network Upgrade costs and on a pro forma cost recovery agreement to mitigate cost shifting. I would add that, for financing purposes, the value of transparency depends on whether cost estimates are firm enough to be relied on. Debt is sized against a project's expected costs and cash flows; an upgrade estimate that can change materially after financial close is a risk lenders price conservatively or refuse to take. Research on interconnection costs has found that projects that withdrew from the queue faced substantially higher estimated interconnection costs than those that completed the study process.[5] A pro forma agreement with estimates that are binding, or bounded within a stated range, would let capital providers size their commitments with confidence while still protecting other customers from cost shifts.

C. Clear services for flexible large loads would support storage financed alongside them (Show Cause Order P 3(c)).

Battery storage is particularly relevant to transmission services for flexible large loads. Under Public Law 119-21, the termination that applies to wind and solar does not apply to energy storage technology,[6] so storage will remain financeable against the federal credit after 2027. Storage paired with a large load can let that load limit its use of the transmission system under the conditions the Commission describes. Clear rules defining how such a flexible load is served, and how its storage is treated, would let that storage be financed on firmer ground and built where it can do the most for reliability.

D. Terms for generation serving electrically proximate or co-located load should be set out in the Tariff (Show Cause Order P 3(d)).

From a financing perspective, the most important feature of the rates, terms and conditions for interconnection customers serving electrically proximate or co-located load is that they are defined in advance. A construction lender sizes its loan, sets its draw conditions and fixes its conversion tests against the project's expected interconnection and service arrangements. Where those arrangements depend on negotiations whose outcome cannot be known at financial close, the lender either discounts the project's value, requires additional equity or reserves, or declines to lend. Defined Tariff terms would allow generation serving these loads to be underwritten on the same basis as other generation, and would reduce the number of projects that stall before financial close.

E. PJM's assessment of adequate generation should reflect delivered output (Show Cause Order P 4).

In reporting how it will ensure adequate generation to serve large loads, PJM should account for the output that generation actually delivers, not only its nameplate capacity. Operating solar plants do not always deliver their expected output. A National Renewable Energy Laboratory study of 8.5 GW of U.S. photovoltaic systems found median availability of 0.99 but tenth- percentile availability of 0.95, meaning a tenth of systems lost at least five percent of production to unavailability alone.[7] Accounting for delivered output would give a more accurate picture of the generation available to large loads, and would reward owners who keep their plants performing.

IV. CONCLUSION
New solar and storage generation can help meet the demand of large loads quickly, but only if it can be financed. I respectfully urge the Commission to direct PJM to adopt Tariff provisions that give large loads and the generation serving them (i) study procedures with defined timelines,

(ii) cost estimates that capital providers can rely on, (iii) clear services for flexible large loads paired with storage, and (iv) defined rates, terms and conditions for interconnection customers 4
