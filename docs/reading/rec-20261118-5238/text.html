Limitations LARS Places On Eligible Agreements And Co-Location

Co-located load and generation supported by a long-term PPA or similar arrangement are similarly situated to electrically proximate pairs in every respect relevant to LARS: both pairings are based on a binding agreement between the two entities, are located in the same local resource zone, are studied together for their combined system impacts, and are intended to bring new generation online to serve a specific load.15  Indeed, co-location arrangements are better situated to achieve the goals of LARS because they are less likely to trigger transmission upgrades relative to generation that is merely “electrically proximate” to the load. B. MISO’s Answer Fails To Meaningfully Address The Unduly Discriminatory
Components Of LARS

Under the LARS process, a generation resource may enter into an agreement to serve a large load and otherwise meet all the criteria to participate in LARS but will nevertheless be ineligible unless a transmission customer—which MISO concedes will often be an LSE—agrees to submit a LARS application on its behalf.18  Moreover, this same generation resource and large load pairing will be ineligible for the LARS process unless the transmission owner of the system they are interconnecting to agrees to a proposed point of interconnection.19  These components of LARS effectively give incumbent utilities, in the form of LSEs and transmission owners, the ability to prevent otherwise viable generation and load pairings from accessing LARS. Although MISO says that it disagrees with commenters’ characterization of the LARS process as placing LSEs in a gatekeeper role,20 it does not offer any arguments or evidence to rebut commenters’ characterization. To begin with, MISO’s argument on this point is circular.  To the extent that LARS is a load-driven process, that is only because MISO designed it as such.  It does not, however, absolve MISO of its obligation to demonstrate that the process it has designed is consistent with the Federal Power Act’s (“FPA”) prohibition on undue discrimination.  Indeed, if MISO’s rationale were to hold, public utilities would be permitted to adopt any unduly discriminatory interconnection policies so long as the design of the policies was intended to accomplish the utilities’ stated goals. MISO similarly dismisses the fact that Southwest Power Pool, Inc.’s (“SPP”) High Impact Large Load Generation Assessment (“HILLGA”) process provides generation resources with a non-discriminatory pathway to interconnect and serve electrically proximate large loads on the grounds that it is a “generator-driven process.”22  But MISO’s claim that LARS is a load-driven process ignores that LARS is not simply a load study; it is also a generator interconnection process that results in a resource obtaining a Large Load Generator Interconnection Agreement (“LLGIA”) and Network Resource Interconnection Service.23  Indeed, the key components of LARS are focused on the interconnection of a generation resource and the interconnection customer’s resulting interconnection rights. MISO’s defense of the requirement that the transmission owner pre-approve a project’s point of interconnection before a LARS application may be submitted is equally unsupported. MISO states that LARS “has stricter site control requirements such that the Transmission Owner must agree on the POI at the time of application submission” to ensure that only shovel-ready projects participate.27  But site control and a transmission owner veto are different things.  An interconnection customer can demonstrate site control and identify a point of interconnection without having to await the interconnecting transmission owner’s approval.  Indeed, in the generator interconnection context, no transmission owner consent to a point of interconnection is required, yet evidence of site control is still provided.28  And requiring transmission owner approval also does not provide meaningful protection against the risk that technical issues could arise that could lead the transmission owner or MISO to request a change in the point of interconnection during the study process.29

MISO also does not demonstrate that it is just and reasonable to require interconnection customers to bear the risk of a transmission owner-initiated change to the point of interconnection that occurs during the study process.
