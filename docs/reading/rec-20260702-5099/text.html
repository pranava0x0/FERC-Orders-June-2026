I. Introduction
Persistence Analytics Group LLC (PAG) respectfully submits these supplemental comments in Docket No.
AD26-7-000 regarding PJM governance and stakeholder reforms. PAG supports the Commission's focus
on concrete, actionable reforms that improve PJM's ability to address operational and market needs in a
timely and efficient manner.
PAG's work focuses on national security and infrastructure risk analytics, including demand durability, grid
stress, load integrity, public-cost exposure, and implementation-risk verification. The central issue
addressed in these comments is whether PJM's governance and stakeholder processes can distinguish
durable, decision-relevant infrastructure needs from incomplete, speculative, weakly supported, or
insufficiently verified assumptions before costs, procurement, transmission planning, interconnection
activity, or public obligations harden.
PJM governance reform should not be treated only as a process-design exercise. Governance determines
what evidence is required, who can require it, when decisions must move, when delay becomes harmful,
and who bears the downside if assumptions fail. For large loads, AI and data center development,
transmission expansion, onsite generation, interconnection timing, resource adequacy, and cost
allocation, the governance question is also an assumption-verification question.
II. Summary of Recommendations
1. Adopt a pre-NTP assumption-verification screen for high-impact load and infrastructure
commitments. Before Notice to Proceed (NTP), PJM and relevant stakeholders should have a structured
way to evaluate whether demand, power deliverability, interconnection timing, contractual support, cost
allocation, and execution readiness are sufficiently verified.
2. Establish minimum evidence standards for large-load and project-readiness signals. PJM
governance should identify what counts as a legitimate, actionable signal for planning and market
purposes, and what remains staged, conditional, speculative, or insufficiently supported.
3. Create fast-path or time-bound review procedures for critical operational and market issues.
Where delay increases reliability risk, public-cost exposure, or market uncertainty, PJM should have
defined procedures to escalate, review, and resolve issues within an identified timeframe.
4. Improve transparency around assumptions, dependencies, and downside ownership.
Stakeholders, states, and the public should be able to understand which assumptions drive decisions,
which dependencies must hold, what evidence supports them, and who bears consequences if they fail.
5. Develop a durable-load and public-cost exposure framework. PJM should distinguish between
announced load, contracted load, financially supported load, interconnection-ready load, and load that is
durable enough to justify infrastructure obligations.
III. Why Pre-NTP Verification Matters
The phrase 'prior to NTP' is critical. Once a project reaches NTP, the capital stack, EPC schedule,
procurement chain, utility planning assumptions, onsite generation commitments, transmission
expectations, and public-cost exposure may already be moving. At that stage, decision-makers may be
managing consequences rather than preventing avoidable risk.
For large-load and data center-related infrastructure, the following questions should be answered before
commitments harden:

VI. Governance Reform Should Identify Assumption Ownership
A recurring governance failure in infrastructure decision-making is not merely that assumptions are wrong.
It is that no one is clearly responsible for naming, testing, updating, or retiring them before they become
embedded in planning and cost recovery. PJM reform should therefore include assumption ownership.
For each high-impact operational or market decision, PJM should identify:
• the assumption being relied upon;
• the source and quality of evidence supporting it;
• the decision that depends on it;
• the timeline on which the assumption must remain true;
• the party or process responsible for updating the record if facts change;
• the consequences if the assumption proves inaccurate; and
• the trigger for reconsideration, escalation, or fast-path review.
This is particularly important where large loads, generation additions, transmission needs, and
interconnection constraints are moving faster than traditional stakeholder processes can comfortably
absorb.

VII. Transparency Should Include Evidence Quality, Not Merely Process
Participation
Transparency reforms should go beyond who voted, who objected, and what committee considered the
issue. Those elements are important, but they do not reveal whether the underlying assumptions were
decision-grade.
For high-impact issues, transparency should include a public or appropriately summarized evidence map
that identifies: the claimed need, the supporting evidence, unresolved dependencies, cost exposure,
timing risk, public impact, and the consequence of inaction or delay. Where sensitive information cannot
be public, PJM should still provide a non-confidential summary sufficient to support governance
accountability.
VIII. Fast-Path Review Should Be Triggered by Risk, Not Stakeholder
Convenience
The Commission's agenda asks whether PJM should increase the use of fast-path or time-bound review
procedures for high-importance topics. PAG supports such reforms. Fast-path review should be available
where delay itself creates material operational, reliability, market, or public-cost risk.
Potential triggers include: material reliability exposure, significant public-cost or ratepayer exposure,
large-load uncertainty affecting transmission or capacity planning, interconnection or equipment
bottlenecks with regional consequences, major fuel or power-delivery dependencies, or evidence that
stakeholder process delay is preventing timely action on an urgent operational or market issue.
Fast-path review should not eliminate stakeholder input. It should structure that input around evidence,
defined timelines, written assumptions, and decision accountability.
IX. Conclusion
PJM's governance challenge is not only whether its stakeholder process is inclusive or whether its Board
has sufficient formal authority. It is whether the regional decision system can verify material assumptions
quickly enough to prevent avoidable reliability, market, infrastructure, and public-cost consequences.
The Commission should encourage reforms that make PJM faster, more accountable, and more
evidence-driven. A pre-NTP assumption-verification screen, minimum evidence standards for large-load
signals, transparent assumption ownership, and fast-path review for high-risk issues would improve PJM's
ability to meet immediate and future challenges while preserving appropriate stakeholder participation.
Markets price the headline. Infrastructure proves whether it can hold. PJM governance reform should help
ensure that the assumptions behind infrastructure decisions are verified before obligations harden.
