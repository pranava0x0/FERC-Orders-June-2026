In Re: Docket No. AD26-7-000 PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms,

The Center for Progressive Reform (the Center) submits the following comments related to Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC) request for stakeholder comments following the Commission-led technical conference on PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms held on July 23, 2026. The Center thanks the Commission for initiating the technical conference, and for the opportunity to submit these comments.

The Center is a non-profit research and advocacy organization that focuses on advancing responsive governance, climate justice, and ensuring public protections from environmental harms. The Center is not a PJM Member.
Although PJM is not a governmental organization, its outsized impact and influence on the planning for and operation of the electric grid and wholesale energy markets across all of part of 13 states and the District of Columbia necessitates that its operation is transparent, accessible, and ultimately responsive to the approximately 67 million people who reside in its footprint. 
PJM’s governance structure is not, currently, responsive to this population. At the outset of these comments, the Center would like to note that PJM’s staff have conducted a major feat in load balancing and ensuring system reliability in the face of increasingly challenging grid conditions – most recently this July when the system broke all time peak load records (factoring in demand response). While this was a laudatory achievement for PJM’s staff, fundamentally, the thin margins of maintaining reliability in which they operated represent a failure in governance to adequately respond to emerging developments in supply, demand, and transmission. Although PJM is to be commended for beginning to take steps to address the myriad issues that have led to both a burgeoning reliability crisis and affordability crisis within its footprint, reform is required to ensure that PJM can more flexibly respond to the challenges it faces. Further, such reforms are required for PJM to be more responsive to its stakeholders – both states who have had their generation-mix planning frustrated in the past decade, as well as the broader public who relies on the electric grid. 

The Center accordingly submits the following select responses to FERC’s questionnaire on governance reform.
Should the Board Members’ terms be extended to five or more years?
NO. Increased term length would reduce accountability and degrade flexibility.
Should the conflict-of-interest rules be revised to reduce the cooling off period for Board members from two years to one year
NO. PJM’s governance structure already substantially weighs in favor of the market power and ability to achieve regulatory capture by incumbent generators and transmission owners. To reduce conflict of interest rules at this juncture would only exacerbate this existing issue in PJM that stymies responsive governance.
In order to increase transparency, should Board votes be made public or the Board be required to explain its reasoning?
YES. As the board’s decision making has outsized impact on the reliability and affordability of electricity in PJM’s footprint, it is important that basic transparency as to how the board votes, and its rationale for action is publicly reviewable. This is a basic tenet of transparency.
In order to increase transparency, should some Board meetings be open or available to the public?
YES. As discussed above, this is a basic tenet of transparency in evaluating the board’s conduct and decision-making.
Should PJM’s governing documents be revised to explicitly state that the responsibility of PJM and its Board is to serve the public interest?
YES. Under the current structure, this is not required. PJM, as discussed, is responsible for an electric grid and electric markets that serve approximately 67 million people in the United States. Historically, PJM decision making does not always serve the interest of this population, but rather by members – generation owners, transmission owners, and utilities who largely operate on a for-profit basis and who benefit from decision making that is subject to advancing said profits which is often at odds with the public good. A requirement to serve the public interest is a sine qua non of ensuring that PJM can be responsive to the needs of the population in its footprint.
Should State Representatives have at least one voting member on the Nominating Committee?
YES. Providing states with the a voice in the nominating committee is crucial to ensure that state representatives have a means to ensure that the populations that they represent have at least some say in the nomination of board members who have an outsized impact on the electric grid they rely upon.
Should the Organization of PJM States inc. (OPSI) have “jump ball” rights over resource adequacy and transmission cost allocation filings when a supermajority of OPSI agrees on an alternate proposal?
YES. This ensures that the states served by PJM have an adequate role in ensuring that PJM is responsive to the needs and priorities of states in PJM’s footprint.
Should the voting rules barring affiliate votes, which apply to senior standing committees, also apply to lower-level committees?
YES. Lower-level committees inform policy changes considered by the senior committees and ultimately the board. Numerous PJM members have multiple (often in the teens) affiliates. Allowing affiliate level voting at lower levels allows for disproportionate influence by these members and biases PJM governance to their priorities.
In addition to OPSI, should there be a second state representative group consisting of governor’s representatives and/or state legislator’s representatives?
YES. The states in PJM have substantial responsibilities to their residents, and inclusion of such a group would improve PJM’s uptake and consideration of state public policy determinations in grid planning and operation that is sorely wanting.

Thank you for the opportunity to submit these comments.
