PRE-FILED STATEMENT OF SUSAN BRUCE, COUNSEL TO  
PJM INDUSTRIAL CUSTOMER COALITION 
 
 
On behalf of PJM Industrial Customer Coalition (PJMICC), I am privileged to offer this 
Pre-Filed Statement on PJM Governance and Stakeholder Reforms.  As discussed in more detail 
below, PJMICC is an ad-hoc coalition of a broad cross-section of energy-intensive manufacturers 
(e.g., steel, cement, consumer products, pharmaceuticals, and paper) and institutional customers 
(e.g., hospitals and universities) doing business in PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”).  PJMICC 
members represent major employers in the PJM region and support the regional and national 
economy.  Thank you for the opportunity to address the Commission on these important issues. 
 
On behalf of PJMICC, my colleagues at McNees and I have directly participated in the 
PJM stakeholder process for more than 25 years and have witnessed the evolution of PJM’s 
governance, including both formal processes and informal practices.  PJMICC members' 
businesses depend on a reliable energy supply, competitive energy costs, and options to manage 
their energy cost exposure through various demand response tools.  For that reason, members of 
PJMICC were the original participants of the PJM End-Use Customer Sector because they 
recognized that, for energy-intensive manufacturers and institutional customers, wholesale energy 
costs account for the majority of their retail electric costs.  Reliable, competitive pricing is essential 
to PJMICC members' ability to remain economically viable in the PJM region, especially as other 
areas of the country, if not the world, use energy as an economic development tool.  
 
For context on PJMICC’s interests in the PJM stakeholder process, PJMICC engages in the 
PJM stakeholder process in an effort to support “just and reasonable rates” by addressing:      
 
 Concerns regarding PJM administrative interventions that affect energy prices; 
 Approaches to ensure system reliability at the lowest possible reasonable cost;  
 The importance of following the principles of cost causation; 
 The need to address the potential for market power exercise; 
 The value of independent planning; and 
 Demand response as a tool for energy-intensive customers to manage their energy costs.

PJMICC has strongly supported PJM, even when it may have disagreed with aspects of PJM 
markets, operations, and planning, because of the value that PJMICC members derive from a broad 
and diverse market with shared reserves.   
 
In this Pre-Filed Statement, I set forth PJMICC’s view on the governance challenges within 
PJM and offer five targeted reforms: retail-impact accountability in PJM’s mission and decision-
making, enhanced Board independence and transparency, lower-committee voting discipline, 
CBIR process discipline, and more useful tools for time-sensitive issues. 
 
I. 
Background 
 
While PJM's markets, operations, and planning function at the wholesale level, PJM’s 
governance structure must appropriately recognize that end-use customers are the ultimate 
beneficiaries of the energy, transmission, and reliability services offered within the PJM region.  
Retail customers bear the ultimate cost of the construct, either directly or indirectly. Industrial and 
commercial end-use customers represent roughly half of all market activity in the PJM region and 
have invested billions of dollars in the region in reliance on stable, well-functioning markets. 
Electricity is an essential service marked by endemic market power concerns, which is precisely 
why a sound, balanced governance structure matters. 
 
Let me state PJMICC’s overarching position plainly: we support fundamental aspects of 
PJM’s existing governance structure, especially its sector-weighted voting foundation.  PJM’s 
framers instituted important checks and balances that, when properly implemented, strive toward 
consensus-building and compromise and, when necessary, independent PJM Board action. 
Widespread stakeholder support for PJM’s market rules—including by retail customers—
minimizes litigation before this Commission and the courts, builds market confidence and stability, 
and supports the prospect for legally durable outcomes.   
 
That is not to suggest that the PJM governance structure and stakeholder process are 
perfect; they are not.  Important reforms are necessary to improve the effectiveness of PJM 
governance and the stakeholder process itself, which has become unduly time- and resource-
intensive without yielding commensurate benefits.  However, constructive reform of the existing 
framework must maintain the benefits of a broad regional market and a liquid, functional energy 
market.  From the vantage point of PJMICC, it is important to focus first on reforms that improve 
confidence in PJM’s independence and accountability: both public confidence in PJM market 
outcomes and investor confidence in the value of investing in the PJM footprint, whether in terms 
of necessary resource infrastructure or energy-intensive customers’ investment in the region.  
 
Since PJM’s inception—and in small measure due to the success of FERC’s and states’ 
policies promoting open access and competition—there has been a marked change in the energy

landscape, with retail customers now able to control how they produce and purchase energy, 
including through competitive suppliers.  Working through wholesale Curtailment Service 
Providers, PJMICC members and others have offered demand response capability to the PJM 
capacity, energy, and ancillary service markets.  Energy-intensive customers may elect behind-the-
meter applications to help manage their energy costs.  Fuel cells and batteries have become part of 
industrial customers’ plans.  Many energy-intensive customers have entered into bilateral 
agreements to support renewable energy development to reach their own corporate sustainability 
goals.  Even residential customers have become linked to PJM wholesale markets through rooftop 
solar and other similar applications, where they may receive LMP energy payments under state 
programs.  FERC’s Order No. 2222 has continued to push for distributed energy resource 
engagement.1  As a result, the 21st-century modern grid looks very different from the wholesale 
grid when PJM became a Regional Transmission Organization (“RTO”).  Even before the “new” 
large computation loads – which dwarf most, if not all, industrial applications – the lines were 
becoming blurry between retail and wholesale jurisdiction. 
 
Notwithstanding PJM’s role as a FERC-jurisdictional utility, even putting aside the latest 
issues with Large Load integration, PJM’s interface with states has been growing.  Now, states that 
may have been willing to defer certain responsibilities to PJM may be unwilling to continue to do 
so.  However, neither PJM nor individual states can succeed on their own.  And each state has 
interests that may diverge from those of others.  Put simply, the region is experiencing a living, 
breathing exercise in cooperative federalism.  Yet, the current framework is limited.  For many 
reasons, including murky jurisdictional boundaries, the current framework for engagement has not 
facilitated the robust coordination required between PJM and the states.  Even more concerning, it 
lacks open, transparent discussions with all affected stakeholders about these challenges, which 
could lead to real problem-solving.   
 
PJM Members have entrusted the PJM Board with the authority to act independently to 
operate markets, plan the system, and perform other functions.  But success requires genuine 
independence.  It requires independence from the PJM Transmission Owners that “gave birth” to 
PJM; it requires independence from the generators of the energy that is forecasted to be in short 
supply in the face of unprecedented load growth; it requires independence from customers who 
are concerned that the price tag to do what may need to be done to support abundant energy supply 
is too much for customers of any size or type to bear.   
 
Building on those themes, PJMICC recommends five focused changes to PJM’s 
governance and stakeholder process.

First, PJM should modify its Operating Agreement (“PJM OA”) to require express 
consideration of the retail-customer impacts of PJM’s wholesale markets, planning, and 
operations. This consideration should include cost modeling and cost-benefit analysis, including 
the cost implications of administrative interventions into PJM markets, and should account for 
impacts on existing bilateral arrangements between suppliers and retail customers. This change 
would signal that PJM’s responsibilities must be understood through the practical effects of its 
wholesale actions on retail customers, particularly in a modern grid where demand response, 
distributed resources, and shared responsibility for resource adequacy have blurred traditional 
jurisdictional lines. 
 
To facilitate this analysis of impacts on retail customers, PJMICC also supports the PJM 
Board qualifications and experience set forth in the OA be expanded to include a Board Member 
with experience in managing significant energy budgets as a consumer, in addition to currently-
established qualifications and experience.2  This change should be memorialized within the PJM 
OA. 
 
These changes to the OA would complement any action to incorporate states into the PJM 
governance or stakeholder process in an enhanced  way.  Without more information about states’ 
preferred role, PJMICC does not take a specific position at this point on the appropriate rights each 
state, or the states collectively, should have in PJM governance.  At a minimum, however, if a 
clearer role for states in PJM governance is being considered, it should be provided along with 
additional guidance on the distinct roles of FERC and the states under the Federal Power Act.  
Given today's pressing problems, a clear and common understanding of who represents the state's 
interests, how to give each state a unique voice in the process, and when to relay state perspectives 
to the Board is vital.    
 
Second, PJM should enhance Board independence and transparency. It is not 
surprising that PJM Members struggle to reach consensus on significant “pocketbook” and cost-
allocation issues.  In these circumstances, the Board is entrusted to act independently.  In so doing, 
given the stakes of these decisions, PJM Members, States, and all stakeholders must have 
confidence that the PJM Board has all relevant information and is making the hard decisions.  The 
Board should not make decisions out of fear influenced by the threat that their decisions may affect 
their tenure in Board service.  At the same time, there must be mechanisms to ensure both PJM 
accountability in general and Board accountability in particular.

The PJM Board’s decisions directly affect energy-intensive businesses' ability to compete 
within the United States and globally.  While different stakeholder communities may not always 
agree with the Board’s decision, they must trust that the Board had all available facts, without 
varnish, and affected stakeholders must understand the basis for the Board’s independent actions.  
As such, we support requiring the Board to issue formal written justifications for its decisions, 
especially those that exceed certain monetary or risk thresholds.  Such written justifications should 
include: (i) identification of the specific problem or need the decision addresses; (ii) explanation 
of the alternatives considered and the rationale for selecting the chosen approach; (iii) quantified 
cost-impact analysis, including the impact on retail markets and their customers; (iv) the success 
metrics by which the decision’s effectiveness will be evaluated; and (v) a commitment to post-
implementation review at a defined interval.  Open Board meetings may be a solution, as may 
enhanced transparency into PJM's corporate goals and the drivers of PJM staff compensation. 
 
Third, PJM should address lower-committee voting practices that can hinder 
innovation and defer customer concerns until too late in the process. Affiliate voting at lower-
level committees can allow incumbent interests to shape the agenda before proposals receive 
sector-weighted review, which weakens early consideration of new technologies, new entrants, 
and customer impacts. 
 
PJMICC supports leveling the playing field from the outset of the stakeholder process.  As 
such, we propose that PJM and its Members reconsider the role of affiliate voting on lower-level 
committees and task forces.  New technologies and entrants’ ideas struggle to secure the necessary 
support to advance their issues in the stakeholder process.  Simply put, new entrants with few 
affiliates have little chance of making real progress in bringing their ideas to the Senior PJM 
Committees for sector-weighted consideration if their innovations affect incumbent market 
participants' revenue opportunities.    
 
Moreover, customers’ voices in lower-level discussions are diluted, and as a result, 
customer concerns are rarely considered until discussion reaches upper-level committees.  At this 
point, any customer concerns are wrongly viewed as a veto when, in fact, they should have been 
helping to shape solutions as the issues progressed through the committee structure.   
 
Fourth, PJM should improve implementation of its consensus-based issue resolution 
(“CBIR”) process. CBIR remains a useful framework, but in practice the process too often 
becomes slowed by extended disputes over problem statements, issue charges, scope, and 
procedural gates before stakeholders can engage the merits. 
 
For example, PJM, PJM Members, and stakeholders spend significant time following the 
CBIR process by identifying their respective interests with respect to an approved issue charge, 
but there is little consideration of cross-checking any proposals developed pursuant to the issue

charge against those identified interests at the end of the process.  This results in a decay in 
accountability.  Also, given the siloed work efforts developed through CBIR, there is little 
opportunity to find common ground across market segments to address the “problem statement.”  
There is a risk of unintended consequences when stakeholders cannot consider the integrated 
energy system as a whole when numerous issues are “off limits.”   
 
Fifth, PJM should use better tools to give the Board actionable information on time-
sensitive issues. The Critical Issue Fast Path (“CIFP”) process has been open and transparent, but 
its votes on numerous divergent proposals often provide less useful guidance than targeted polling, 
written submissions, or structured Board questions designed to identify the most material areas of 
agreement and dispute. 
 
In summary, PJMICC recommends five meaningful but incremental reforms that preserve 
the stability of the PJM market platform while improving independence, accountability, 
transparency, and stakeholder efficiency. Energy investors, energy-intensive customers, including 
data centers, manufacturers, and institutional consumers, all depend on a stable market platform 
to make long-term commitments with confidence; accordingly, PJMICC urges the Commission to 
preserve market stability as a foundational value even as the region adapts to today’s resource 
adequacy challenges.

Response to Commission Questions  
A. 
PJM Governance Mechanics – Board Authority, States’ Role, Filing Rights, 
and Document Architecture  
1. 
PJM Board Role  
 
In responding to this question, PJMICC emphasizes the importance of the PJM Board being 
independent and having the right to submit to FERC any changes to PJM’s market and transmission 
planning rules that it believes are necessary, which the Board has the right to do, under Section 
205 or 206 of the Federal Power Act, as appropriate.3 The PJM OA’s requirement that the Members 
Committee approve any change to the Operating Agreement in order for PJM to modify the PJM 
OA under Section 205 of the Federal Power Act does not detract from the Board’s essential role.4

For the reasons explained below, PJMICC does not support the PJM Board's assumption of 
additional Section 205 rights.  Fundamentally, Section 205 rights are about PJM’s burden of proof 
before this Commission when it files changes to PJM’s operative document.  PJMICC’s position 
is that where there is no broad consensus from within PJM, heightened review by this Commission 
pursuant to Section 206 is appropriate.   
 
Today, the PJM Board has unilateral Section 205 filing rights only with respect to its 
Reliability Assurance Agreement, which governs the PJM capacity construct, and certain aspects 
of the Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) not otherwise reserved by the Transmission 
Owners.5  PJM does not possess Section 205 filing authority over the PJM OA, which governs the 
energy market, or transmission planning under the Tariff.  Only upon PJM Members’ super-
majority sector-weighted approval, does PJM have Section 205 rights over the PJM OA and Tariff 
(with respect to those sections for which the Transmission Owners have not reserved certain 
rights).  Otherwise, PJM must submit any PJM OA or eligible Tariff change under Section 206 of 
the Federal Power Act.   
 
The D.C. Circuit has called Sections 205 and 206 of the Federal Power Act "related but 
distinct."6 Whether rates are changed through Section 205 or Section 206, both "require that the 
rates charged by utilities subject to FERC's jurisdiction be just and reasonable."7  But, as the D.C. 
Circuit said recently, "they enforce that mandate differently.”8 
  
Proceedings initiated under Section 206 have a heavier burden to meet than those initiated 
under Section 205.  As explained by the courts, "In a section 205 proceeding, the utility is not 
required to show that a previous rate was unlawful."9  Rather, "FERC has construed its Section 
205 authority as 'limited to an inquiry into whether the [proposed] rates ... are reasonable,' without 
regard to whether the rates are ‘more or less reasonable’ than other possible rate designs."10  The 
D.C. Circuit also stated, "We have described FERC's role under Section 205 as 'essentially passive 
and reactive'; so long as a utility's rates fit within the zone of reasonableness, FERC is obligated 
to approve them."11

In contrast, under Section 206, FERC must "determine whether an existing rate is 'unjust, 
unreasonable, unduly discriminatory or preferential....'"12  "Only after having made the 
determination that the utility’s existing rate fails that test may FERC exercise its section 206 
authority to impose a new rate."13 While Section 205 and Section 206 must both result in just and 
reasonable rates, courts have made clear that Section 206 has a higher burden, with its two-part 
analysis.  
 
This statutory framework has direct implications for how Section 205 filing rights should 
be allocated within PJM's governance structure.  Because Section 205 relieves the proponent of 
any burden to justify the unreasonableness of the status quo, the lighter burden is properly reserved 
for changes that already command broad support within the PJM Membership.  In PJMICC’s view, 
broad stakeholder support serves as evidence that a proposed rule reflects a balanced consideration 
of competing interests and is more likely to produce durable, workable market outcomes.  Where 
a rule change lacks such support, the heightened scrutiny of Section 206 is the appropriate check. 
 
PJM's right to file under Section 205 of the FPA is a privilege.  For this reasons, the 
privilege of filing under Section 205 should continue to require the moderating influence of sector-
weighted supermajority support as evidence of a balanced proposal.  If PJM Members do not 
widely support a rule change, enhanced FERC scrutiny under Section 206 of the Federal Power 
Act is appropriate, especially given the impacts on customers.  The impact, however, transcends 
cost and includes whether the proposed changes are reasonably targeted to achieve the desired 
objective, whether they involve changes in market behavior or investment. 
 
The practical consequences of unilateral Section 205 authority are evident in PJM's 
capacity construct—the one area where the Board already possesses such authority.  The PJM 
capacity construct has generated sustained, recurring stakeholder concern and dissatisfaction.  That 
observation suggests the challenges facing PJM’s governance may be more cultural and 
operational than structural or documentary.  Notably, the non-capacity aspects of PJM's markets, 
where Section 205 rights require supermajority support, have not experienced comparable rule-
revision churn. 
 
PJMICC urges the Commission to preserve the existing balance rather than expand the 
Board's unilateral filing authority.  If the PJM Board believes strongly that a proposed change is 
just and reasonable, it should have the confidence to pursue that change through the customary 
process, including filing such changes under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act, rather than 
seeking expanded unilateral filing rights.

2. 
States’ and OPSI’s Interface with the Board 
 
In PJMICC’s view, the states' role in PJM's governance is best exercised through structured 
consultation with the Board and the PJM Membership, rather than through an expanded or 
decisional role in PJM's stakeholder-driven, sector-weighted governance structure.  
 
States' perspectives on PJM's markets and operations are necessarily informed by 
considerations that extend beyond wholesale market outcomes, including retail policy objectives 
and states' regulatory relationships with the utilities they oversee.  PJMICC believes those 
considerations are best channeled through existing consultative mechanisms.  OPSI already has 
the opportunity to meet privately with the PJM Board at OPSI’s annual meeting, and OPSI 
provides a standing report at each PJM Members Committee meeting.  These existing channels 
offer a meaningful venue for candid dialogue between the states and the Board, reflect a sound 
foundation on which to build further engagement and can be built upon without any need for rule 
changes. 
 
Should the Commission or PJM consider affording states a greater voice in governance, 
PJMICC would favor a framework centered on state public utility commissions and organized 
around consultation rather than a decisional role in PJM's governance process.  In addition to 
OPSI’s current engagement (which is a strong base upon which to build),  it is worth noting that 
state consumer advocate offices often exercise voting rights in the PJM End-Use Customer Sector.  
While not direct extensions of state authority, state consumer advocate offices are, in many cases, 
appointed and, in at least some cases, confirmed by instruments of the state.  This is another vehicle 
in which states’ interests have been represented directly in PJM governance and stakeholder 
process.  
 
At the same time, PJMICC recognizes that greater state engagement may yield meaningful 
benefits, particularly to the extent it strengthens states’ ability to help craft constructive, durable 
outcomes, especially given the cooperative federalism model.  PJMICC would support formalizing 
OPSI's existing consultative access and expanding it to include individual states —for example, 
by establishing a recognized opportunity for states to address the Members Committee or the 
broader PJM membership in advance of a vote on matters of particular interest to states.  Such 
consultative access should also include adequate time to address the Board on relevant issues being 
addressed in a CIFP-like stakeholder process.  PJMICC views this approach as a constructive 
means of enhancing states' voice within PJM's governance structure while preserving the sector-
weighted, stakeholder-driven framework that has long supported the effective functioning of PJM's 
markets.

3. 
Governance Reforms for Accountability and Responsiveness 
 
Especially during this time of unprecedented stress on PJM as the RTO, PJMICC supports 
building meaningful and enforceable accountability into PJM's governance.  As discussed in the 
Introduction, PJMICC recommends that PJM engage in a comprehensive customer cost-impact 
assessment at the outset of any significant rule change and a clear statement of what the change is 
expected to cost or save customers, consistent with the PJM OA amendment PJMICC proposed 
above. Second, each significant rule change should include defined, measurable success metrics—
articulated at the proposal stage—against which the rule’s performance can be objectively 
evaluated. Third, a structured after-the-fact review of whether the rule delivered its intended result, 
including with respect to its cost impact, should be required within a defined period (e.g., 18 to 24 
months following implementation).  If the results did not yield the intended results, PJM should 
be required to report publicly on the variance, identify contributing factors, and propose corrective 
action for stakeholder consideration. 
 
Recent changes to the PJM capacity construct illustrate the risk of proceeding without such 
accountability.  By way of a hypothetical example, if a change is represented by PJM as having 
minimal impact yet proves substantially more consequential once implemented, there should be 
accountability for that initial change and a willingness by PJM to address the inconsistency.  For 
that reason, PJMICC would support building accountability mechanisms directly into any 
significant or fast-tracked rule changes — potentially including sunset or reversion features — so 
that a change in rules or market design failing to deliver its represented outcome within a defined 
period results in a defined consequence or a path back, rather than becoming permanently 
entrenched irrespective of its actual results. 
 
4.  
Division of Section 205 Filing Rights 
 
Through PJMICC’s members who are also members of PJM, PJMICC has valued having 
a seat at the governance table through the Members Committee’s role in the PJM Section 205 
approval process.  To be clear, the End-Use Customer Sector, while representing customers that 
are ultimately responsible for all of the costs of the PJM market, only has 20% of the overall vote 
in sector-weighted voting.  While a reasonable case could be made that customers should have 
more of a voice in sector-weighted voting, PJMICC supports maintaining the status quo and the 
balanced voting framework.  As noted above, PJMICC does not support expanding PJM’s 
unilateral Section 205 filing authority—particularly absent any requirement that PJM consider 
customer rate impacts as part of its mission.  We acknowledge that PJM Transmission Owners 
separately hold the exclusive right to file changes to their own transmission rates under Section 
205.

While the results of the PJM Board having Section 205 rights may be swifter, the attendant 
risks of such action are greater.  As a threshold matter, PJM’s markets are undeniably complex, 
and PJM lacks visibility into how changes to those markets may affect, for example, load-serving 
entities and their customers. PJM rule changes directly impact the region’s energy-intensive 
manufacturers and may have far-reaching implications for their operations, opportunities to 
manage their energy costs through demand response, and reliability.  In that same vein, PJM lacks 
visibility into LSEs’ contractual arrangements with their retail customers.  Most retail supply 
contracts contain Change in Law provisions, allowing LSEs to pass through the impacts of FERC-
approved rule changes.  If End-Use Customers’ voice in the stakeholder process is muted by 
reduced voting rights, there is a risk that this important customer voice may be ignored. 
 
Separately, any discussion of filing rights or PJM’s independence should be paired with 
commitments from Transmission Owners not to leverage threats of withdrawal from PJM as 
negotiating leverage.  Recent appellate-level attention to RTO independence in the context of a 
PJM TO proposal to empower PJM with Section 205 rights stressed the importance of PJM 
independence.14   
 
5. 
Governing Document Provisions That Impede Timely Decision-
Making 
 
In our experience, as discussed above, the primary source of delay is less about the text of 
PJM’s governing documents and more about how PJM’s consensus-based issue resolution process 
(“CBIR”), which is set forth in Manual 34, is implemented.  The CBIR process consumes 
enormous stakeholder time and resources.  In this respect, all PJM stakeholders, including PJM 
Members, other stakeholders, and PJM itself, share responsibility for a constructive process.  For 
example, the problem statement and issue charge process, which is a key part of the CBIR process, 
while well-intentioned and meant to define the scope of any stakeholder work effort, has become 
bloated and, at times, used to delay rather than advance substantive engagement.  It also warrants 
emphasizing that the complexity of PJM’s market rules means that many issues implicate 
provisions across multiple governing documents, requiring significant education efforts before 
work efforts can meaningfully begin.  This dynamic itself complicates and slows the pursuit of 
otherwise well-supported reforms.

In addition, issues tend to be worked in a siloed fashion under the current process, which 
limits stakeholders’ ability to develop cross-cutting packages or compromises—the kind of horse-
trading across related issues that used to occur more naturally. Stakeholders have previously 
reached a compromise across related but technically distinct issues — for example, agreeing to 
support one proposal in exchange for progress on a complementary reform. The current siloed 
structure of the PJM stakeholder process makes this kind of cross-issue accommodation far more 
difficult to achieve.  The siloed approach also makes it harder to determine whether the stakeholder 
process is meant to build consensus, to generate information for PJM’s independent decision-
making, or both. 
 
PJMICC recommends that PJM, in consultation with its Members and stakeholders, 
strengthen the structure and discipline of the CBIR process to ensure discussions remain focused 
and productive.  This would include creating a defined metric for success, and a mechanism for 
after-the-fact accountability. At the same time, the process should allow more flexibility to 
consider related issues together where appropriate.  In PJMICC’s experience, this provides more 
opportunity to create mutual gains, an important underpinning of the CBIR process. 
 
Also, as discussed below, delays may be attributable to the practice that lower-level 
committees allow affiliate voting, whereas the senior committees (i.e., the Markets and Reliability 
Committee and Members Committee) use sector-weighted voting.  There is a structural 
inefficiency in the lower-level committees' setup: a vote that is generally doomed to fail when a 
more balanced voting approach is used, which better reflects the votes of wholesale and retail 
customers.  This dynamic can be resolved and more durable outcomes developed if the lower-level 
committees were to model the same voting approach as the senior committees, even if on an 
advisory basis to help discern whether proposals may garner supermajority, sector-weighted 
support. 
 
PJMICC's observations here apply equally to the stakeholder-process-specific questions 
addressed in Panel 2 below. 
 
6. 
Lessons from Other RTO/ISO Governance Structures 
 
PJM’s stakeholder process provides arguably the most extensive customer participation of 
any RTO or ISO. That is a distinction worth preserving, not diminishing. PJMICC has participated 
in PJM governance for roughly 30 years; PJMICC has long understood the need for a seat at the 
table to protect the interests of energy-intensive customers. 
 
PJMICC is cautious about wholesale importation of other regions’ governance models, 
particularly proposals that would reduce RTO independence or dilute member and stakeholder 
checks on the Board.  PJMICC would rather see PJM build on its own comparatively strong record

of multi-sector engagement than adopt structures from regions with less robust customer 
participation. 
 
B. 
PJM Stakeholder Process – Design, Transparency, and Decision-making 
1. Sector-Weighted Voting and Voting Thresholds 
PJMICC’s answer is: yes, sector-weighted voting strikes the right balance—but the 
implementation is imperfect. Sector-weighted voting works because it requires broad buy-in from 
both supply and demand interests before a proposal receives the requisite support to enable PJM 
to file a change to the PJM OA or to certain provisions of the Tariff under Section 205. It is not an 
unanimity requirement, but it demands meaningful support from each market segment. Most 
proposals pass by broad acclamation.  Generally speaking, only the most contentious proposals do 
not receive super-majority support.  It is in precisely these instances that Section 206’s more 
searching review is warranted.  The structure also protects PJM’s markets and reliability against 
perceived or real favoritism. 
 
Because customer purchases account for roughly half of all market activity in the PJM 
region, and customers have invested billions of dollars in the region, customers must continue to 
have a strong voice in voting outcomes. Any effort to unravel sector-weighted voting would be 
viewed by PJMICC as an unacceptable effort to disenfranchise customers. 
 
In PJMICC’s view, the existing voting thresholds do not preclude timely action.  PJM has 
tools at its disposal, including the CIFP process, to accelerate issues when needed.  PJM can bring 
forward its own problem statements/issue charges to initiate a stakeholder discussion.  Issue 
Charges require only majority support to advance, and most Issue Charges do, in fact, generally 
move forward.15  PJM also plays a significant role in setting the stakeholder meeting calendar, 
which can be used to prioritize pressing issues.  If PJM communicates a pressing reliability need 
to the Members or stakeholder community, our experience is that the stakeholder community will 
rally to support the necessary effort.   
 
The need for timely action has grown, given the pace of new large-load interconnection 
requests.  This issue has proven difficult for stakeholders to resolve by consensus, despite 
substantial investment of time and resources by stakeholders in PJM’s two most recent CIFP 
processes on this topic, both of which have been focused on resource adequacy-related Tariff 
changes where the PJM Board already has Section 205 rights.  Even if PJM Members were to 
reach consensus on issues falling within the PJM Tariff and the super-majority sector-weighted 
threshold were exceeded, the PJM Members' votes have been  advisory only.  As such, the voting 
threshold is not directly relevant to PJM’s decision about what to bring forward to the Commission.

That said, as a general matter, as explained previously, there is inefficiency in the PJM

To be clear, PJMICC is not seeking to disenfranchise those with affiliate votes. PJMICC

structure. PJMICC simply asks that lower-committee discussions to be conducted with an eye 
toward the ultimate sector-weighted vote. This would produce more durable, well-vetted outcomes 
and reduce wasted effort. And PJMICC respectfully requests that those with affiliate votes respect 
customers’ right to be heard when load interests raise concerns at the Markets and Reliability or 
Members Committees, the two PJM senior committees, which both utilize sector-weighted voting. 
2. 
Transparency Reforms 
PJMICC believes that transparency reforms are necessary to improve the quality of PJM’s 
stakeholder process and increase stakeholder confidence in its decision-making, especially when 
the Board takes unilateral action.  As a threshold matter, especially as the effects of PJM’s markets 
are becoming broader and even more impactful, PJM customers have consistently requested more 
detail on the practical implications of proposed changes, to help all stakeholders better understand 
the impact of PJM’s rules and changes thereto.  More stakeholders that are not PJM Members, 
such as states, new data center customers, manufacturers, other energy-intensive customers, 
developers, are making unprecedented investments in doing business in PJM.  They depend on 
PJM’s markets.  As market rules evolve, there needs to be clear communications as to their impact 
to avoid such investments becoming stranded.  Complexity of information is a real barrier to 
meaningful stakeholder engagement, particularly for parties without large in-house technical staff. 
Clear, plain-language communication about the impacts of proposed rule changes would be a 
significant improvement. 
 
PJMICC also supports improvements to the accessibility and organization of information 
PJM makes available, including on its website, which stakeholders have found difficult to navigate. 
At the front end, cost-benefit studies should be a more regular, expected component of any 
significant rule-change proposal, including those of stakeholders.  Stakeholders cannot 
meaningfully evaluate proposals they cannot fully understand.  Similarly, while PJM is responsible 
for the functioning of its wholesale markets, there should be careful and public consideration of

15 
 
the impacts of action (or inaction) on retail markets and retail customers alike.  To the extent that 
PJM Staff cannot support Members' or stakeholders’ alternate proposals, PJM should plainly 
explain the specific reasons for its decision.    
 
More transparency about the basis for PJM Board decisions would also increase 
stakeholder confidence in its decision-making.  It is understandable that certain aspects of PJM 
Staff and Board interactions may need to occur without transparency, but, as discussed above, there 
should be transparency regarding corporate goals to build stakeholder understanding.  
Additionally, the Board should commit to publishing, within a defined period following any 
significant independent action, a summary report that includes the projected outcomes and success 
metrics underlying its decision, and the Board should affirmatively commit to convening a 
stakeholder review session at a specified interval (e.g., 18 to 24 months post-implementation) to 
assess whether the rule is achieving its intended objectives. 
3. 
Structured Opportunities for State Participation 
As noted under Panel 1, PJMICC supports continuing and formalizing OPSI’s existing 
consultative access to PJM’s Board and membership, including the existing practice of private 
OPSI-Board meetings at OPSI’s annual meeting and OPSI’s standing report-out opportunity at 
Members Committee meetings. PJMICC would also support a defined opportunity for states to 
address the membership before a vote on matters of particular interest to states.   
 
PJMICC believes consultative engagement is the appropriate role for states within PJM’s 
governance structure. This preserves PJM’s independence while giving states a structured channel 
to be heard on issues that affect their ratepayers. 
4. 
Impediments to Stakeholder Decision-Making  
Especially with the current large-load/resource-adequacy discussions, a significant 
impediment to stakeholder decision-making is the lack of clarity about jurisdictional limits 
between state and federal authority in the 21st-century modern grid.  Moreover, state jurisdiction 
may vary under state law.  From a stakeholder perspective, it is also not always clear who is 
empowered to speak for a state.  In a very real way, the lack of clear jurisdiction to address certain 
identified problems is an impediment to decision-making.   
 
Another impediment that is worth underscoring is that stakeholder discussions on many of 
these complex proposals are siloed. Although PJMICC understands that PJM staff may be having 
holistic discussions, PJM stakeholder discussions occur on a more discrete basis, preventing a full 
understanding of proposed solutions to complex problems.

5. 
Reforms to Help PJM Meet the Region’s Challenges 
In response to this question, PJMICC must underscore its importance: the region’s 
challenges must be met; the economic and human consequences of failing to do so are simply too 
great.  Such an effort requires coordinated efforts at all levels of government as well as with and 
within the PJM stakeholder community.  Meeting these challenges is a shared imperative for PJM, 
its Members, and the region's states.  As we consider governance changes, such reforms are not a 
“cure-all” for the pressing problems facing the region today and should not distract from 
addressing them. 
 
As the Commission considers the need for timely action to enable PJM to help meet the 
region’s challenges, PJMICC encourages the Commission to recognize a tension in capital-
intensive industries like energy between action and stability.  Energy investors require stability to 
support the investments that are necessary for the region to meet today’s resource adequacy 
challenges.  Energy-intensive customers, including not only data centers but also manufacturers 
and institutional customers, require a stable platform to make their investments, too.  Volatility 
comes with risk, and risk is often ultimately borne by retail customers.  Acknowledging that certain 
changes will be necessary for the region to meet today’s challenges, PJMICC encourages keeping 
an eye on stability to enable capital to be deployed with some measure of confidence. 
 
Four reforms would meaningfully improve PJM’s ability to meet regional challenges. First, 
building meaningful accountability into the stakeholder process: clear success metrics identified 
at the problem statement stage, and after-the-fact review of whether implemented changes 
achieved their stated goals, including cost impacts.   
 
Second, allowing stakeholders to evaluate related issues as packages rather than only in 
narrow, siloed work efforts. The ability to build cross-issue compromises—to say, ‘we will accept 
this change if that complementary reform also moves forward’—has been lost in recent years. 
Restoring that flexibility would produce more durable outcomes and better serve the region. 
 
Third, PJMICC suggests that alternative facilitation tools be considered to make meeting 
time, especially for pressing issues of the day, more productive.  For example, recent experience 
has been that PJM Members and stakeholders can often wait for hours to ask clarifying questions 
and then may not have the opportunity to do so.  With advance notice (i.e., not last-minute), PJM 
could schedule multi-day transparent working sessions with decision-makers from across the PJM 
community, including federal and state regulators.  PJMICC recognizes that any such sessions 
would need to be carefully structured to respect the Commission’s ex parte requirements and each 
participating state’s independent decision-making role.   
 
Finally, while these pressing issues are being considered, PJM should consider 
consolidating its regular cadence of stakeholder meetings, perhaps by deferring non-essential

of stakeholder work have been unrelenting, and these issues will require considerable thought and

priorities. 
 
6. 
Near-Term Reforms to Improve Speed While Preserving Stakeholder 
Input 
 
PJMICC supports restoring more robust engagement and compromise-seeking at the 
lower-committee level, rather than deferring hard conversations to the Markets and Reliability and 
Members Committees. If lower committees were to develop proposals with an eye toward the 
ultimate sector-weighted vote—rather than pushing proposals forward on the strength of affiliate 
votes alone—the results would be more durable, surprises at the Markets and Reliability 
Committee would diminish, and overall pace would improve.  We also support exploring a 
position-paper process for major issues—allowing parties to submit written positions for 
consideration in lieu of extended, time-intensive stakeholder meetings.  
 
Given the heavy day-to-day meeting burden the current process places on stakeholders, 
particularly those with limited resources, a transparent paper-hearing approach could be a 
meaningful time-saver for well-developed issues without sacrificing the quality of stakeholder 
input.  Additionally, reducing the number of issues under active consideration at any given time 
would allow more time between stakeholder meetings for offline discussion and deliberation. 
Reducing the volume of changes to PJM’s governing documents also enhances market rule 
stability, which is something customers value highly. 
 
7. 
Committee Structure Elements Contributing to Delay 
 
Delays are frequently attributable to components of the CBIR process becoming extended 
without meaningful progress, such as through extended consideration of fine-tuning the problem 
statement and issue charge process and debates over whether issues are in or out of scope, as I 
have described. Additionally, meeting facilitation and management challenges also can 
contribute—for example, extended, unfocused discussion by a small number of participants can 
consume hours without advancing the substantive work. 
 
PJMICC supports streamlining problem statement and issue charge templates and 
exploring new tools for meeting facilitation to keep discussions focused on the defined scope and 
success metrics. Education is a key component of any work effort and must continue in a structured 
manner, with PJM inviting subject matter experts when necessary.  Better discipline at the process 
level would reduce delay more effectively than structural changes to the committee architecture.

8. 
Fast-Path and Time-Bound Review Procedures 
 
PJMICC sees value in time-bound stakeholder review for genuine emergencies. However, 
PJMICC is not convinced the current the CIFP process is well-designed to identify and prioritize 
the right solutions.  The issues that PJM has identified for CIFP treatment have been appropriate.  
However, the process was not designed to handle issues of such breadth and scope, resulting in the 
Board considering numerous competing proposals, none of which have had the time to develop a 
critical mass of support.   
 
A gating question may be whether the Board’s objective is to achieve consensus, or instead 
to generate the most useful information for the Board to exercise its independent judgment.  If it 
is the former, more time should be afforded to allow for such consensus-building to occur, perhaps 
through small-group facilitation.  If it is the latter, it may be more useful to model a paper hearing 
process with an opportunity for the Board to ask clarifying questions to facilitate their review and 
make an independent judgment.   
 
9. 
Lessons from Other RTOs 
 
PJMICC’s principal lesson from other RTOs is caution. PJM should preserve its 
comparatively robust customer participation and stakeholder checks while adopting targeted 
process improvements that strengthen independence, transparency, and efficiency. 
 
III.  
Conclusion  
 
On behalf of PJMICC, I appreciate the Commission's invitation to participate in this 
important technical conference.  PJM's markets have, for decades, delivered substantial value to 
the region.  This record of success reflects the strength of PJM's existing governance framework, 
including the checks and balances embedded in its sector-weighted, stakeholder-driven structure. 
These structures embody the checks and balances necessary for a market in which electricity is an 
essential service and market power is an ever-present concern.  
 
PJM’s independence, neutrality, and transparency are prerequisites for any workable 
governance approach—without such commitments by PJM, no governance structure will succeed. 
The recommendations set forth in this statement are offered in that spirit: as constructive, targeted 
improvements intended to help PJM and its Members make more effective and efficient use of the 
stakeholder process and broaden the lens by which PJM views its mission to include the impact of 
its wholesale market on retail markets and customers.   
 
While PJMICC does not seek fundamental restructuring, PJMICC believes that certain 
changes are necessary to ensure PJM's stakeholder process can keep pace with the region's

19 
 
evolving operational and market needs—and that any such changes must preserve, rather than 
diminish, the voice and representation of end-use customers in PJM's governance.  PJMICC seeks 
concrete, actionable reforms: meaningful and enforceable accountability for the cost impacts of 
rule changes, including quantified pre-implementation projections, defined success metrics, 
mandatory post-implementation review, and clear consequences for rules that fail to meet stated 
objectives; strengthened and disciplined stakeholder processes that permit cross-issue 
compromise; respect for the sector-weighted vote at every level of the process; and discipline in 
the use of fast-path procedures. These are achievable improvements that would serve the region 
well without destabilizing the governance architecture that has supported one of the world's most 
successful and competitive wholesale electricity markets for decades. 
 
Thank you for the opportunity to present these views. PJMICC looks forward to continued 
engagement on these important issues.
