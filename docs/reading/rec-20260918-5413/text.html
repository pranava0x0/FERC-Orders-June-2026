MOTION FOR LEAVE TO ANSWER AND ANSWER OF 
AMERICAN ELECTRIC POWER SERVICE CORPORATION 
 
 
Pursuant to Rules 212 and 213, AEP hereby submits this motion for leave to answer and 
answer (“Answer”) to the September 10 answer filed by PJM Interconnection, LLC (“PJM”) in 
the captioned proceeding. 1  PJM’s Answer fails to meaningfully respond to significant flaws in 
the proposed Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) exit provisions.  Most of these flaws 
emanate from the proposal that a departing member not just cover its load obligations for RBP 
Charges, but also hold third-party generators harmless through some combination of accelerated 
payments, pseudo-ties, or renegotiated contracts, depending on which generators PJM elects in its 
sole discretion to retain.  PJM suggests that these issues can be worked out in “extensive 
negotiations” upon a member’s exit.2  This response from PJM exposes the problem well:  RTO 
membership remains voluntary, and PJM governing documents have never required “extensive 
negotiations” before a member can exercise its legal right to leave.  This lack of clear, 
administrable standards renders the RBP exit provisions unjust and unreasonable.  PJM’s proposal 
to have unfettered discretion in those negotiations further renders this new exit fee unacceptable.

I. 
MOTION FOR LEAVE TO ANSWER 
While an answer to a protest or comment is generally not permitted under the 
Commission’s rules,3 AEP respectfully moves for leave to file this Answer and asks that the 
Commission consider this response.4  The Commission permits an answer when it provides useful 
and relevant information that will assist the Commission in its decision-making process or where 
the answer will clarify the issues before the Commission.5  This Answer clarifies the issues and 
provides critical responses to arguments presented in PJM’s Answer that will assist the 
Commission in its decision-making process in this proceeding.

B. 
PJM’s Answer Does Not Provide a Basis for the Commission to Approve 
PJM’s Unfettered Discretion Over the Calculation of the RBP Exit Fee 
 PJM’s Answer is unresponsive to AEP’s arguments regarding the new exit fee and thus 
provides an inadequate basis for the Commission to approve this portion of the RBP Proposal.  
Because the proposed RBP exit fee goes well beyond the financial obligation allocated to departing 
load for its RBP Charges, the single largest variable in PJM’s calculation of the exit fee is the 
treatment of RBP Resources in the departing member’s zone.  PJM’s proposal insists that PJM 
retain sole discretion over this critical input to the exit fee.  Specifically, PJM seeks complete 
discretion over whether to retain an RBP Resource via pseudo-tie upon a Transmission Owner’s 
departure without any objective, predetermined criteria governing which RBP Resources to retain 
and which to release.

PJM first asks the Commission to disregard these concerns by AEP and others on the 
grounds that they can be addressed later in the “extensive negotiations” that should happen upon 
a transmission withdrawal.11  This answer illustrates AEP’s point.  The current PJM governing 
documents do not require a Transmission Owner to negotiate its way out of PJM.  As long as it

preferred outcome in any such negotiation.  It is inadequate to simply state, as PJM does, that “it

principles, the RBP exit provisions would ultimately afford PJM considerable discretion over a 
Transmission Owner’s ability to withdraw.  Indeed, PJM could either elect not to keep RBP 
Resources and require the departing Transmission Owners to make them whole, or it could elect 
to keep more resources than it needs and force the departing Transmission Owner to facilitate 
expensive and needless pseudo-ties.  PJM’s related argument that a departing member has 
“flexibility” in exit terms is also an overstatement.  This optionality would only apply after PJM 
has exercised its discretion to decide which RBP Resources to retain.    
Finally, PJM’s reliance on the Ratepayer Protection Pledge to justify this portion of its 
proposal is misplaced.  Utilities’ pledge to protect customers from hyperscaler costs has nothing 
to do with PJM’s exit fee.  In any event, this argument supports AEP as much as PJM.  When a 
member departs PJM for alternative arrangements, it would do so to better serve its customers.  
Burdening departing load with costs well in excess of its RBP Charges is flatly contrary to the 
Ratepayer Protection Pledge.

C. 
The RBP Exit Provisions Represent a Fundamental Change to the 
Framework for Withdrawal from PJM that PJM Has Not Justified  
The proposed provisions would materially alter the financial calculus of RTO withdrawal 
in a manner that is untested and unprecedented.  As described in AEP’s Protest, the current terms 
for withdrawing from PJM are clear and well-established.13  They provide notice to members 
electing to withdraw from PJM regarding what to expect upon withdrawal and do not:  (1) contain 
an exit fee; (2) mandate PJM’s negotiation of said exit fee with the withdrawing party; (3) provide 
PJM with discretion how or whether to provide departing Transmission Owners with an 
opportunity to mitigate the exit fee; or (4) require that the Commission determine the final sum of 
the exit fee.   
PJM’s proposed RBP exit terms ask the Commission to approve each of these elements, 
which would implement a new, wholly unvetted and untested framework in PJM and subject 
departing Transmission Owners to PJM’s choice as to what exit fee to assign a departing 
Transmission Owner.  Given the magnitude and novelty of the proposed RBP exit provisions, the 
Commission should carefully consider whether this proceeding—a one-time, expedited backstop 
procurement designed to address a near-term capacity shortfall—is the appropriate vehicle through 
which to fundamentally alter the terms of Transmission Owner departure from PJM.  The RBP 
was developed under an extraordinarily compressed timeline, driven by the urgent need to procure 
capacity for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year.  The exit provisions, however, would create financial 
obligations extending through the 2042/2043 Delivery Year and would establish a precedent with 
implications far beyond this one-time procurement.  The Commission should not allow an 
expedited, one-time filing to serve as the mechanism for establishing new long-term exit
