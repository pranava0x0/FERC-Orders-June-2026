The constraining nature of existing infrastructure and the costs to plan and construct new transmission and distribution capability is not unique to data centers, new loads or even the electric power industry.7

NERC describes the simplest driver of varying power flows: the load’s consumption patterns differing from the generator’s production.16 In explicitly advising the Commission about reliability risks to the grid, NERC refers to circumstances when the co-located generation is not providing the same level of power as the co-located load is consuming.17 That is, when the co- located load has demand which is greater than the output of the co-located generator, energy flows from the transmission system to the co-located load. When the co-located load is operating with energy demand less than the output of the co-located generator, energy flows from the generator to the transmission system. When the co-located load has demand that is the same as the production by the co-located generator, real energy does not flow to the transmission system. C.    Transmission Service Requirements
Please explain whether co-location arrangements are or should be required to take Network Integration Transmission Service or Point-to-Point Transmission Service; or whether a more tailored new category of transmission service is warranted.

Co-location arrangements should be required to take either Network Integration Transmission Service or Point-to-Point Transmission Service. The existing terms and conditions of these services are sufficient.  As noted in the Show Cause Order, the Commission has defined transmission service as the provision of “electric transmission, network or point-to-point service, ancillary services or other methods of electric transmission, or the interconnection with jurisdictional transmission facilities.”44 The co-location arrangements all include one or more pieces of equipment (conductors, breakers, and or substations) between the co-located load and the co-located generator. 45 These are attached to the generator’s own Interconnection Facilities (specifically the Interconnection Customer’s Interconnection Facilities) which are jurisdictional.46 By the definition cited, there is Commission-defined transmission service separate and prior to the initiation of operations and the variable flow of energy to serve the load.

PJM must maintain a margin of transmission capacity separate from providing reserves in generation to meet supply shortfalls. PJM, as the Transmission Provider and Transmission Operator, calculates available transmission capacity with a reserve known as the Transmission Reliability Margin which is set “to represent historical load forecast error” in the day-ahead time horizon. 47 In this way, PJM uses the transmission system to manage reliability impacts from unforeseen changes in load. New variability in the load not previously included in calculating this margin will quite possibly require new Transmission Reliability Margin levels in some or all of PJM.

IV. Conclusion
In these comments we explain what it means to be “electrically connected and synchronized to the PJM Transmission System when consuming power” and how that implicates the use of both ancillary services and transmission service. We illustrate how the fully isolated load idea is a fiction, supported only by a private contract that does not establish different physical facts. The loads require transmission and ancillary services because the host generators are unable to operate without the grid. The host generators are unable to provide all the required capabilities to match the variable load. There is evidence enough to conclude there are no fully isolated loads in these cases of existing generators proposals. “In order for a co-location arrangement (i.e., the generator plus data center) to obtain these services, the grid itself must exist. Ancillary services pass through transmission lines, not the air.”48
