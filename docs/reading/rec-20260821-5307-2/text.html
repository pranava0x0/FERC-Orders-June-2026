I.  QUALIFICATIONS AND ASSIGNMENT
1. My name is Carl F. Coscia. I am the Principal of Toller Energy, an independent energy consulting firm. My business address is Wynnewood, Pennsylvania 19096. I hold a Ph.D. in Economics from the University of Minnesota, and a B.S. in Economics and a B.A. in Mathematics from the University of Kansas. I have more than twenty-five years of experience in wholesale power and commodity markets, concentrated in credit risk, market design and regulatory proceedings, and have provided testimony, affidavits or economic analysis in more than forty proceedings before the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, and other regulatory and self-regulatory bodies.

2. From September 2022 to March 2026 I served as Chief Risk Officer of PJM Interconnection, L.L.C., where I led the corporate risk function across the energy, capacity and financial transmission rights markets. The credit provisions of Attachment Q to the Open Access Transmission Tariff fell within my responsibility, as did the Key Risk Metrics reported to the Risk Management Committee. Based on that experience I am familiar with how collateral requirements in the organized markets are derived from measured exposure, administered as that exposure changes, and released as it declines. Before joining PJM I held senior risk and commercial roles at EnBW AG, Hartree Partners, LP, Constellation Energy and TXU, and earlier served as a Branch Chief in the Office of Enforcement at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission and at The Brattle Group.

3. I have been retained by Northern Virginia Electric Cooperative (“NOVEC”) to evaluate the credit and collateral provisions proposed at Tariff, Attachment DD, section 18.8 as they apply to the cooperative and to the NOVEC zone/area.

4. I have also submitted an affidavit in this docket on behalf of East Kentucky Power Cooperative addressing the proposed credit construct generally. Both cooperatives are aware of the other engagement and each has consented to it. The opinions expressed here are consistent with those expressed there. This affidavit does not repeat the general analysis. Where a conclusion in this affidavit rests on analysis set out at length in that affidavit, I cite it rather than restate it.

5. The opinions in this affidavit are my own. My compensation is not contingent on the outcome of this proceeding.

II.  SUMMARY OF OPINIONS
6. My opinions are as follows. The cooperative's financial and operating figures stated in this summary are drawn from the certified report described in Section III.

7. (a)  The Reliability Backstop Procurement settles as a contract for differences. In each Delivery Year load pays the difference between the RBP price and the RPM clearing price, not the RBP

PDF page 2

price itself. The Credit Rate was not derived that way. At $555.00/MW-day over fifteen years the gross contract value is approximately $3.04 million per MW, and the $1.5 million Credit Rate is approximately half of it — a figure computed as though the large-load-serving Load Serving Entity owed the full contract price rather than a difference between two prices. PJM has sized collateral for a fixed-price contract and filed a contract for differences.

8. (b)  The exposure can be measured. Every input to the first two Delivery Years will have been published within twenty days of the commitment binding. On the prices established and PJM's illustrative RBP price, the exposure attributable to the NOVEC zone over those two years is approximately $214.7 million, against which the requirement is approximately nine times. Across the full fifteen-year term the requirement exceeds the exposure under every RPM price path I have examined.

9. (c)  The allocation itself comes from a planning document. The cooperative's 1,302.6 MW is traceable to the large load forecast it submitted to PJM's Load Analysis Subcommittee for use in the annual load forecast, at a time when neither the Reliability Backstop Procurement nor section 18.8 existed. A submission made for transmission planning has become the basis of a fifteen-year financial obligation, and the tariff provides no reconciliation of the allocated megawatts against the load actually realized.

10. (d)  PJM already holds collateral against this cooperative. Approximately $34.4 million secures all of the cooperative's existing activity in PJM's markets, for a system serving a coincident peak of approximately 2,360 MW. The procurement would add an obligation of 1,302.6 MW, an increase of approximately 55 percent, and section 18.8(b) would require approximately $1.954 billion against it. A 55 percent increase in obligation produces a fifty-seven-fold increase in collateral. The new requirement is incremental to the existing one, which continues. Once RBP billing commences, the same megawatts are secured twice — by the Credit Rate under section 18.8(b) and again through Peak Market Activity under Attachment Q.

11. (e)  Two risks arise and they fall on different parties. The first is that a Load Serving Entity fails to pay the RBP Charge; the parties exposed are PJM and, through the default provisions, the remaining Members. The second is that a large load included in the forecast fails to materialize, the cooperative still owes the RBP Charge on capacity procured for it, and its native load carries that cost until a replacement large load arrives; the party exposed is the cooperative's membership. Section 18.8(b) collateral secures the first only. The second is the risk PJM described when it explained why an opt-out was needed, and nothing in the filed construct measures or secures it.

12. (f)  PJM identified the overbuy problem during the stakeholder process and built a remedy for it at section 18.1(d). It then made that remedy available only to cooperatives whose rates are not regulated by a state. This cooperative's rates are regulated by the Virginia State Corporation Commission, so it is excluded — although its customers cannot choose another supplier, no other Load Serving Entity can assume the obligation, and its members would bear an overbuy in full. The condition does not track the problem PJM described.

13. (g)  It is highly unlikely that the collateral requirement can be met. Approximately $1.954 billion is roughly eight times the cooperative's cash and temporary investments, 114 percent of its total assets, and 212 percent of its total margins and equities.

14. (h)  The consequence of a failure to post would not be confined to this cooperative. The NOVEC zone carries approximately nineteen percent of the Initial RBP Target, and sections 18.9(a) and 15.2.2 distribute the resulting shortfall across the footprint.

III.  THE COOPERATIVE, THE ZONE, AND THE OBLIGATION
15. Northern Virginia Electric Cooperative is a member-owned distribution cooperative and a borrower of the Rural Utilities Service, borrower designation VA0055. For the year ended December 31, 2025 it reported 184,104 consumers served, energy sales of approximately 14.649 billion kilowatt-hours, and a coincident peak demand of approximately 2,360 MW. Except where otherwise stated, the financial and operating figures in this affidavit are taken from its Financial and Operating Report Electric Distribution for that period, prepared with audited data and filed with the Rural Utilities Service under certification. The report is prepared on the unconsolidated regulatory basis of that agency and is not consolidated with the cooperative's subsidiaries.

16. The cooperative is the Electric Distributor for the zone/area PJM designates NOVEC and which is referred to in this affidavit as the NOVEC zone and the Load Serving Entity for the load within it. Virginia's retail choice provisions do not extend to the customers of a distribution cooperative. The large-customer retail access provision reaches only customers of a Phase I or Phase II Utility, and a distribution cooperative is neither. Va. Code § 56-577(A)(3). No other Load Serving Entity serves load in the zone, and no customer within it can select a supplier that would assume the obligation.

17. PJM's illustrative allocation assigns the NOVEC zone 1,302.6 RBP UCAP MW, approximately 19.07 percent of the Initial RBP Target and the second largest allocation in the procurement. Croop Aff. ¶ 46. At the Credit Rate of $1.5 million per RBP UCAP MW the collateral obligation is approximately $1.954 billion.

18. Two features of that allocation should be stated at the outset. First, 1,302.6 MW is approximately fifty-five percent of the cooperative's entire coincident peak demand. Second, the allocation is derived from a forecast of large load additions in the zone rather than from any measurement of the cooperative itself. The obligation is a function of what is projected to be built in the territory, not of the entity that must pay for it.

19. The cooperative's own operations reflect that concentration. Of energy sold in 2025, 73.0 percent went to 193 commercial and industrial customers taking service above 1000 KVA; 15.8 percent went to 169,027 residential members; and 11.1 percent to 14,866 smaller commercial and industrial accounts. Purchased power was approximately $1,310.1 million of a total cost of electric service of approximately $1,454.6 million, or 90.1 percent. This is an entity whose business consists almost entirely of purchasing power in PJM's markets and delivering it, with the large majority of that power going to a small number of large customers.

IV.  THE ALLOCATION IS DERIVED FROM A PLANNING SUBMISSION
20. The cooperative's allocation of 1,302.6 RBP UCAP MW is approximately 19.07 percent of the Initial RBP Target of 6,831.3 MW. That share is not derived from the cooperative's load, its credit, or its participation in any procurement. It traces to the large load forecast the cooperative submitted to PJM's Load Analysis Subcommittee under Manual 19, Attachment B. PJM states the derivation directly: the initial allocation to each Electric Distributor zone or area “will be based on Load Adjustments, as set forth in PJM Table B9b from the 2026 PJM Load Forecast, and each zone/area’s share of the Initial RBP Target of 6,831.3 MW.” Croop Aff. ¶ 46. The accompanying table records a Load Adjustment of 1,722.4 MW for the zone PJM designates NOVEC, a 19.07 percent share, and an initial allocation of 1,302.6 RBP UCAP MW.

21. The cooperative made those submissions in October 2024 and again in September 2025. Their stated purpose was the annual load forecast, which is the input to transmission planning and to the reliability requirement. The submissions are prepared on that basis. They distinguish contracted

PDF page 4

capacity from metered demand and apply a utilization rate, because a data center's contracted capacity and its actual usage differ substantially and for extended periods.

22. No participant in that process was told, and none could have known, that the figures submitted would be used to allocate a collateral obligation. The Reliability Backstop Procurement did not exist. Section 18.8 did not exist. The cooperative supplied a planning estimate, and PJM has converted it into a fifteen-year financial commitment of approximately $1.954 billion in collateral and approximately $109.4 million a year in charges.

23. The consequence for this cooperative is direct. It is among the most diligent participants in the load forecast process. It prepares a long-term forecast annually, updates a medium-term forecast monthly, coordinates with Dominion Energy's transmission planners, and appears before the Subcommittee to document its methodology. That diligence is what produced the largest cooperative allocation in the procurement. An Electric Distributor that had forecast conservatively, or not at all, would carry a smaller obligation.

24. A forecast is an estimate, and PJM has treated this one as a fixed quantity. The tariff provides no reconciliation of the allocated RBP Obligation MW against realized load at any point in the fifteen-year term. Where the forecast load does not materialize, section 18.8(b)(i)(B)(2) directs the resulting charge to the cooperative's native load. The estimate becomes binding on the members without ever being tested against what was actually built.

V.  PJM ALREADY MEASURES THIS COUNTERPARTY
25. PJM is not a stranger to this cooperative. It bills the cooperative weekly, observes whether it pays, and has an unbroken record of that performance for as long as the cooperative has taken service in its markets. Under Attachment Q, PJM measures the cooperative's credit exposure continuously through Peak Market Activity, calculated on actual observed billings, and adjusts the collateral required as that measurement changes. PJM also monitors the cooperative's financial condition outside the Peak Market Activity calculation, through external and internal credit rating systems, through the annual recertification required of Members, and through the financial statements the cooperative provides to PJM. The measurement is current, it is specific to this counterparty, and it is PJM's own.

26. The result of that measurement is reported. At December 31, 2025 the cooperative reported approximately $34.4 million of cash collateral posted to PJM, held as an investment and earning approximately 3.8 percent. Against a coincident peak of approximately 2,360 MW that is approximately $14,590 per MW of peak, and it secures every activity the cooperative conducts in PJM's markets — energy, capacity, transmission and financial transmission rights alike.

27. Section 18.8(b) would require approximately $1.954 billion. Stated as quantities, PJM today holds approximately $34.4 million against a system serving approximately 2,360 MW of coincident peak; the procurement adds a forecast 1,302.6 MW, and the collateral demanded rises by a factor of approximately fifty-seven. Stated as a rate, the $1.5 million per RBP UCAP MW is approximately 103 times the $14,590 per MW of peak that PJM's own continuous measurement of this counterparty presently produces.

28. I do not suggest that these two figures secure the same obligation; they do not, and I address the distinction in Section V. The point is narrower and does not depend on their being comparable in scope. Whatever explains a hundredfold difference, it is not the credit quality of the counterparty. PJM measures that credit quality continuously, and it is not what changed. Section 18.8(b) makes no reference to the cooperative's credit quality at any stage.

29. The two requirements are also additive. Nothing in section 18.8 relieves the cooperative of its obligations under Attachment Q, and the activities giving rise to them continue. Total credit

PDF page 5

support demanded of this cooperative would therefore rise from approximately $34.4 million to approximately $1.988 billion. The two requirements also overlap. Once the first Delivery Year begins and RBP Charges are billed, those charges enter the cooperative's Peak Market Activity through the ordinary operation of Attachment Q and are secured a second time by the financial security that Peak Market Activity requires. Section 18.8 does not net the two. For the period in which RBP Charges are being billed, the cooperative would post against the same megawatts twice.

30. PJM measured the obligated population before it filed. In the Credit and Securitization Overview presented to stakeholders on March 4, 2026, PJM identified 105 Load Serving Entities, of which 24 were investment grade and 81 sub-investment grade.1 This cooperative is one of the 24.

31. A requirement of approximately $1.954 billion would place that standing at risk. The cooperative would have to fund or secure an amount exceeding its total assets, and the ratings that presently make it one of the stronger counterparties in the obligated population reflect a balance sheet that carries no such obligation. In my opinion the requirement would imperil the cooperative's current rating and would call into question whether investment grade standing could be maintained at all. The construct would therefore operate to weaken the credit of the participants on which PJM relies, which is the opposite of what a credit policy is for.

VI.  THE CONSTRUCT ADDRESSES ONE RISK AND PJM DESCRIBED ANOTHER
32. Two distinct risks arise under the Reliability Backstop Procurement, and the filed construct addresses only one of them.

33. The first is counterparty credit risk: that a Load Serving Entity fails to pay the RBP Charge. The party exposed is PJM, and through section 18.9(a) and Operating Agreement, section 15.2.2, the remaining Members. This is the risk section 18.8(b) collateral secures, and it is the risk Attachment Q measures.

34. The second is the overbuy risk: that a large load included in the forecast fails to materialize, that the cooperative nonetheless owes the RBP Charge on capacity procured against that forecast, and that its native load carries the cost until a replacement large load arrives. The party exposed is the cooperative's membership. Section 18.8(b)(i)(B)(2) directs that consequence to native load expressly.

35. The distinction matters because PJM described the second risk, not the first, when it explained the opt-out at section 18.1(d). It stated that cooperatives had raised the concern that an Electric Distributor would be forced to have its customers absorb the costs of an overbuy if a particular large load included in the forecast were to fail to materialize. That is this cooperative's position precisely, and for the reasons set out in Section VIII the opt-out is not available to it. The risk PJM identified is therefore borne here without either the security the construct provides or the remedy the tariff offers.

36. Section 18.8(b) does nothing about the second risk. Collateral posted under it secures PJM against non-payment, and if drawn it is applied to the unpaid RBP Charge. It does not reduce what the cooperative owes when a forecast large load fails to appear, and section 18.8(b)(i)(B)(2) directs that charge to native load whether or not collateral has been posted. The members therefore fund security against the exposure PJM bears, while the exposure they bear themselves is neither measured nor secured anywhere in the filed construct.

Securing the second would mean securing the interval between a failed large load and its replacement, and the measurable quantity for that is the RBP Charge on the at-risk megawatts over the expected replacement period. At the cleared 2028/2029 price that charge is $83,950 per MW-year, so a three-year bridge would be approximately $252,000 per MW, applied only to the megawatts actually at risk. VII.  THE EXPOSURE CAN BE MEASURED
A.  The instrument is a contract for differences
38. The RBP settles as a contract for differences. In each Delivery Year the cooperative pays the difference between the RBP price and the RPM clearing price for that year, multiplied by its allocated RBP UCAP MW. It does not pay the RBP price. Where RPM clears above the RBP price the payment runs in the opposite direction.

39. The Credit Rate is not derived from that difference. At an RBP price of $555.00/MW-day, fifteen years of the gross contract price is approximately $3.04 million per RBP UCAP MW, and the Credit Rate of $1.5 million per MW is approximately 49 percent of that figure. It is half the gross value of a contract on which only a difference is ever payable.

40. Because the Credit Rate makes no reference to RPM, the requirement bears no stable relationship to what the cooperative will owe. Where RPM clears close to the RBP price the difference approaches zero while the collateral does not move. Where RPM clears far below it the difference widens and the collateral is unchanged again. A requirement calibrated to exposure would vary with the second price; this one is fixed against the first.

B.  The exposure that can be measured
41. A collateral requirement is ordinarily derived from an identified position, a price at which that position would be replaced, and a period over which the exposure is held before replacement is complete. Those three elements are standard across margining regimes — cleared derivatives, bilateral credit support arrangements, and PJM's own Attachment Q, which sizes financial security from observed billings over a defined look-back and adjusts it as those billings change. A requirement derived in that manner responds to the position it secures: it rises when exposure rises, falls when exposure falls, and ends when the position ends. Each of those elements is available here.

42. RBP commitments bind when procurement results post on December 2, 2026. The 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction has already cleared at $325.00/MW-day. The 2029/2030 Base Residual Auction posts on December 22, 2026, twenty days later. Within three weeks of commitment, therefore, every input to two Delivery Years of exposure will have been published.

43. On the RBP price of $555.00/MW-day that PJM has used illustratively, and taking the 2029/2030 year at $333.44/MW-day, the exposure attributable to the NOVEC zone over those two Delivery Years is approximately $214.7 million — approximately $109.4 million and approximately $105.3 million respectively. I state below the assumptions on which those figures rest.

44. PJM requires no collateral against that amount for approximately seventeen months. The first posting under section 18.8(b)(i)(B)(1) falls forty-five days before the start of the 2028/2029 Delivery Year, or approximately April 17, 2028. The requirement then imposed is approximately $1.954 billion, approximately nine times the exposure that will have been determinate throughout the intervening period.

VIII.  THE OPT-OUT WAS WRITTEN FOR THIS COOPERATIVE AND EXCLUDES IT
52. PJM identified the overbuy problem during the stakeholder process and provided an opt-out at section 18.1(d) to address it. Explaining that provision, PJM stated that cooperatives and municipal utilities are not governed by a state regulatory authority that would be responsible for addressing the potential reallocation of RBP costs, and described the difficulty as a limited regulatory gap faced by cooperatives and municipal utilities whose rates are not regulated by the states.

53. The remedy is therefore available only to an entity whose rates are not state-regulated. The retail rates of Virginia distribution cooperatives are regulated by the Virginia State Corporation Commission. The cooperative is a utility consumer services cooperative under Chapter 9.1 of Title 56 of the Code of Virginia, and its regulated utility services are subject to the jurisdiction of that Commission in the same manner and to the same extent as those provided by other persons under the laws of the Commonwealth. Va. Code §§ 56-231.33, 56-231.34; see also Va. Code § 56-585.3. Its filed rate schedules provide that service is available subject to terms and conditions filed with that Commission, and the cooperative states that the Commission approves its rates, fees and all terms and conditions of service. On the face of the provision, it is ineligible.

54. The condition and the problem are not the same question. PJM identified the absence of a mechanism to reallocate an overbuy. It then conditioned the remedy on the absence of state rate

PDF page 9

regulation. For an investor-owned utility in a retail choice state those may correlate. For a member-owned cooperative they do not, for two reasons.

55. First, there is no one to reallocate to. Retail choice does not reach the customers of a Virginia distribution cooperative. Va. Code § 56-577(A)(3). No competitive supplier serves load in the NOVEC zone and none can assume the obligation. The State Corporation Commission does regulate the cooperative's rates, and that is what renders it ineligible for the opt-out. But rate regulation has no bearing on whether any party exists to which the obligation could be reallocated, and here none does.

56. Second, rate regulation of a member-owned cooperative is not a mechanism for shifting cost away from members. A cooperative has no shareholders. Its equity is patronage capital owed back to the members who supplied it. When a regulator approves a cooperative's rates, it determines what the members will pay; it does not identify some other party to pay instead. State rate regulation, applied to a cooperative, is the mechanism by which the members are confirmed to bear the cost.

57. The condition therefore selects against the entities the remedy was written for. The more effectively a cooperative's rates are regulated, the more certainly its members bear an overbuy — and that is precisely the circumstance in which the opt-out is withheld. The zone carrying the second largest allocation in the procurement, and the only zone in which the obligation exceeds the reported assets of the entity serving it, cannot use the remedy PJM built for the problem PJM identified.

IX.  WHO BEARS THE OBLIGATION
58. The obligation is generated by one group and stands on the capital of another. The allocation of 1,302.6 MW is derived from forecast large load additions in the zone. Large commercial and industrial customers account for 73.0 percent of the cooperative's energy sales and number 193. The equity that would have to support a $1.954 billion posting is patronage capital contributed by 169,027 residential members who account for 15.8 percent of energy sales, together with the cooperative's other members.

59. Total margins and equities at December 31, 2025 were approximately $919.7 million, of which approximately $824.0 million was patronage capital. That is not permanent capital subscribed by investors. It is retained member margins, carried as an obligation to the members who provided them and retired over time. The cooperative retired approximately $17.9 million of capital credits in 2025 and approximately $416.5 million cumulatively.

60. A collateral obligation of approximately $1.954 billion is 2.4 times all patronage capital on the books, and equal to some 109 years of capital credit retirements at the 2025 rate. Compliance would require the suspension of retirements to identified members for the foreseeable future. That is a determinate consequence to identified persons, and it does not appear in any figure PJM has presented.

61. Expressed per member, the collateral requirement is approximately $10,614 for each of the 184,104 consumers served. The RBP Charge those consumers would pay over the first two Delivery Years is approximately $1,166 each. The cooperative can tell its members what the charge will cost. It cannot tell them what the collateral is calculated to secure, because the Credit Rate is not derived from any exposure they can be shown.

63. Section 18.8(b) permits two forms of collateral: a cash deposit or a letter of credit. It provides no unsecured credit allowance, permits no corporate guaranty, and does not accept surety bonds, although PJM held approximately $606 million of surety bonds across its markets on July 30, 2026.

64. Cash is not available. The requirement is approximately eight times the cooperative's entire cash and temporary investments and approximately 3.4 times all of its current and accrued assets.

65. A letter of credit of this size would have to be supported by the cooperative's balance sheet. The cooperative is a borrower of the Rural Utilities Service, which holds a mortgage over substantially all of its assets, and its loan documents restrict the incurrence of additional secured obligations and liens. To the extent those provisions apply, the difficulty is not that the collateral would be costly. The difficulty is that neither of the two instruments the tariff permits is available to this cooperative on the terms the tariff assumes.

66. Carrying cost compounds the problem. Taking the most favourable assumption available — that a letter of credit can be obtained at 100 basis points — the annual cost would be approximately $19.5 million. The cooperative reported patronage capital and operating margins of approximately $20.8 million for 2025, after existing interest expense of approximately $0.8 million. At 100 basis points the carrying cost alone would consume approximately 94 percent of that margin; at 150 basis points, approximately 141 percent; at 200 basis points, approximately 188 percent; and at 300 basis points, approximately 281 percent. Each is between approximately twenty-five and seventy-five times the cooperative's entire existing annual interest expense, and each would recur for as long as the requirement remains outstanding. Section 18.8 provides no mandatory reduction schedule.

67. Nothing in section 18.8 tests any of this. The Credit Rate is applied per megawatt of allocated obligation. The financial capacity of the entity required to post is not an input to it at any stage.

XI.  THE CONSEQUENCE IS NOT CONFINED TO THE COOPERATIVE
68. Section 18.9(a) provides that a defaulted RBP Charge is socialized pro rata across the remaining Load Serving Entities. Under Operating Agreement, section 15.2.2, the residual reaches PJM Members that hold no RBP allocation and post no RBP Collateral.

69. The NOVEC zone carries approximately 19.07 percent of the Initial RBP Target. A failure to post is not a payment default. The cooperative would have paid every invoice PJM has rendered and would remain willing and able to pay the RBP Charges as billed. What section 18.8(b) would establish is a default triggered by the inability to post collateral in advance of any charge — a credit event converted into a liquidity stress event by the size of the requirement alone. A failure of that kind in this zone is not a localized event. It is the largest single point of failure in the requirement, and the mechanism by which it would propagate is written into the same attachment.

70. The Commission has held that the role of a Regional Transmission Organization is to balance the competing goals of minimizing risk to non-defaulting market participants while ensuring that credit or collateral requirements are not so stringent that they unnecessarily inhibit access to the marketplace, and has rejected a credit provision on that ground notwithstanding that it emerged from a stakeholder process and was approved by that organization's board. Sw. Power Pool, Inc., 126 FERC ¶ 61,153, at PP 19, 21 (2009). A requirement set too low would leave native load bearing the cost of another party's default. A requirement set high enough that the obligated entity cannot meet it produces the same default it was designed to prevent, and does so at the largest cooperative-served allocation in the procurement.

XII.  CONCLUSION
71. PJM took the gross contractual value of a fifteen-year commitment, halved it, and called the result collateral. Applied to this cooperative, that step produces a requirement of approximately $1.954 billion. That is roughly nine times the cooperative's exposure over the first two Delivery Years, the only period for which the record supplies prices; it is more than the entire charge the cooperative would pay across all fifteen years at the price most recently cleared; and it is greater than the total assets of the entity required to post it.

72. The same record contains everything needed to derive a requirement properly. The position is 1,302.6 RBP UCAP MW. The prices are established or will be established before any collateral is due. The cooperative's financial capacity is set out in a report it files under certification with a federal agency. PJM used none of it, and applied a fixed rate per megawatt to an entity whose creditworthiness it measures every month.

73. This cooperative does not contend that the Reliability Backstop Procurement requires no credit support. A procurement of this size plainly does, and a default would fall on load elsewhere in the footprint. My opinion is that the requirement as filed is not derived on terms that would be recognized as collateral in any commercial context, including PJM's own; that the entity required to post it cannot determine from the filed rate what the requirement secures or when it declines; and that the entity cannot post it.

74. The opt-out PJM designed for this exact circumstance is closed to this cooperative by a condition that does not describe the problem PJM identified. Correcting that condition would not by itself cure the requirement, but it would restore to the cooperative the remedy PJM represented to stakeholders that it had provided.
