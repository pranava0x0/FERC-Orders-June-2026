I. ANSWER
In their April 24 answer, the PJM Transmission Owners explained that the Commission should reject arguments arguing for a Retail BTMG threshold higher than 50 MW because the 50 MW threshold strikes the appropriate balance between allowing continued use of Retail BTMG with the appropriate limitations to ensure reliability.  In response, the Answering Parties contend that 50 MW is “arbitrarily low” and would include facilities that pose no reliability risk to PJM.5 These arguments broadly miss the point: the issue is less that existing Retail BTMG arrangements create substantial reliability risks on their own, but rather that the proliferation of large loads, including those that opt to utilize arrangements with on-site generation, pose an increasing risk due to the fact that Retail BTMG are not fully accounted for in resource adequacy planning and can shift costs onto other transmission customers contrary to cost causation principles.6

These were the concerns underlying the Commission’s finding that PJM’s existing Retail BTMG arrangements are no longer just and reasonable, with the Commission specifically agreeing with PJM’s explanation that the existing BTMG rules were conceived and implemented for substantially smaller loads, and that problems could occur if large loads were allowed to use the existing BTMG rules.7  Although Commission did not make a finding as to a particular just and reasonable materiality threshold in the December 18 Order, it did note as “points of reference” that PJM requires individual metering for generators above 10 MW and that the Commission’s definition of a small generator is 20 MW.8  In this light, PJM’s 50 MW threshold is entirely reasonable, as it is high enough to exempt substantially-sized commercial and industrial facilities, but is not so high as to create ongoing reliability risks as well as shifting an unreasonable amount of costs to other ratepayers.  The need to avoid cost-shifting is particularly apt given concerns over ratepayer affordability and subsidization, which will only increase as more large loads come online.  For these reasons, the Commission should accept PJM’s 50 MW threshold. The Commission should also reject arguments that PJM failed to comply with the

December 18 Order because it proposed a Retail BTMG materiality threshold based on generation capacity rather than load size. In discussing potentially appropriate thresholds in the body of the December 18 Order, the Commission did not preclude a generation-based threshold. Finally, it is clear from Answering Parties arguments that their primary concern involves the treatment of existing Retail BTMG customers, rather than new large loads.  The PJM Transmission Owners agree it is important that an appropriate materiality threshold rule should be paired with grandfathering provisions recognizing that industrial and commercial customers have relied on Retail BTMG rules.  To that end, the PJM Transmission Owners indicated their support for certain additions and clarifications to PJM’s grandfathering proposal suggested by Answering Parties, and other commenters.  The PJM Transmission Owners are open to additional suggestions as well as the development of further detail regarding the implementation of the grandfathering provisions in order to avoid substantial financial harm to existing Retail BTMG customers. However, the need for robust grandfathering provisions is not a convincing reason to increase the materiality threshold going forward.  The two mechanisms address fundamentally different concerns—the grandfathering provisions are meant to protect existing customers that have relied on existing BTMG rules, while the materiality threshold is intended to ensure that the proliferation of new large loads does not compromise reliability or shift costs to other PJM ratepayers.  The Commission should reject the attempts of the Answering Parties to conflate the two issues.
