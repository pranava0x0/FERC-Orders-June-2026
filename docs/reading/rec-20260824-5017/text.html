COMMENTS AND PROTEST OF E-CUBED POLICY ASSOCIATES, L.L.C. AND MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE ONE DAY OUT OF TIME

Pursuant to Rule 212[fn:1] of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC” or  “Commission”) Rules of Practice and Procedure, E-Cubed Policy Associates, L.L.C. (“E-Cubed”)[fn:2] respectfully submits these comments and protest one day out of time[fn:3] in response to PJM Interconnection, L.L.C.’s (“PJM”) filing submitted to the Commission on July 31, 2026, pursuant to Section 205 of the Federal Power Act (“FPA”),[fn:4] regarding proposed revisions to the PJM Open Access Transmission Tariff (“Tariff”) to implement a Reliability Backstop Procurement (“RBP”) as a means to cure the current shortfall of 6,831.3 MW of Unforced Capacity (“UCAP”) following the 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction (“BRA”).[fn:5]  On August 14, 2026 PJM filed a supplemental Affidavit of Danielle Croop to correct errors and provide further explanation of how the RBP would operate.[fn:6]
As discussed herein, E-Cubed provides comments and protests PJM’s RBP filing on two distinct, but related dimensions. First, E-Cubed provides publicly available data and evidence that indicates the $555/MW-Day volume weighted average constraint on purchases in the RBP is not commensurate with the actual cost of bringing capacity into PJM via the RBP for any resource type. Thus, PJM will have trouble attracting capacity to offset any portion of the 6,831.3 MW UCAP shortfall that exists in the wake of the 2028/2029 BRA. From both a reliability and economic perspective such a PJM’s proposed $555/MW-day UCAP volume weighted average price constraint is not just and reasonable, is unduly discriminatory and/or preferential because the constraint is not compensatory even under PJM’s assumptions. 
Second, PJM’s proposed volume weighted average constraint is not a budget, but a de facto price cap rather than an offer cap. Instead, E-Cubed proposes a true budget concept that is a function of both the $555/MW-day price[fn:7] and the 6,831.3 MW UCAP shortfall[fn:8] that will procure capacity so long as the expenditures do not exceed the budget while allowing offers that are commensurate with the actual costs of bringing new capacity into commercial operation in PJM. The proposed budget concept will help reduce the shortfall and also provide price discovery that will inform future bilateral contracting opportunities between Large Loads and new Capacity Resources that will further aid in curing the shortfall over time.
RATIONAL OFFERS INTO THE RBP
PJM has proposed to provide long term contracts at up to a 15-year term beginning with delivery for the 2028/2029 Delivery Year (“DY”) running through the 2042/2043 DY.[fn:9] PJM also allows potential sellers to offer into the RBP with in service data up through, but no later than the start of the 2032/2033 DY implying that the shortest contract term is 11 years.[fn:10]
It should not be a surprise that when offered the opportunity for a long term contract of an 11-15 year duration, that investors would want to cover their capital recovery and desired returns over the duration of the contract, rather than taking the risk that in the out years beyond the contract term, which are highly uncertain,[fn:11] a contract framework provides some certainty, albeit incomplete, so long as the new generation resource performs as expected.[fn:12] 
Why would offers in the RBP attempt to cover all investment costs within the 11–15-year contract term as offered by the RBP? Investors in capital are looking for as much certainty as possible, though it is impossible to guarantee any kind of certainty over the life of a long-lived asset such as power generation. Uncertainty comes in the form of overall economic conditions, future net energy revenues, state and federal government policies, and changes in PJM market rules as being major areas of uncertainty.
Regarding net energy revenues, this depends on demand growth, future fuel costs, and the overall configuration of the generation fleet in PJM. Table 4 and Table 10 in Section II below show the high variability in net energy revenues over the most recent 12 years of market operation for combined cycle and simple cycle resources. 
Changes in state and federal policy have impacts. Currently the market has swung from one set of federal policies driving renewable energy development, to one that is openly discouraging renewable development.  Recently, states have become more hesitant to pursue data centers as a strategy for economic and tax base growth, and it has been the prospective data center load growth that has driven the need for RBP.[fn:13]
Overall economic conditions are showing that the capital spending on data centers is one of the biggest items driving overall GDP growth in the US.[fn:14] Yet there are concerns in the financial markets that this prospective data center buildout is a bubble that could burst and completely change the dynamic for the entire economy[fn:15] as well as the trajectory of the need for new capacity in PJM should data center build out not materialize.
Finally, changes in PJM rules and uncertainty regarding PJM’s markets are key drivers. First, PJM has not run a BRA three years forward (on time) since 2018 due to various PJM decisions, regulatory decisions, and market rule changes. Only will PJM get back on time, it seems, for the May 2027 BRA for the 2030/2031 DY…a nine-year hiatus for on time BRAs. PJM also moved to an interconnection cluster study process, ultimately a wise move, but while the backlog of projects under the old serial queue process was cleared, no new projects were allowed to enter the interconnection queue between mid-2021 and April 2026 but for PJM’s RRI mechanism.[fn:16] 
Further, PJM made an enormous change in resource accreditation by moving to ELCC for all resources starting with the 2025/2026 BRA and DY. For those investors with projects in the old serial queue process, this completely changed their economic decisions on whether to move forward. Further, with ELCC, accreditation changes year-over-year for reasons that remain unclear to investors. How are investment decisions to be made when the underlying capacity product that one can sell is highly variable and largely out of the control of the generation owner give the use of history that cannot be changed in modeling ELCC?
Incentives for the structure of offers under uncertainty

PJM has proposed a “pay-as-bid” framework for the RBP. Leaving aside the known incentive problems with pay-as-bid auctions as well as inefficiencies that PJM itself has recognized,[fn:17] Investors will want to include a monetary value for uncertainty into their offers.
The tables below showing that even if there is “certainty” on investment costs and EAS for Net CONE, any prospective offers will likely be higher to account for uncertainty and risk. PJM has stated clearly that those who offer into RBP face the risk of interconnection costs[fn:18] and ELCC variation.[fn:19] For most potential offers, interconnection costs are not yet well known. Transition Cycle 2 (“TC2”) is not yet complete,[fn:20] and Cycle 1 has only started so there is no clear line of sight on ultimate interconnection costs for many projects that could offer into the RBP.
Furthermore, ELCC values have varied significantly for many resource types, especially for gas resources that are likely to be the highest ELCC value resources that could reach commercial operation and offer into the RBP.[fn:21] Given the analysis in Section III below, it is not feasible for any resource type available to meet the $555/MW-day UCAP volume weighted average price cap as an offer price for any of the contract duration terms on offer in the RBP. This becomes even more untenable with ELCC risk and variance.
Accounting for updated installed costs and uncertainty, the only reasonable conclusion to draw from PJM’s proposed RBP is that it will fail to attract the capacity it seeks under its $555/MW-day UCAP cap. But there is a possible way forward that will help provide some price discovery and will admit offers and commit resources with offers well in excess of $555/MW-day and still meet the intent and spirit of the willingness to pay as discussed in Section III.

Other Technology Net CONE UCAP Cost Estimates Show it is Not Financially Feasible to Offer at or Below $555/MW-day

Battery Energy Storage Systems (“BESS”)
The case for batteries being unable to enter at under $555/MW-day is shown in the Brattle CONE study in the last periodic review. Even including ITC, the Gross CONE ranges between $650-$726/MW-day over a 20-year horizon,[fn:42] so the Gross CONE costs for contract terms of 11-15 years would be even higher on an ICAP basis.
The IMM has not provided any new entrant battery net revenues in the SoM thus the best estimate available is Brattle Net CONE ICAP value of $409/MW-day.[fn:43] But when applying the 2029/2030 ELCC value to 4-hour BESS of 57%,[fn:44] this comes to $717/MW-day UCAP over 20 years. There is simply no room for BESS to enter via the RBP as the costs will be even higher under terms of 15 years or less.
Solar
In the most recent PJM update for MOPR tracking solar has a Gross CONE ICAP of $659/MW-day for 2030 delivery.[fn:45] The maximum average EAS is $250/MW-day ICAP for new entry solar over the 2014-2025 period.[fn:46] This implies an estimated $409/MW-day ICAP Net CONE (absent any REC credits). The ELCC value of solar is only 7%[fn:47] so resulting in a $5824/MW-day UCAP for solar absent REC credits.
If we take REC credits into account as estimated by the IMM,[fn:48] only solar in New Jersey would be able to enter the RBP. But even so, the UCAP contribution is minimal at 7%. The current Cycle 1 queue only shows 4 MW of solar based on maximum facility output (“MFO”) in NJ[fn:49] while the transition queues show on 253 MW of energy in other locations. 7% of that value is de minimis and solar elsewhere in PJM is simply not able to make the $555/MW-day.
Onshore Wind
In the most recent PJM update for MOPR Onshore wind $786/MW-day ICAP for 2030 delivery.[fn:50] The maximum average EAS is $235/MW-day ICAP for new entry onshore wind over the 2014-2025 period.[fn:51] This implies an estimated $551/MW-day ICAP Net CONE (absent any REC credits). The ELCC value of solar is only 37%[fn:52] so resulting in a $1,489/MW-day UCAP for onshore wind absent REC credits.
If we take REC credits into account as estimated by the IMM,[fn:53] the maximum average is $122/MW-day ICAP. This would reduce the Net CONE ICAP to $429/MW-day but with the ELCC value of 37%, this still comes to $1159/MW-day UCAP over 20 years making new onshore wind more expensive than the $555/MW-day UCAP de facto price cap.


A TRUE BUDGET WOULD ALLOW SOME RESOURCE TO BE COMMITTEED IN RBP TO ALLEVIATE THE SHORTFALL
PJM’s filing states that their so-called “budget” is a MW-weighted average of $555/MW-day in net present value terms which would allow for some offers below $555/MW-day to offset some offers that would come in above $555/MW-day.[fn:54] Notwithstanding the source of the choice of $555/MW-day, it is clear from the discussion above that it is unlikely that many offers, if any, could possibly come in below $555/MW-day, and thus, it is unlikely that any capacity will be committed via RBP to offset the 6,831.3 MW UCAP shortfall being targeted. PJM itself admits this is a de facto price cap.[fn:55]
The so-called flexibility PJM avers assumes, and as shown in Section II above incorrectly so, that some offers will come in below the $555/MW-day UCAP value while others will come in above that providing offer flexibility. While it may be theoretically possible this may occur, the likelihood of that happening is slim given the implied offers required under the most optimistic of circumstances as discussed above.
This result alone makes PJM’s proposal unjust, unreasonable and unduly discriminatory and preferential in that not only in the proposed de facto price cap not reflective of actual costs, but it is also not solving the capacity shortfall in any meaningful way. 
However, if one takes the idea of a budget seriously, a budget is simply an amount of money that can be spent, as opposed to a target weighted average price as PJM has proposed. With that in mind it is possible to construct a budget from the willingness to pay at $555/MW-day and the target quantity procured of 6,831.3 MW UCAP.  A simple way to do this would be to multiply the $555/MW-day by 6831.3 MW by 15 years by 365 days per year which would indicate a budget of $20.75 billion to procure capacity.[fn:56] 
But this would violate the spirit and intent of what PJM has proposed regarding the levelized costs discounted at 9.5% WACC. In the alternative, a net present value budget can be determined easily by assuming the purchase of the 6,831.3 MW UCAP shortfall at $555/MW-day for each of the 15 years of the maximum contract term available in RBP. This results in a net present value budget of $11.86 billion.[fn:57] This is simply another way of expressing willingness to pay, but in the form of how much money can be spent on capacity.
This proposed “true” budget approach caps the amount that can be spent on capacity purchased through the RBP, while allowing for the purchase of some capacity to reduce the shortfall and allowing offers above the $555/MW-day threshold to be committed, and a levelized weighted average price above $555/MW-day UCAP so long as the total expenditures are at or under the budget amount.
The point of a true budget-based approach as proposed here is to allow offers to reflect the true cost of bringing new capacity into service while accounting for uncertainties and still honoring the spirit of the willingness to pay, albeit in the form of total expenditures rather than as a price per unit as PJM is doing. It also provides a means to reduce the MW UCAP shortfall, albeit not likely all the way. 
For example, using the simple levelized cost approach PJM has proposed, if the weighted average offers were $1000/MW-day UCAP, PJM would be able to procure 3,780 MW UCAP under the proposed budget approach, rather than procuring nothing as PJM’s current proposal would do. If the weighted average offers came in at $850/MW-day UCAP, PJM would be able to procure up to 4,450 MW UCAP. While both figures do not fully cure the 6,831.3 MW shortfall, reliability would be enhanced compared to procuring nothing in the RBP.
Such an approach would provide a truer sense of price discovery as a known budget and known awards will allow market participants to back out the implied weighted average cost without being privy to the actual offers, and this would inform future bilateral contracting discussions. as market participants can estimate the weighted average offer from the outcome of the RBP.  In contrast the $555 de facto price cap keeps the true nature of the costs opaque to bring capacity into operation and thus hindering future bilateral contracting.
