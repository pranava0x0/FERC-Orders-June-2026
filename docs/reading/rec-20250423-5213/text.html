I. INTRODUCTION AND NEED FOR REFORM
The United States stands at the precipice of an unprecedented surge in electricity demand from large loads including data centers and large industrial and manufacturing facilities.9 Extraordinary load growth is fueled mostly by the proliferation of data centers that support artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and other services.  Over the next 15 years, PJM anticipates significant peak demand growth, with the summer peak rising by roughly 70 gigawatts (“GW”), and the winter peak rising by roughly 65 GW.10  Enchanted Rock understands that securing a global competitive advantage in artificial intelligence is a U.S. priority and that maintaining data centers within U.S. borders is a critical national security interest.11  These extraordinary changes in demand require federal and state regulators, grid operators, market participants, and customers to pivot and look to innovations that optimize infrastructure without creating new reliability risks—particularly during periods when new generation capacity and transmission infrastructure is planned, permitted, and constructed.  The accelerating demand for electricity coupled with supply delays caused by lengthy permitting processes for generation and transmission infrastructure, as well as persistent supply chain delays for equipment, means the industry must devise immediately implementable solutions that optimize existing infrastructure and ensure that transmission expansion occurs in the most cost-effective way possible.

II. LCDG OPTION: LOAD CO-LOCATED WITH DISPATCHABLE GENERATION
As PJM demonstrates in its answer, co-location may exist through a spectrum of different configurations.23  Enchanted Rock agrees.  At one end of the spectrum is what Enchanted Rock would describe as “Complete Electrical Isolation,” where the load and generation are entirely self-sufficient and islanded; at the other end is “Full Grid Integration,” with unrestricted power exchange with the grid, operating as a fully integrated load, using exclusively grid-sourced energy and capacity. Under Enchanted Rock’s view, Flexible Grid Integration is a co-location arrangement that relies on the PJM-operated transmission network for grid-sourced energy but provides flexibility commitments through dispatch of the customer’s co-located dispatchable generation during periods of system stress.  Flexible Grid Integration facilitates a system committed to grid- sourced energy and regulated markets, integrates large loads into system planning, provides operational transparency into large loads, and enhances grid operator capabilities by giving large loads the opportunity to provide flexibility when grid operators need it.

Enchanted Rock’s LCDG option, a type of Flexible Grid Integration, shares
characteristics with PJM New Options 6 and 7, while also offering additional functionality.  The premise of the LCDG option is that load flexibility provides substantial benefits to a constrained system.  Load flexibility is key to accelerating large load interconnections and optimizing the efficient and cost-effective utilization of grid infrastructure.  Under the LCDG option, load and generation may be separately metered but are studied together for purposes of retail load interconnection and integration, transmission and resource adequacy planning, and operations. The LCDG option provides more than a traditional demand response or peak shaving program during the operating period.  The flexibility provided by the LCDG option would be considered in the planning and study process for large load interconnection, local and regional planning, and resource adequacy.  The LCDG option therefore permits large loads to utilize flexibility to achieve faster interconnection through the deployment of dispatchable, co-located generation.  Under the LCDG option, the firm commitment to dispatch co-located generation would serve as a bridge to permanent grid upgrades for projects that would otherwise be delayed while new infrastructure is planned, permitted, and constructed.

Specifically, under Phase 1 of Enchanted Rock’s proposal, LCDG load would be treated as curtailable non-Network Load, subscribing to Interruptible Service (as contemplated in PJM’s New Option 7) for generation service, and subscribing to Enchanted Rock’s proposed Available Transmission Capacity Service for transmission service.  Under Phase 1, LCDG generators would be classified as non-Network Resources (i.e., interconnected on a non-network basis), and would be limited to serving the co-located load’s main function of reducing or zeroing demand.25 Under Phase 2, LCDG load would be eligible to go through the normal PJM Tariff process as Network Load, when PJM deems that the supply/demand conditions are no longer constrained, and necessary transmission infrastructure (e.g., Network Upgrades) have been

Currently, load and generation interconnection requests are studied separately.  This disjointed approach creates several issues, particularly for co-located arrangements where the load and generation components are intended to function as an integrated system.  First, it can lead to a mismatch in timing.  The generation and load portions of a co-located arrangement may

face different timelines for interconnection, making it challenging to bring the entire facility online in a coordinated manner.  This mismatch in study timelines can delay project completion and create bottlenecks, as project timelines become constrained by the interconnection study process with the longest duration.

Second, separate studies fail to account for the interdependencies between the load and generation components of a co-located arrangement.  When studies are conducted independently, the load interconnection study may not consider the impact of the co-located generation, and vice versa.  This can result in inaccurate assessments of the necessary transmission upgrades and can lead to inefficient interconnection solutions.

Recognizing and respecting jurisdictional boundaries between load and generator interconnections, Enchanted Rock recommends improving coordination so that grid planners in multiple jurisdictions can gain a more accurate understanding of the co-located arrangement’s overall impact on the grid.  For example, by enabling the generation component to offset some of the load’s impact on the grid, it may defer the need for certain upgrades for reliable interconnection until such time that infrastructure expansion can occur.  Consideration for co- located arrangements in the retail load integration process would also allow for faster load interconnections and mitigates commercial risks for both load and generation developers. Facilitating improved coordination aligns with the response to the Show Cause Order of certain indicated transmission owners in PJM, which transmission owners also emphasized the need to value load flexibility in the overall planning process.31

Backup generators can indeed provide benefits to customers and help minimize system costs. Additionally, diesel generators carry risks related to fuel availability and delivery, especially after widespread outages when a surge in demand from numerous diesel users simultaneously attempting to refuel GWs of backup generation capacity can quickly overwhelm supply chains.

iii. Parties may also choose to address how co-location arrangements may
provide benefits, including potentially reducing required transmission

Enchanted Rock’s Response:
Co-location arrangements, particularly those involving LCDG, can provide several benefits to the grid and its operation.  By deploying dispatchable co-located generation, a large load can effectively become an interruptible load.  If this capability is valued in the transmission planning process before load interconnection, it may allow grid planners to defer the need for certain transmission upgrades that would otherwise be required to serve the new load.  The co- located generation can be used to offset the load’s impact on the grid during peak demand and stress periods.

In PJM utility territories that rely heavily on centralized transmission and lack sufficient local generation, co-location arrangements can help reduce congestion and backfill any required transmission contingencies in the interim as new transmission is built to solve the violations.  In most cases, these co-located arrangements can even be configured to export excess generation to the grid because normal industry practice is to oversize backup generation for redundancy purposes. This capability helps to stabilize the grid, avert blackouts, and optimize the utilization of existing grid infrastructure.

Co-location also contributes to improve overall system efficiency by reducing transmission line losses, which represent a not insignificant portion of the total electricity

generated, with some amount lost between the generation source and the end-use customer.33 The amount of energy lost is dependent on factors such as the distance the electricity travels, the characteristics of the transmission lines, and the level of current flowing through the lines.  By locating generation sources closer to load, co-location arrangements minimize the distance that electricity needs to travel across the transmission network.  Lower transmission losses mean that less electricity needs to be generated to serve the same amount of load, freeing up capacity on existing transmission infrastructure for use by other customers.

III.REASONS TO UPDATE THE PJM TARIFF (POTENTIAL ISSUES WITH THE
CURRENT PJM TARIFF)
9. Co-located arrangements with new generation and new large loads are differently situated than a point of interconnection involving only generation or only load.  Further, co-located arrangements with new generation and new large loads are distinguishable from proposed co-location arrangements where there is a pre-existing relationship with PJM involving assets with current and continuing operations.  For example, with existing generation, the PJM transmission system was likely modified to accommodate that generation.

10. Updating the PJM Tariff to accommodate co-located arrangements with new generation and new large loads would align with removing impediments to competition in the PJM wholesale power markets to serve large loads.  This would align with a fundamental principle of Order No. 888 in promoting competition and efficiency.2

11. Updating the PJM Tariff will help avoid undue discrimination and will help allow open access to the PJM transmission system to co-located arrangements with new generation and new large loads.

IV.AVAILABLE TRANSMISSION CAPACITY SERVICE
12. The Commission should consider creating a new transmission service to unlock the unused capacity of the transmission system, which can be utilized by co-located arrangements.
