Pursuant to section 313(a) of the Federal Power Act (“FPA”), 16 U.S.C. § 825l (2024), 
and Rule 713 of the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the Federal Energy Regulatory 
Commission (“Commission” or “FERC”), 1 Eolian, L.P. (“Eolian”), requests clarification or,  in 
the alterative, rehearing of the Commission’s June 18 Orders in the above-captioned proceedings 
(“Large Load Show Cause Orders” or “Orders”).  
The Large Load Show Cause Orders take critical steps to facilitate more timely 
interconnection for large load customers while ensuring the affordability and reliability of the 
electric system for existing customers. Eolian strongly supports the steps taken in these orders, 
which demonstrate exactly the bold leadership this moment demands. We request rehearing of 
one aspect of the Orders that would severely limit the scope of any subsequent replacement rates 
and prevent the Orders from achieving the robust benefits to just and reasonable rates and

reliability that they aim to achieve. Specifically, while the Orders preliminarily find it unjust and 
unreasonable for RTO/ISO tariffs to fail to recognize the ability of large loads to limit their 
withdrawals from the transmission system under certain conditions and propose that transmission 
services that reflect their use of the transmission system be available on both an interim and 
permanent basis,2 the Orders appear to practically limit the availability of the new transmission 
services by failing to provide for matching availability of permanent large load-limited generator 
interconnection services.  
Because the Commission appears to categorically limit the availability of permanent 
generator interconnection services to co-located generation,3 while requiring any load-limited 
generation interconnection service for electrically proximate generation serving large loads to be 
temporary, electrically proximate generators that are equally capable of serving large loads and 
enabling them to limit their withdrawals from the transmission system face a significant barrier 
to development. 4 In short, large loads’ ability to make use of the new transmission services will 
be severely limited if new front-of-the-meter generation projects that would be willing and able 
to serve a large load on a long-term basis are unable to support use of the new permanent 
contract demand transmission service and interim network integration transmission service 
(“Interim NITS”). Limited uptake of the new transmission services will, in turn, greatly limit the 
ability of the Commission’s Orders to ensure just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory 
rates.

First, we request that the Commission clarify, or grant rehearing if necessary, to provide 
that electrically proximate generation may access permanent load-limited generator 
interconnection service where it is equally capable of enabling the large load to limit its 
withdrawals from the transmission system on a long-term basis without creating system 
reliability risks. Failure to do so will greatly limit the potential solutions that RTO/ISOs may 
propose in response to the Orders, and therefore greatly limit the real-world impact and benefits 
of the Orders. At minimum, we request that the Commission make clear that RTO/ISOs are not 
limited in their ability to provide permanent load-limited generator interconnection service where 
the generator is capable of enabling the large load to limit its withdrawals from the transmission 
system on a long-term basis without reliability concerns, and that the Commission strongly 
encourage RTO/ISOs to make such service broadly available.  
Second, and relatedly, we seek rehearing or clarification of the Orders because they 
appear to leave too much flexibility to RTO/ISOs to provide only very limited reforms to their 
generator interconnection study processes and services. While we do not believe the Commission 
intended to afford so much flexibility to RTO/ISOs that they could adopt minimal reforms that 
do not achieve the objective of ensuring just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory rates, 
the Orders include some language that could be interpreted as such. The Commission must 
clarify this discussion, so that it does not inadvertently create a loophole that would undermine 
the critical benefits of its Orders to reliability, affordability, limiting cost shifts, and speed to 
power.   
I. 
CONCISE STATEMENT OF ISSUES AND SPECIFICATION OF ERRORS 
(1) The Commission’s Orders arbitrarily and capriciously limit RTO/ISOs’ ability to put 
in place a replacement rate that will ensure just and reasonable and not unduly

discriminatory rates by not requiring and/or by limiting their ability to adopt a 
permanent load-limited generator interconnection service for front-of-the-meter 
resources that are capable of enabling large loads to limit their withdrawals from the 
transmission system under certain conditions on a long-term basis without resulting in 
reliability concerns. See 16 U.S.C. § 824d(a)-(b); Consol. Edison Co. of New York v.

preliminary finding of unjust and unreasonable rates. Without commensurate reforms 
to the generator interconnection process to enable generating resources to timely 
serve large loads, the Commission’s Orders cannot achieve its stated aims and ensure 
just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory rates. See Motor Vehicle Mfrs., 463 
U.S. at 43. 
II. 
ARGUMENT 
A. 
The Commission’s Orders Seek to Address Urgent Problems Due to Large 
Load Growth, but Cannot Achieve Sufficient Scale Impact Without a Key 
Modification.    
Eolian fully supports the Commission’s preliminary findings that a series of actions is 
necessary to ensure just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory rates in RTO/ISOs in light 
of the significant acceleration of large loads seeking to interconnect, which poses the potential 
for impacts to reliability and affordability, cost shifts among transmission customers, and delays 
in interconnection and service of large loads. Eolian seeks rehearing or clarification of the 
Commission’s Orders solely to address one unreasonable limitation on the scope of actions that 
RTO/ISOs would be required to take under the Orders, because that limitation would create a

significant barrier to large loads’ ability to use the new transmission services directed under the 
Orders, and therefore would significantly impair the ability of the Orders to ensure just and 
reasonable and not unduly discriminatory rates. To be able to use the new transmission services 
on a widespread basis rather than in limited circumstances, large loads must be able to 
commercially transact with new generation resources to serve them, so that they can limit their 
withdrawals from the transmission system. Large loads will not be able to transact with new 
front-of-the-meter resources if resource developers face significant commercial barriers to 
interconnection, including the cost and timing uncertainty of the traditional generator 
interconnection queue procedures. Because the Commission’s Orders limit the availability of a 
permanent large load-limited generator interconnection to only a small subset of front-of-the-
meter configurations that would be capable of serving large loads on a long-term basis, the 
Orders unduly constrain the ability of these generators to serve large loads.

2. 
The Commission’s Orders will not achieve widespread benefits if few 
large loads are able to avail themselves of the new transmission services. 
The uptake of the new transmission services will be limited, however, if large loads 
cannot identify sufficient new generation resources to support their ability to limit their 
withdrawals from the transmission system during certain conditions. Large loads vary in their 
ability to curtail their operations depending on their business model. Large loads that cannot 
commit to curtail their operations may, nonetheless, be able to commit to limit their use of the 
transmission system by transacting with generation resources that will continue to serve their 
load at times when withdrawals from the broader network must be limited. The more diverse the 
configurations of resources that are eligible to serve a large load and enable it to limit its 
withdrawals from the transmission system—i.e., the ability to rely on behind-the-meter 
generation, front-of-the-meter generation that is co-located, or front-of-the-meter generation that 
is electrically proximate—the more likely it is that a large load will be able to identify an 
arrangement that works for its business model. If few new generating resources are able to 
transact to serve large loads because resource developers face insurmountable or significant 
commercial barriers to offering such services, large loads will not be able to procure such 
services at reasonable cost and there will be much more limited uptake of the new transmission 
services. This, in turn, will mean that making the new transmission services available has little 
real-world impact.

In summary, for the Commission’s Orders to ensure just and reasonable and not unduly 
discriminatory rates, not only must large loads have the theoretical capability to use the new 
transmission services, but generating resources must also be available on a timely basis to serve 
these new large loads and enable the large loads to reduce their use of the bulk power system.     
3. 
Significant limitations on the front-of-the-meter resource 
configurations that may use permanent large load-limited generator 
interconnection service create a barrier to commercial development and 
constrain the resources available to serve large loads.  
 
While the Orders recognize the need for reforms to RTO/ISO generator interconnection 
processes that would afford some resources the opportunity to more timely serve large loads, the 
Orders contain a key limitation on the scope of the generator interconnection service and study 
process that RTO/ISOs must provide. Because the Orders appear to unreasonably limit 
permanent load-limited generator interconnection service to only co-located resources, many 
new generating resources that are capable and willing to commit to reliably serving a large load 
on a long-term basis will be unable to do so.   
The Orders preliminarily find that RTO/ISOs’ generator interconnection processes require 
reforms to “reflect an interconnection customer’s commitment in an interconnection agreement 
to limit the generating facility’s output to minimize the impact on the transmission system while 
serving an electrically proximate large load or large co-located load.”10 The Commission 
recognizes that “[w]here a generating facility’s output is matched to the demand of the 
electrically proximate large load or large co-located load or if the generating facility’s output is 
limited to ensure no new injection, the impacts to the transmission system of interconnecting the 
generating facility to serve that electrically proximate large load or large co-located load may be 
limited, thereby potentially reducing the need for Network Upgrades, which can accelerate the

Yet even as the Commission recognizes the value of an interconnection service and study 
process tailored to more limited service to a specific load, the Commission imposes practical 
limits on the availability of such a load-limited interconnection study process and service. The 
Orders specify that while “it may be reasonable for the generator interconnection service to be 
permanent” “[t]o the extent that the interconnection customer’s generating facility is serving a 
large co-located load,” where “the interconnection customer’s generating facility is serving an 
electrically proximate large load, we believe that the generator interconnection service should be 
of an interim nature until the interconnection customer obtains Energy Resource Interconnection 
Service (ERIS) or Network Resource Interconnection Service (NRIS).”13 This potential 
restriction, unless clarified, threatens to greatly limit the use of load-limited generator 
interconnection service.  
As a practical matter, to be able to provide limited service to a large load and enable that 
large load to avoid or reduce its reliance on the transmission system, a generating resource must 
have (i) access to an interconnection process that enables it to interconnect and commence 
operation in time to serve the large load and (ii) reasonable cost certainty to understand if the

But, based on Eolian’s experience as a developer of generating facilities that seek to serve 
large loads, few developers will be able to develop resources that are electrically proximate if the 
only available load-limited interconnection service is temporary and requires the resource to 
make its way through the standard generator interconnection process to obtain permanent 
interconnection service. The standard generator interconnection process remains plagued by long 
delays and the potential for high-cost, project-killing network upgrades. A developer that is 
provided access to only a temporary interconnection service must make the decision to invest in 
the full cost of developing its generating facility years before it will learn the total all-in cost of 
the project, because it will face a decision whether to proceed to commercial operation under a 
temporary load-limited generator interconnection service long before it will be studied and learn 
its actual network upgrade costs to obtain permanent NRIS or ERIS interconnection service. Cost 
uncertainty that can range in the tens to hundreds of millions of dollars for additional network 
upgrades, or even more in particularly constrained parts of the transmission system, typically 
results in a potential project being unfinanceable and non-viable. It is therefore Eolian’s

This challenge of cost uncertainty for temporary interconnection service means that, even 
for Interim NITS customers, generation developers can much more readily provide 
corresponding load-limited service if that service is permanent. A permanent interconnection 
pathway provides the resource with the financial certainty necessary to support development, 
even if in the future that resource likely will elect to proceed through the standard queue 
(pending study results that show a commercially workable level of network upgrade costs). 
While a load-limited interconnection would restrict the generation to serving the load, which 
would itself graduate to full network service, the generation resource could continue to provide 
its service to the load. Permanent service could, for instance, facilitate a long-term power 
purchase agreement between the load and generator, whereas temporary service would likely 
render such an arrangement non-viable (because the generator’s ability to serve the load over the 
long-run would remain highly uncertain given the potential for project-killing cost assessments 
associated with obtaining permanent service).  
And for transmission customers taking contract demand service, temporary load-limited 
generation interconnection is an even worse match. Where a transmission customer plans to 
reduce its long-term reliance on the transmission system by leveraging the capability of a 
generation resource, the obligation to transition to NRIS or ERIS will result in unnecessary

B. 
Absent modification or clarification, the Commission’s Orders unreasonably 
limit RTO/ISOs’ ability to use a significant tool in the toolkit to address the impacts 
of large loads. 
The Commission’s justification for limiting permanent load-limited generator 
interconnection service to co-located resources appears to be that, for such configurations, “there 
will be no injections onto the transmission system and therefore” permanent load-limited 
interconnection service “does not present operational or planning risks for reliability.”15 But as 
explained in the affidavit of Andrew Levitt, imposing a “co-location requirement for permanent 
LLGIS would disqualify many configurations that present the same simplified reliability picture 
as co-located arrangements.”16 Such broad disqualification is not necessary because many 
resources that are not “co-located” as that term is defined in the Commission’s orders, 
nevertheless could connect on a permanent load-limited basis while imposing essentially the 
same lack of reliability impacts to the broader system. RTOs have identified locations that are 
“electrically equivalent”, which means load and generation could be at locations that, while not 
the same, are electrically equivalent in terms of their impacts on the grid.  This would allow for a 
broader set of load-generation agreements than “co-location” as currently defined by the 
Commission.  SPP, for example, defines electrical equivalence as “[a] relationship between

Points of Interconnection (POI) that are (1) at the same substation and nominal voltage level, (2) 
on the same branch or on a collection of in series two-terminal branches and associated buses 
and facilities, or (3) on the same radial branch and associated facilities.”17 
This broader category of generation-load pairings nevertheless imposes the same lack of 
system impact risks as co-located resources because “[e]lectrically equivalent interconnection 
points are defined to be functionally identical for reliability purposes.”18 Numerous RTOs have 
implemented services and study processes based on this general concept because, as MISO 
states, electrical equivalence criteria “‘limit potential impacts to flow in the transmission 
system’, ‘will not cause new constraints in the contingency analysis’, will ‘minimize impacts to 
flow and therefore other projects in the interconnection queue’, all ‘without subjecting other 
projects to an unreasonable risk of causing network upgrades’.”19  
As Levitt explains, regions also implement this same sort of analysis when conducting 
generator replacement analysis: “In generator replacement, the test asks whether moving the 
point of injection from Point A to Point B materially changes transmission impacts. For 
permanent LLGIS, it asks the same question about locating new generation at Point A (behind 
the load’s point of interconnection) or at Point B. In each case, the central planning question is 
whether the alternate generation connection point produces material grid impacts: new 
constraints, materially different power flows, or harm to queued projects.”20 While it is true that 
“[w]ith permanent LLGIS, electricity may flow across a short segment of public transmission 
between the generator and load, . . . that fact alone does not establish the kinds of regional

C. 
The Orders fail to require broad enough reforms to the generator 
interconnection study process and services to ensure the Orders achieve their 
critical objectives. 
Finally, the Commission’s Orders contain contradictory discussion of RTO/ISOs’ 
obligation to implement reforms to the generator interconnection processes that could lead to 
confusion and may limit the Commission’s ability to require reforms that are sufficient to ensure 
just and reasonable and not unduly discriminatory rates. On the one hand, the Orders 
preliminarily find most RTO/ISOs’ tariffs unjust and unreasonable because they do not contain 
provisions to allow “an interconnection to seek the generator interconnection service(s) that

reflects the operational dynamics of serving either: (1) an electrically proximate large load; or (2) 
a co-located load, as defined in this order, that has a high peak load (i.e., large co-located loads 
that are 50 MW or greater).”23 Such a deficiency would only be addressed by ensuring that 
resources that are similarly situated in their interest and ability to serve a specific large load have

such access. If the new generator interconnection study process and services are broadly 
available to resources willing and able to provide such limited service, the reforms will be 
expansive enough to achieve the Commission’s aims, as discussed above, by avoiding unneeded 
transmission infrastructure build-out, providing reliability benefits, reducing the cost impacts, 
and enhancing speed-to-power. The reforms would be right-sized to the scale of the problem, 
because sufficient generation can be interconnected through the generator interconnection 
reforms to enable significant numbers of large loads to utilize the new transmission services, 
which in turn, mitigates the impacts of large load interconnections.  
Yet later in the Orders, the Commission appears to suggest that a much more limited set 
of generator interconnection reforms may be sufficient to address the deficiency. The 
Commission suggests that “another potentially reasonable approach to address our concerns” 
could include (i) allowing new generating facilities to interconnect with a new load behind an 
existing generator’s point of interconnection or (ii) allowing a new generating facility and a new 
load to interconnect behind the same new point of interconnection.24 While Eolian is not opposed 
to these as potential additional pathways to enable a generating facility to receive limited

IV.A. Lack of a Permanent Load-Limited Generator 
15 
Interconnection Service Option Would Work Against the 
16 
Purpose of Non-Firm Transmission Service 
17 
In these show-cause orders, the Commission identifies the opportunity to achieve speed-to-power 
18 
by offering a new type of accelerated interconnection for a new generator that is dedicated to 
19 
providing power to a large load, and is co-located with or electrically proximate to it. This Load-
20 
Limited Generator Interconnection Service (LLGIS) is premised on the generator’s commitment 
21 
to limiting its output to match the consumption of the load it is supporting. The Commission 
22 
finds that “[w]here a generating facility’s output is matched to the demand of the electrically 
23 
proximate large load or large co-located load or if the generating facility’s output is limited to 
24 
ensure no new injection, the impacts to the transmission system of interconnecting the generating 
25 
facility to serve that electrically proximate large load or large co-located load may be limited,

The Commission could broaden permanent LLGIS further to include electrically proximate arrangements. 
These would not create further reliability issues as compared to temporary LLGIS. Given the inherent front-of-
the-meter structure of (non-co-located) proximate generation and load pairings, the grid operator would retain 
the ability to manage the system in operations, including by curtailing individual load and/or generation as 
needed to preserve reliability where intervening transmission facilities are impaired. In that respect, permanence 
does not remove the operational tools that grid operators already use to address transmission constraints or other 
system conditions on an interim basis. That said, electrically proximate arrangements may present some 
additional considerations relative to co-located arrangements, because they rely on facilities between the load 
and generation rather than being located entirely behind a single point of interconnection.

1 
situated with respect to the electrical-equivalence test. In generator replacement, the test asks 
2 
whether moving the point of injection from Point A to Point B materially changes transmission 
3 
impacts. For permanent LLGIS, it asks the same question about locating new generation at Point 
4 
A (behind the load’s point of interconnection) or at Point B. In each case, the central planning 
5 
question is whether the alternate generation connection point produces material grid impacts: 
6 
new constraints, materially different power flows, or harm to queued projects. Therefore, if the 
7 
test fails in either case, the generator must go through the standard interconnection queue 
8 
process. With permanent LLGIS, electricity may flow across a short segment of public 
9 
transmission between the generator and load, but that fact alone does not establish the kinds of 
10 
regional impacts addressed by the full interconnection queue.13 Rather, as with generator 
11 
replacement, the test of interest is whether the arrangement creates material grid impacts. 
12 
MISO’s and SPP’s generator replacement tests for electrical equivalence reflect that impact-
13

14 
generators connecting close to their paired loads.

IV.C. 
Reliable and Resource-Neutral Load-Limited Generator 
9 
Interconnection Service Must Include Studies of Storage 
10 
Charging 
11 
To ensure LLGIS maintains resource and technology neutrality, the service must be accessible to 
12 
storage resources. Consistent with NRIS and ERIS today, the LLGIS application process will 
13 
therefore need to include study provisions associated with reliable energy withdrawals for 
14 
charging of a potential storage resource (analogous to the ESR-LA process for storage in SPP, 
15 
which is included in the HILLGA process15). This study would need to be included in the LLGIS 
16 
interconnection process even if the storage resource does not reserve transmission service (i.e., 
17 
because it is charging pursuant to the provision of a grid service).  
18 
These charging studies—which reflect the dispatch capability of storage—are already conducted 
19 
as part of the ISO/RTO generator interconnection processes for storage requesting NRIS and 
20 
ERIS. It is important that they be extended to storage requesting LLGIS.
