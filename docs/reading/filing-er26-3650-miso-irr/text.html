EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
The MISO region is experiencing a significant increase in both the scale and pace of Large 
Load development.  MISO’s 2026 Long-Term Load Forecast5 indicates that the region is 
experiencing load growth at levels not seen in decades, driven in large part by unprecedented large 
load activity and accelerating project announcements.  Data centers and advanced manufacturing 
are among the primary drivers of this growth, with some projects seeking to develop on timelines 
significantly shorter than those traditionally associated with generation and transmission 
infrastructure.  This combination of increased volume and accelerated development is changing 
the manner in which MISO must plan for and reliably integrate new load.   
 
As this growth has emerged, MISO, its member states, and stakeholders have been 
developing multiple initiatives to address evolving system conditions and to support timely, cost-
effective and reliable interconnection of Large Loads.  For example, MISO’s Expedited Project 
Review (EPR) Process and Expedited Resource Addition Studies (ERAS) complement MISO’s 
determination of needed additional transmission infrastructure, which are being planned and

Interconnection Agreement (ZGIA), is currently pending action at the Commission in Docket No. 
ER26-3552.

BACKGROUND 
A. 
Large Load Challenges  
 
The United States is in the midst of an unprecedented energy transition.  Large Load 
customers, including artificial intelligence (“AI”) data centers and next-generation manufacturing 
technologies, are driving a surge in interconnection requests. 7  As NERC has observed, the largest 
contributor to this accelerated demand growth is data center additions, with 18 GW of data center 
loads projected by 2035.8  The MISO region is no exception to this energy transition.  Energy-
intensive loads such as data centers, manufacturing expansion, and electrification are adding 
sustained, concentrated load at levels not seen in decades.9  While electricity demand growth has 
been relatively flat with an annual average of just 0.5% between 2009 and 2024, MISO is 
projecting a substantial increase of 1% to 2% per year through 2044, with even higher growth rates 
in the near term.10

Authorities (“RERRA”), stakeholders and federal policymakers.  On January 23, 2025, President 
Trump issued an Executive Order stating, “[i]t is the policy of the United States to sustain and

A. 
New Definitions Related to the IRR for Large Load 
 
 
1. 
Large Load 
The Order posits that MISO’s Tariff appears to be unjust and unreasonable because it lacks 
a definition of Large Load sufficient to capture loads that present the unique challenges associated 
with Large Loads.28  This filing proposes to address the Order’s concern by implementing a clear 
and consistent Large Load definition, as follows:   
Large Load:  Load with Demand of 50 MW or more associated with 
any facility or aggregation of facilities located at a single substation 
behind one or more Point(s) of Withdrawal from the Transmission 
System that is owned, operated, or controlled by: (a) a single entity 
or (b) a group of entities that will coordinate with respect to the 
profile of the Load.  Large Load does not include Load used 
exclusively for charging an Electric Storage Resource. 
 
As explained by Ms. Li, MISO developed the proposed Large Load definition through a 
review of emerging industry practices, consideration of the reliability needs in the MISO region, 
and stakeholder feedback.29  Based on that review, MISO selected a 50 MW threshold as a 
reasonable and administratively clear starting point, consistent with industry precedent and 
sufficiently significant to warrant application of MISO’s Large Load requirements.  The definition 
is based on gross load rather than net load so that MISO has visibility into the full electrical demand 
and characteristics of the facility.  Further, MISO defined Large Load at the facility or site level to 
allow loads under common ownership or coordinated operational control at a single site to be 
considered together, including where the site is served through multiple points of interconnection.

As explained by Ms. Li, the Computational Load definition distinguishes a subset of Large Loads 
that may exhibit operational characteristics different from more traditional loads, including rapid 
changes in demand, highly coordinated control behavior, and power-electronic responses during 
system disturbances.31  This distinction allows MISO to apply certain incremental requirements—
such as Ramp, PMU, and Ride-Through requirements—only where those characteristics warrant 
additional reliability consideration.  The Computational Load definition is necessary as certain 
proposed requirements are intended to apply only to Large Loads with operating characteristics 
that warrant additional reliability consideration.  Computational Loads may exhibit rapid and 
coordinated changes in demand, significant power-electronic behavior, and distinct responses to 
Transmission System disturbances.  Defining Computational Load supports a more targeted and 
risk-informed approach, rather than applying the same requirements to all Large Loads regardless 
of facility type or operating behavior.

expectations for the Computational Load component of a Large Load to maintain connection and 
stable operation during voltage and frequency excursions, while supporting system voltage 
recovery, frequency performance, and overall system stability. 
 
Consistent with emerging industry practices and NERC guidance, the proposed ride-
through expectations are intended to establish scalable and reliability-based performance criteria 
proportional to demonstrated system risk and facility operating characteristics.
